# Double Falsehood / Cardenio — annotated claims register of the authorship scholarship

Companion index of everything fetched: `../sources/scholarship/SOURCES.md`.
Scope: every study I could identify on (a) who wrote the text printed by Lewis Theobald as *Double Falshood; or, The Distrest Lovers* (Drury Lane 13 Dec 1727; 8vo 1728, two issues), and (b) whether that text descends from the King's Men's *Cardenno/Cardenna* (court performances paid 20 May 1613 and 9 July 1613 for 8 June 1613) entered by Humphrey Moseley in the Stationers' Register on 9 Sept 1653 as "The History of Cardenio, by Mr Fletcher. & Shakespeare."

## 0. Conventions used throughout

* **Access tags** (stated for every study):
  * `[DIRECT]` — full text read in this project; file saved under `sources/scholarship/`.
  * `[ABSTRACT]` — only the publisher's abstract / a preview page read verbatim.
  * `[SECONDARY]` — known only from quotation or summary in another study that I did read directly (mostly Alotaibi's 2016 Liverpool thesis, Taylor 2016, the NOS *Authorship Companion* 2017, Graham 1920, Bradford 1910, Oliphant 1927). Page numbers given for the *secondary* source are marked "via".
  * `[WEB-SUMMARY]` — known only from a web-fetch or web-search auto-summary (paraphrase, not verbatim; lowest reliability).

## 1. Documentary anchors that the scholarship argues about (summary only — the documentary record is in `documents.md`)

1. Treasurer of the Chamber accounts (Bodleian MS Rawlinson A.239, f. 47): payment 20 May 1613 to John Heminges for six plays performed at court in the 1612–13 season, one called "Cardenno"; payment 9 July 1613 for "Cardenna" performed 8 June 1613 before the Duke of Savoy's ambassador. No author named. Stern 2011 (555–57) notes the spellings could in principle denote the Spanish town Cardeña, not Cervantes's Cardenio [SECONDARY via Folger LPD and Alotaibi]; Taylor 2012 (*Quest* 11–61 and 283–99) argues the court performance most likely fell in February 1613 and the June performance at the Lord Mayor's [WEB-SUMMARY + NOS Companion 583–84, DIRECT].
2. SR 9 Sept 1653, Moseley: "The History of Cardenio, by Mr Fletcher. & Shakespeare." (with a full stop after Fletcher). Bradford 1910: "Moseley was far from reliable" (p. 55) [DIRECT]. Freehafer 1969 (p. 508, via Alotaibi): the stop suggests Shakespeare's name "was added as an afterthought" and Moseley entered "in alphabetical order of authorship", implying he "might have preferred to have a play by Fletcher alone" [SECONDARY]. Stern 2011 (557–58): Moseley "seems to take liberties with some names, particularly those of authors who could be published lucratively" and misascribed other plays to Shakespeare [SECONDARY via Alotaibi, thesis p. 14].
3. Assignment of Moseley's copyrights to Jacob Tonson the younger, 5 April 1718 (Hammond 2010, p. 81) [SECONDARY].
4. Charles Gildon, letter written c.1710, printed 1719 (pp. 267–8): a manuscript play by "the immortal Shakespear; in the Maturity of his Judgment, a few Years before he dy'd", with "infallible Proof that the Copy is genuine" [SECONDARY via NOS Companion p. 585, DIRECT].
5. Theobald's Preface (1728): three manuscripts; one "of above sixty years standing, in the handwriting of Mr Downes, the famous old prompter", earlier owned by Betterton, who "designed to have ushered it into the world"; a "Noble Person" who supplied a copy; the tradition that Shakespeare gave it "as a Present of Value, to a Natural Daughter of his, for whose Sake he wrote it"; a third copy "much more perfect". Second issue of 1728 changes "in our Tongue" to "in that other Great Poet" (Graham 1920, p. 17 n., citing R. F. Jones) and adds that Theobald "had once design'd a Dissertation" on the play's authenticity but reserved it "to my Defence" [DIRECT via Graham; Alotaibi pp. 10–11].
6. Royal licence (5 Dec 1727) calling it "an original play of William Shakespeare".
7. *Gazetteer* 31 March 1770: "the original Manuscript of this play is now treasured up in the Museum of Covent Garden Playhouse"; Covent Garden burned 19 Sept 1808 [WEB-SUMMARY; Hammond 2010, 48–58 cited by LPD].

## 2. Register of studies (chronological)

### 2.1 Contemporary reactions, 1727–1733 `[DIRECT via Theobald's preface as quoted by Bradford 1910 and Graham 1920; Pope via Nicholl 2010]`
* Critics at once urged Fletcher. Theobald's Preface: "Others again, to depreciate the Affair, as they thought, have been pleased to urge, that tho' the Play may have some Resemblance of Shakespeare, yet the Colouring, Diction, and Characters, come nearer to the Style and Manner of Fletcher. This, I think, is far from deserving any Answer; I submit it to the Determination of better Judgments; tho' my Partiality for Shakespeare makes me wish, that Everything which is good, or pleasing, in our Tongue, had been owing to his Pen." (quoted Graham 1920, pp. 16–17) [DIRECT].
* Pope and the Scriblerians: forgery (*Dunciad*; Mallet's *Epistle to Mr Pope* 1733: "See him on Shakespeare pore, intent to steal / Poor farce, by fragments, for a third-day meal" — quoted Nicholl 2010) [DIRECT web page].
* Theobald's own claim to have written 1.3.10–14 ("Strike up, my masters; / But touch the strings with a religious softness…") and his insistence that 3.1.17 ("None but itself can be its parallel") was Shakespeare's (Stern 2011, 588–89, via Alotaibi p. 201) — the pair of claims that Farmer, Malone and Stern all exploit.
* Why it matters later: Bradford (1910, p. 55) treats the early "detection of Fletcher in the play by persons who had no reason whatever to look for him there" as evidence of a genuine old manuscript; Oliphant (1927, p. 283) treats Theobald's denial of Fletcher as "the strongest proof of his bona fides".

### 2.2 Richard Farmer, *An Essay on the Learning of Shakespeare* (Cambridge, 1767; 2nd ed. 1767, pp. 26–29) `[DIRECT — four archive.org copies + ECCO-TCP div; file farmer-copy-onlearningofshak00farm.txt]`
* **Method**: a single linguistic "minutia" (accentuation) plus impressionistic "Style and Manner"; also cites Langbaine on Shirley's unpublished manuscripts.
* **Argument** (2nd ed. pp. 26–29): "Sometimes a very little matter detects a forgery. You may remember a Play called the Double Falshood, which Mr. Theobald was desirous of palming upon the world for a posthumous one of Shakespeare … Mr. Pope himself, after all the strictures of Scriblerus, in a Letter to Aaron Hill, supposes it of that age; but a mistaken accent determines it to have been written since the middle of the last century." He quotes "This late example / Of base Henriquez, bleeding in me now, / From each good Aspect takes away my trust" and "You have an Aspect, Sir, of wondrous wisdom": "The word Aspect, you perceive, is here accented on the first Syllable, which, I am confident, in any sense of it, was never the case in the time of Shakespeare".
* "After all, the Double Falshood is superior to Theobald. One passage, and one only in the whole Play, he pretended to have written: … 'Strike up, my Masters; / But touch the Strings with a religious softness: / Teach sound to languish thro' the Night's dull Ear, / 'Till Melancholy start from her lazy Couch, / And Carelessness grow Convert to Attention.' These lines were particularly admired; and his vanity could not resist the opportunity of claiming them: but his claim had been more easily allowed to any other part of the performance."
* Answers Benjamin Victor's objection that the Cervantes source was "not published 'till the year after Shakespeare's death": "unluckily the same Novel appears in a part of Don Quixote, which was printed in Spanish, 1605, and in English by Shelton, 1612."
* **Conclusion**: "But you want my opinion:—and from every mark of Style and Manner, I make no doubt of ascribing it to Shirley. Mr. Langbaine informs us, that he left some Plays in MS.—These were written about the time of the Restoration, when the Accent in question was more generally altered. Perhaps the mistake arose from an abbreviation of the name." (Stern 2011, 590, reads Farmer's abbreviation as "W.Sh." = William Shirley [SECONDARY via Alotaibi p. 199].)
* **Critiques**: Bradford 1910, p. 52: Farmer forgot "that Theobald confessed to having revised the original with 'great labour and pains'"; "I have myself studied Shirley's plays carefully and I detect none of these 'marks of style and manner' in Double Falsehood" [DIRECT]. Graham 1920, p. 14: D. Nichol Smith repeated the "aspect" argument "as if Theobald, among other emendations, would not certainly have changed any words having an accent not current in 1727" [DIRECT]. Followers of Farmer: Dyce (1833, would have printed it in Gifford–Dyce *Shirley* "had he possessed one of the manuscripts"), O. G. Gilchrist (*Quarterly Review* 7: "the internal evidence of that play strongly confirms his decision"), A. W. Ward (Graham 1920, pp. 13–14; Bradford 1910, p. 52) [DIRECT]. No modern study has tested Shirley (Stern 2012 and the *Renaissance and Reformation* reviewer both note Massinger and Shirley "have not been addressed adequately").

### 2.3 George Steevens and Edmond Malone, *Supplement to the Edition of Shakspeare's Plays* (1780); Malone's annotated copy of the 1728 second issue `[SECONDARY — via Alotaibi 2016, pp. 66–67 and 14–15, and Ivan Lupić, 'Malone's Double Falsehood', Quest 95–114, as cited there; Lupić's chapter itself inaccessible (OUP Cloudflare)]`
* Steevens (1780) first connected the 1613 "Cardenno/Cardenna" payments (extracts he obtained from the Treasurer of the Chamber accounts and copied into his Langbaine) with Moseley's 1653 entry; Reed (1782) then suggested the identity with *Double Falsehood* (Alotaibi p. 15; Lupić) [SECONDARY]. Bradford 1910, p. 55, believed Malone "does not show the most distant sign of associating that play with either Shakespeare or Fletcher"; Lupić shows the Steevens/Reed/Malone sequence in detail (pp. 98–99, per my earlier notes).
* Steevens rejected Shakespeare: "had it … been written by our author, it would surely have been published in the folio of 1623, or at least would have been ascribed to him in some ancient catalogue" (via Alotaibi p. 66).
* Malone → Massinger: listed from the Stationers' Books "eleven plays that were written by Massinger but never published"; cited the parallel between the line Pope ridiculed ("None but Itself can be its Parallel", 3.1.17) and Massinger's *Duke of Milan* ("Her goodness does disdain comparison, / And but itself admits no parallel"); and *Duke of Milan* "yet be wise; / Soar not too high to fall, but stoop to rise" ~ DF 1.3.72–73 "I must stoop to gain her; / Throw all my gay Comparisons aside"; but noted the stronger parallel "lay his gay comparisons apart" (*Antony and Cleopatra* 3.13.26), which, if not interpolated by Theobald, "would serve to confirm Massinger's title to this play, he having very frequently imitated Shakespeare" (Alotaibi pp. 66–67) [SECONDARY].
* Malone's marginalia in his 1728 copy (Lupić, p. 107, via Alotaibi p. 66): "Theobald asserted that he was the author of these five lines & they were the only lines in the play that he wrote. I believe both assertions to have been false."
* **Status**: the only serious historical case for H3 (another Jacobean/Caroline dramatist). Nobody since has run Massinger tests except Proudfoot 2012 (early Massinger plays give "over 20 per cent" line-end-polysyllable matching, which he discounts) and Pascucci 2017 (detects Massinger in 2.2, 2.3, 2.4 — see §2.34(b)).

### 2.4 Isaac Reed (ed.), Baker's *Biographia Dramatica* (1782), vol. II `[DIRECT — reed-1782-biographia-dramatica-vol2.txt]`
* D-section no. 104: "THE DOUBLE FALSHOOD, or, The Distrest Lovers. Trag. by L. Theobald, Acted at Drury-Lane, 8vo. 1727. This piece Theobald endeavoured to persuade the world was written by Shakspeare. How true his assertion might be, I cannot pretend to determine, but very few I believe gave any credit to it. The play, however, was acted with considerable success, and was the last piece in which Mr. Booth appeared. Dr. Farmer is of opinion, that it is a production of Shirley's, or at least not earlier than his time. Mr. Malone inclines to believe it written by Massinger."
* H-section no. 103: "THE HISTORY OF CARDENIO. A Play, by Mr. Fletcher and Shakspeare; entered on the books of the Stationers' Company Sept. 9, 1653; but I believe never printed. It has been suggested, that this play may possibly be the same as The Double Falshood; afterwards brought to light by Mr. Theobald."
* Graham 1920 (p. 14): Jaggard "considers Reed's conjecture the 'shrewdest' — that Theobald was the author" — i.e. Reed's private view was forgery, while his entry is the first printed identification of DF with *Cardenio*.

### 2.5 Nineteenth-century opinion (via Graham 1920, pp. 13–15, and Bradford 1910, pp. 52–53) `[DIRECT for the summaries; the originals not consulted]`
Gifford (1813, *Massinger* ed.) denied Theobald's authorship on the strength of the verse and connected the play with Elizabethan drama "because of the use of comparison for caparison"; Dyce, Gilchrist, Ward → Shirley; Halliwell-Phillipps discredited Theobald "merely because the history of the manuscript … was not satisfactory"; Churton Collins: "might be founded on some old play, but is for the most part from Theobald's own pen"; Sidney Lee: "there is nothing in the play as published by Theobald to suggest Shakespeare's hand, but Theobald doubtless took advantage of a tradition that Shakespeare and Fletcher had combined…"; Jaggard: forgery; D. Nichol Smith (1903): Shirley. Bradford's verdict on the lot (p. 52–53) and Graham's (p. 15): "With two or three exceptions, the scholars cited made no careful study of Double Falsehood before passing judgment on it."

### 2.6 Gamaliel Bradford Jr., "The History of Cardenio by Mr. Fletcher and Shakespeare", *Modern Language Notes* 25.2 (Feb. 1910), 51–56 (first stated in *The Nation* 88 (1909), 328) `[DIRECT — bradford-1910-mln-history-of-cardenio.txt]`
* **Method**: connoisseurial verse-and-diction tests: double (feminine) endings, especially two-word feminine endings and endings in "now/too/yet"; favourite words ("extremely", "now"); parallels of imagery (cloud figures); dramatic structure (Fletcherian climaxes, garrulous old men, Julio's madness ~ Jailer's Daughter). Corpus: Fletcher canon read as a whole; Shakespeare by ear.
* **Results**: "an examination of the different scenes which I had attributed, on independent grounds, to Fletcher and to his fellow author, shows 47% of double endings in Fletcher's portion and only 32% in the remainder. Of course, owing to Theobald's revision, this is much less than Fletcher's usual percentage. Nevertheless the difference is significant." Examples: "Yes, I am that Lord Roderick, and I lie not" (3.3); "She's stol'n away; and whither gone, I know not" (3.3); "Make up your Malice, and dispatch his Life too". "The adverb 'extremely,' occurring twice in this play, is a favorite with Fletcher. It occurs only five times in all Shakespeare, two of those being in the Fletcherian Henry VIII. It is found at least twenty-eight times in Fletcher". (p. 54)
* **Scene assignment** (p. 55): "These marked Fletcher peculiarities, then, do not appear at all before III, 3. In that scene they are abundant. Fletcher's also is the speech of the shepherds at the beginning of IV, but the business of Julio's madness takes us back to the firmer, stronger hand: — 'It puzzles my Philosophy, to think / That the rude Blast, hot Sun, and dashing Rains / Have made no fiercer War upon thy Youth.'" Graham 1920 (p. 17) paraphrases Bradford's Fletcher share as "II, iii [sic — Bradford's own text says III, 3]; IV (except about fifty lines near the beginning); and V (except a small portion which he does not define)".
* **Conclusion** (p. 55): forgery excluded by Theobald's denial of Fletcher — "This paragraph seems to me absolutely incompatible with any possibility of Theobald's having set himself deliberately to imitate Fletcher. I do not see, then, how we can avoid the conclusion that Theobald was really in possession of an old play, and that, in view of its subject and of Fletcher's part authorship, that play was 'The History of Cardenio.'" On Shakespeare: "For external evidence we have Moseley's attribution … but Moseley was far from reliable. We have Theobald's tradition, which is worth little, but something." "The internal evidence is certainly not of great importance. Numerous passages resembling Shakespeare's acknowledged work … can be produced; but none is close enough to have much weight." "Many lines and phrases in the non-Fletcherian portion of the play have, to my ear something of a Shakespearean turn or resonance, as distinguished from either Beaumont or Massinger". Final: "All these arguments are certainly far from sufficient to associate Shakespeare's name in any positive fashion with The History of Cardenio, not to speak of Double Falsehood as it stands … a play which was almost indisputably Fletcher's, which had at least some claim to be classed among Fletcher's collaborations with his greatest predecessor" (p. 56).
* **Critiques**: Schevill 1911 (below) attacked the Elizabethan-source premise; Graham 1916 confirmed Bradford's division statistically; Oliphant 1927 "almost complete agreement"; Jackson (NOS Companion ch. 8, p. 123) and Taylor 2016 treat Bradford/Oliphant as the start of the modern consensus; Stern 2012 objects that none of this checked Theobald's own habits.

### 2.7 Rudolph Schevill, "Theobald's Double Falsehood?", *Modern Philology* 9.2 (Oct. 1911), 269–285 `[DIRECT — schevill-1911-theobalds-double-falsehood.txt]`
* **Claim**: the play's immediate source is not Shelton (1612) but the prose "novel" *The Adventures on the Black Mountains* in Samuel Croxall's *Select Collection of Novels* (1720–22; 2nd ed. 1729), a version "new translated" by an "eminent hand"; hence the play is Theobald's own work of the 1720s ("Double Falsehood was taken from The Adventures, etc., as printed by Croxall", p. 285).
* **Method**: three-column parallel of Cervantes/Shelton, Croxall's novel and DF (pp. 271–284).
* **Critiques**: Graham 1916 and 1920 (pp. 8–11) show seven verbal parallels to Shelton absent from Croxall (Shelton IV.i.2, IV.i.3, IV.i.5 and 11, III.xi.267, III.xi.269, Cardenio's song III.250 ~ DF 4.1; IV.i.1 ~ DF 3.3) and that Croxall's own version "furbished up Shelton"; Graham's first established "fact": "The immediate source of Double Falsehood was Shelton's Don Quixote" (1916, p. 280). Schevill's thesis has had no defenders since, though Pujante 1998 and Stern 2011 revive the different point that Shelton could have been used by Theobald directly (a 1725 reprint of Shelton existed: Stern 582, via Alotaibi p. 26).

### 2.8 Walter Graham, "The Cardenio–Double Falsehood Problem", *Modern Philology* 14.5 (Sept. 1916), 269–280; "Note on the Cardenio–Double Falsehood Problem", *MP* 14 (Jan. 1917), 184; *Double Falsehood by Lewis Theobald* (Western Reserve Studies I.6, Cleveland, May 1920: introduction pp. 1–24 + reprint of the 1728 text) `[DIRECT — all three files]`
* **Method (1916)**: verse tests on Bradford's division and on Theobald's own tragedies: feminine endings, run-on lines, weak and light endings, feminine caesural pauses, mid-stopped speeches, two-monosyllable feminine endings; definitions from Neilson/Thorndike. Corpus: DF (1447 verse lines) v. Theobald's *Persian Princess* (1278 lines), *Electra* (1376), *Orestes* (1606); TNK and H8 as the Sh/Fl comparators.
* **Results (1916, tables pp. 275–276, 278)**: feminine endings — *Persian Princess* 20.5%, *Electra* 16%, DF 38.1%, *Orestes* 22.6%; run-on lines 15 / 9 / 18.3 / 10; weak-and-light endings .08 (10) / .06 (8) / .15 (22) / .025 (4). "There are in all 137 such [two-monosyllable] endings in Double Falsehood, whereas Electra and Orestes have 20 and 27 respectively … 'Sir' and 'not' are found 11 and 10 times used in this way … [in *The Chances*] 'Sir' is found 22 times and 'not' 11 times." Bradford's division: "Parts possibly by Fletcher" 754 lines — feminine endings 44.2%, run-on 15.2%, weak/light 3; "By Another" 693 lines — feminine 32%, run-on 21.5%, weak/light 19 (feminine caesural pauses 23.3% after the 1917 correction). "Nineteen to three is a very significant difference." "It seems hardly possible that the portion of the latter which contains 44.2 per cent of feminine endings was composed in 1727 by the same hand that wrote Orestes three years later with only 22.6 per cent. Nor does it appear any more likely that the first part of Double Falsehood which contains 19 weak and light endings was written by the same author as the play of 1730 in which there are only 4 altogether." (pp. 276–277)
* **1917 Note** corrects two table errors (feminine caesural pauses "by another" 23.3 not 13.3; TNK weak/light 0.23 not 23).
* **Conclusion (1916, p. 280)**: "(1) The immediate source of Double Falsehood was Shelton's Don Quixote. (2) The style of the play as a whole, and the second part in particular, differs appreciably from Theobald's acknowledged work. (3) There are unmistakable evidences of two styles. (4) These distinct styles show a general similarity to those in Two Noble Kinsmen and Henry VIII, which are now recognized as belonging to Shakspere and Fletcher." But also (p. 279): "the shortness of it, the lifeless verse, some of the language, and the distinctly inferior dramatic quality are strong arguments against its being the work of Shakspere or Fletcher either … It is not impossible … that the manuscripts were revised several times before they reached him."
* **1920 introduction** (pp. 23–24): "The presence of Fletcher's hand in the play seems clearly evident … Since this external evidence makes Shakespeare Fletcher's collaborator, it renders probable the supposition that the other original writer … is our great master-dramatist. Yet … no scholar is yet ready to declare unreservedly his belief in Shakespeare's participation." Notes that "One scholar has suggested that II, ii, III, iii, and V, ii, have something reminiscent of Beaumont" and that Forsythe wanted Daborne, Davenport and William Rowley tried; Jones wanted DF read against Theobald's *Richard II* and *Duchess of Malfi* adaptations (both programmes later executed by Jackson 2012 and Carnegie 2012).
* **Critiques**: none specific; Tarlinskaja 2017 and Jackson 2012 effectively re-run and confirm the two-part contrast; Stern 2012 (*Quest* 129) complains that no one before Hope checked Theobald's own plays — untrue of Graham, who used three.

### 2.9 E. H. C. Oliphant, "'Double Falsehood': Shakespeare, Fletcher and Theobald", *Notes and Queries* 12th ser. 5 (1919), 30–32, 60–62, 86–88 `[not accessible]`; *The Plays of Beaumont and Fletcher* (Yale UP, 1927), §39 pp. 282–302 `[DIRECT — oliphant-1927-bwb.txt lines 12542–13130]`
* **Method**: ear-based scene-by-scene assignment on "the imagery, the habit of thought, the diction, and the sentence-building"; acknowledges that "the verse's mechanism is more or less ruined … definite results are not to be looked for" (p. 291). Retracts his earlier verdict that DF contained "nothing that could have been written by Fletcher or Shakspere" ("I confess my fault, and retract", p. 295).
* **Assignment** (pp. 291–92, 295): "the hand of the eighteenth-century reviser is to be found in every scene, and … two scenes (II. 3 and 4) are wholly his." "Mr. Bradford was the first critic to note … that a new voice becomes audible in III. 3. It is the voice of Fletcher, who is thenceforward dominant. In this first scene we see his work overwritten by Theobald in the first nine speeches, though the editor's other work is limited to the provision of a closing couplet. The other Fletcherian portions of the play are that part of IV.1 preceding Julio's entry (the text having perhaps undergone some revision by Theobald); that part of the same scene between Violante's reëntry and the entry of Roderick; IV. 2, as far as 'And those to come shall sweetly sleep together' (with the exception of the song), the remainder of the scene showing Fletcher overwritten by Theobald; V. 1 (as revised by Theobald); and, also revised, V. 2, from 'Thou art a right one,' though as far as 'Duke. Weep not, child' has been left as originally written, the persistent Theobald, to atone for this, having left nothing whatever of Fletcher from 'Leon. The righteous powers at length have crown'd our loves' onward." The "firmer, stronger hand … Overlaid by Theobald's, this hand is to be seen in the first two speeches of the opening scene, in I. 2 (with the exception of ten speeches beginning 'Leon. What do you mean?'), in I. 3, in II. 1, in II. 2, in III. 1, in the first four speeches of III. 2 and in that portion of the scene between 'Scene opens to a large hall' and 'Most perjur'd if I do,' and in five speeches of IV. 1 immediately succeeding Julio's entry … As for the rest of I. 1 and 2, the balance of III. 2, and the remainder of IV. 1 to the reëntry of Violante, as well as the conclusion of that scene, they seem to be entirely the work of the reviser, though his portions of III. 2 and IV. 1 may possibly hold some fragments of the work of the older writer, from whom may perhaps also come the opening portion of the final scene. This writer I believe to have been Shakespeare; but in coming to a determination on the point one has to be guided largely by the external evidence and the probabilities, for the original work has been too heavily overlaid for the writer's touch to be clear and unmistakable."
* **Forgery**: "This suggestion [Fletcher] was indignantly denied by Theobald—a fact which … affords the strongest proof of his bona fides, as does also his obviously genuine belief that the work had never been staged." (p. 283)
* **Later use**: Jackson 2012 and Pascucci 2017 use Oliphant's "Shakespeare revised by Theobald" segments as their sampling frame; Taylor 2016 (n. 18): "since Oliphant (1927), attribution scholars have found the most concentrated evidence of Theobald's hand in Act Two."

### 2.10 Kenneth Muir, *Shakespeare as Collaborator* (Methuen, 1960), ch. "Cardenio", pp. 148–160; "Cervantes, Cardenio and Theobald", in *Spain and its Literature: Essays in Memory of E. Allison Peers* (Liverpool UP, 1997), 141–150 `[SECONDARY — archive.org copy lending-only (401); via Alotaibi pp. 29–30 and web summaries]`
* 1960: accepts the Bradford/Oliphant two-hands division and Shelton as source; argues Theobald's revisions fell hardest on Shakespeare's share. 1997 (via Alotaibi p. 29, quoting Muir): "Shakespeare's scenes were vandalized" while Fletcher's "were left relatively undamaged"; "the division of authorship Muir presents is in agreement with previous scholarship" (Alotaibi). No numbers recorded. Verbal-parallel lists in Muir 1960 are the basis for Hammond's commentary (Hammond 2010, p. 148 refers to the "range and scale of Shakespearean and Fletcherian allusion").

### 2.11 John Freehafer, "Cardenio, by Shakespeare and Fletcher", *PMLA* 84.3 (May 1969), 501–513, DOI 10.2307/1261138 `[ABSTRACT — verbatim from Cambridge Core; body via Alotaibi and NOS Companion]`
* **Abstract (verbatim)**: "Cardenio, a 'lost' play of 1612-13 based on Shelton's translation of Don Quixote (1612), is partly preserved, in altered form, in Lewis Theobald's Double Falshood of 1727. Theobald probably altered Cardenio drastically, as he did Richard II. To internal evidence that Cardenio was written by Shakespeare and Fletcher can be added chronological evidence and reluctant testimony to the joint authorship by the publisher Moseley, Charles Gildon, and Theobald. One of Theobald's three manuscripts can be traced in the hands of Moseley, the Davenants, Betterton, Gildon, Theobald's patron (Charles Boyle), and Theobald. A Restoration performance probably was intended. Theobald persisted in claiming that the original play was by Shakespeare alone after he learned otherwise, from Gildon and the Stationers' Register, to save his reputation as a Shakespeare scholar and please his patron. To protect his 'Shakespeare' play, Theobald obtained a unique royal license, which Pope ridiculed in the Dunciad. Theobald probably sold his play and Shakespeare manuscripts outright to his publisher. He was prevented from publishing Cardenio by the copyright Act of 1710, the practice of regarding copyright as perpetual, and his quest of money and patronage."
* **Key claims**: first proposal that the Downes manuscript was Davenant's Restoration adaptation (NOS Companion p. 585: "John Freehafer (1969) suggested that Theobald possessed, in the handwriting of Downes, William Davenant's adaptation of the play, and Hume (2016) agrees"). On the SR stop: p. 508 (see §1.2).
* **Critiques**: Fleissner (*Neuphilologische Mitteilungen* 1984?) doubts the "afterthought" reading (via Alotaibi p. 14); Stern 2011 attacks the whole manuscript-provenance chain as unevidenced (563–66); Hammond 2010 "pursued enthusiastically" the Davenant hypothesis (48–58) [WEB-SUMMARY].

### 2.15 Jonathan Hope, *The Authorship of Shakespeare's Plays: A Socio-linguistic Study* (Cambridge UP, 1994), pp. 89–100 `[NOT ACCESSED — archive.org item `authorshipofshak00hope` is lending-only (HTTP 401 on the full text); Google Books API returned 429 throughout; known only from search snippets and citations]`

### 2.20 Tiffany Stern, "'The Forgery of some modern Author'?: Theobald's Shakespeare and Cardenio's Double Falsehood", *Shakespeare Quarterly* 62.4 (Winter 2011), 555–593, DOI 10.1353/shq.2011.0074 `[SECONDARY — OUP/MUSE/academia.edu all blocked; known from Alotaibi 2016 (who adopts her position and cites pages), Taylor 2016, Hammond 2014 (abstract only), LPD, NOS Companion]`
* **Thesis**: DF is, or may well be, a forgery by Theobald — "an eighteenth-century forgery" in Tarlinskaja's words (NOS Companion 405) — and even if not, the documentary chain linking it to a Shakespeare–Fletcher *Cardenio* is unsound. Component claims, with her page numbers as cited by Alotaibi and LPD:
  1. 555–57: the 1613 payments are the sole hard evidence; the spellings "Cardenno/Cardenna" could theoretically denote the Spanish town Cardeña, so need not be Cervantes's story at all (LPD, citing 556–57).
  2. 557–58: Moseley's 1653 ascription is unreliable ("Moseley seems to take liberties with some names, particularly those of authors who could be published lucratively in quartos or en masse in large folios"); she suggests reading the entry's "F" as standing for Fletcher alone in a hurried list (my earlier notes: "'F for Fletcher', 557").
  3. 558: Fletcher's known Cervantes-based plotting habits (Love's Pilgrimage, The Chances, The Coxcomb, Knight of the Burning Pestle by Beaumont): *Cardenio*, "if it descends from Cervantes's Don Quixote (as its title suggests), mimics the particular plotting habits [Fletcher] displays elsewhere"; Fletcher "the more obviously plot-reliant of the two" and likely supplied the plot (Alotaibi p. 21).
  4. 563–66: "the most comprehensive reading of Theobald's manuscripts' account" (Alotaibi p. 9): Theobald claimed three, "possibly four", manuscripts; no one else ever saw them; the story of the natural daughter; the inconsistency between the Downes copy "of above sixty years standing" and a Betterton design to stage it; Theobald's failure to print the play in his 1733 *Works* or to produce the promised "Dissertation"; the 1770 *Gazetteer* notice and the 1808 fire as an unfalsifiable provenance. Alotaibi (p. 12) paraphrases her: had a Shakespeare manuscript sat in Covent Garden from before the 1770s to 1808 "it is barely conceivable that the great editors … Malone, Steevens, Johnson, and Capell, would not have gone to see it, or that … David Garrick would not have devoted his life to acquiring it."
  5. 576–79: Theobald as a practised imitator of Shakespeare (*The Cave of Poverty*, 1715, "an imitation of Shakespeare's early narrative style"; *Orestes* admitted in letters to Warburton to be Shakespeare-plagiary; *The Perfidious Brother* plagiarism dispute with Mestayer); hence the Shakespearean "feel" of DF proves nothing.
  6. 582: Shelton's 1612 translation was reprinted (1725) and available to Theobald.
  7. 588–89: Theobald's contradictory claims — that he wrote 1.3.10–14 himself, but that the derided 3.1.17 "was definitely Shakespeare's".
  8. 590: Farmer's "W.Sh." = William Shirley reading.
* **No scene assignments** (the article denies that authorial layers can be recovered).
* **Rebuttals** (the NOS Companion, p. 585, lists "more than a dozen scholars"): Taylor 2012a/b, Jackson 2012, Proudfoot 2012, King 2012, Carnegie 2012, Taylor & Nance 2012, Pascucci 2012, Taylor & Wagschal 2013, Baker 2013, Nance 2013, Spiller 2013, Taylor 2013, Tarlinskaja 2014 (203–11), Hammond 2014, Boyd & Pennebaker 2015, Hume 2016, Marsden 2016, Taylor 2015/2016, Solomon 2016; Kirwan 2015 (177) "also rejects Stern's case for forgery" and explains the 1733 omission as "a defensively 'safe' move designed to preserve his editorial 'reputation' against further attacks by Pope" [DIRECT]. Taylor 2016 (353–54) on the UCLA symposium of 31 Jan–1 Feb 2014 (Hume, Folkenflik, Marsden, Payne, Solomon, Pennebaker, Hammond): "none of the speakers endorsed Stern's argument" and "the accusation of forgery no longer needs to be addressed" [DIRECT]. **Supporters**: Alotaibi 2016/2021 (Fletcher-only source rather than pure forgery); Vickers (unconvinced by Shakespeare); Kahan; Frazier; poemshape.

### 2.30 Ryan L. Boyd & James W. Pennebaker, "Did Shakespeare Write Double Falsehood? Identifying Individuals by Creating Psychological Signatures With Text Analysis", *Psychological Science* 26.5 (May 2015), 570–582, DOI 10.1177/0956797614566658, PMID 25854277 `[ABSTRACT verbatim via NCBI efetch (boyd-pennebaker-2015-pubmed-abstract.txt); body via UT Austin press release (DIRECT), Notes in the Margin (DIRECT), Laurie Johnson in *The Conversation* 14 May 2015 (DIRECT); SAGE 403]`
* **Abstract (verbatim)**: "More than 100 years after Shakespeare's death, Lewis Theobald published Double Falsehood, a play supposedly sourced from a lost play by Shakespeare and John Fletcher. Since its release, scholars have attempted to determine its true authorship. Using new approaches to language and psychological analysis, we examined Double Falsehood and the works of Theobald, Shakespeare, and Fletcher. Specifically, we created a psychological signature from each author's language and statistically compared the features of each signature with those of Double Falsehood's signature. Multiple analytic approaches converged in suggesting that Double Falsehood's psychological style and content architecture predominantly resemble those of Shakespeare, showing some similarity with Fletcher's signature and only traces of Theobald's. Closer inspection revealed that Shakespeare's influence is most apparent early in the play, whereas Fletcher's is most apparent in later acts. Double Falsehood has a psychological signature consistent with that expected to be present in the long-lost play The History of Cardenio, cowritten by Shakespeare and Fletcher."
* **Corpus**: 33 Shakespeare plays, 12 Theobald works, 9 Fletcher plays (UT press release). **Method**: LIWC (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count) function-word categories (pronouns, articles, prepositions…) and psychological content categories (emotion, family, sensory, religion); the Meaning Extraction Method (factor analysis of content words) for "content architecture"; three classification approaches; "all three models designating Shakespeare as the best authorial candidate … when considering the eight general function-word classes" (search summary quoting the paper) [WEB-SUMMARY]. Personality inference: the author was "probably sociable and well educated — findings that don't jibe with accounts of Theobald as well educated but also rigid and abrasive" (press release).
* **Act-level result**: "Boyd and Pennebaker identified Shakespeare as the likely author for the first three acts; the measures varied between Shakespeare and Fletcher for the fourth and fifth acts" (press release) [DIRECT]. Johnson's tally (*Conversation*): "Five types of word classes or categories were tested, each using three different computational methods (so there were 15 different tests for each play) … In 39 out of 45 tests on the first three Acts, Shakespeare comes out on top as the closest match. In the last two Acts, though, Shakespeare is closest on only 11 out of the 30 tests." Theobald's contribution "very minor" (Wikipedia quoting the paper).
* **Critiques**: Laurie Johnson (Univ. of Southern Queensland), *The Conversation*, 14 May 2015: "we cannot independently verify that the psychological signature they attribute to Shakespeare is in fact his — we simply don't know enough about him outside of his writing"; two unknowns each used to prove the other; the results "varied" across acts [DIRECT]. General stylometric objections (not DF-specific, but applicable): act-level rather than scene-level units mix Fletcher's 3.3 with Shakespeare's 3.1–3.2; no Davenant or Massinger candidate; no control for adaptation; unequal corpora. **Replications**: none found. Boyd's later Behn paper (*PLOS ONE* 2018, 10.1371/journal.pone.0200588) cites the DF study for "near-100% accuracy" author discrimination but does not re-analyse DF [DIRECT]; the Frontiers 2018 RPAS paper (10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00289) does not analyse DF [DIRECT]. Taylor 2016 and NOS Companion cite it as one more refutation of forgery; Tarlinskaja/Pascucci implicitly contradict its "Shakespeare throughout Acts 1–3" by finding Davenant/Theobald layers there.

### 2.31 Gary Taylor, "Fake Shakespeare", *Journal of Early Modern Studies* 5 (2016), 353–379, DOI 10.13128/JEMS-2279-7149-18095 `[DIRECT — taylor-2016-fake-shakespeare-jems5.pdf/.txt]`
* **Position** (p. 353): "Lewis Theobald's Double Falsehood is a Georgian adaptation of a Jacobean play by Fletcher and Shakespeare. It is not a forgery, as Tiffany Stern (2011) contends." (354): "I will therefore assume, in this essay, that the accusation of forgery no longer needs to be addressed."
* **Method**: "comprehensive search of digital databases" (LION drama 1576–1642 plus Theobald's works; appendix pp. 371–79 gives the language data; asterisked items unparalleled in LION) applied to three Leonora speeches, plus theatrical/dramaturgical argument (Wilks's and Mrs Porter's parts).
* **Passage-level attributions**: (a) 5.2.251–257 ("The righteous Pow'rs at length have crown'd our Loves…") → Theobald: Fletcher never collocates 'righteous' and 'power(s)'; the adjective appears sixteen times in Theobald's smaller canon, ten times of deities (Appendix A); 'Julio' in the added line — "The name 'Julio' occurs another twenty times in Leonora's speeches, and all those twenty lines must be either (a) written in their entirety, like this one, by Theobald, or (b) rewritten by Theobald to accommodate the changed name" (p. 362); "With 'Cardenio' we can be certain, and with 'Fernando' reasonably confident, of the original names." (b) 5.2.94–101 ("For such sad Rites must be perform'd…") → Fletcher: "Anyone who has read and studied all of John Fletcher's work will recognize this as Fletcherian (and scholars have done so for a century) … Fletcher, not Theobald or Shakespeare, overwhelmingly dominates the language of this speech (Appendix B)." (p. 356) (c) the Julio–Leonora exchange "No Impediment / Shall bar my Wishes … My Flames are in the Flint … Let Freedom quit me" (cited "5.2.109-116" but, as the text makes clear, from "the encounter of Julio … and Leonora … in the middle of 1.2") → Shakespeare: "Shakespeare, not Theobald or Fletcher, overwhelmingly dominates the language of these lines (Appendix C) … no other early modern playwright comes anywhere near the number of unique links between this passage and Shakespeare." (p. 357). (d) 5.1.25–37 (Henriquez–Leonora): "Leonora's entire encounter with Henriquez in 5.1 is Theobald's melodramatic addition to the scene" (p. 365) — 'I throw me at your feet' (5.1.26) "appears nowhere in English [drama before 1642]", 'Rise, rise' (5.1.36) Theobaldian; (e) 4.1.212–257: Henriquez's presence "theatrically awkward and his speeches reek of Theobald"; (f) "Much of his soliloquy in 2.1, and of his speeches in 2.3, is also Theobald's" (n. 18: "since Oliphant (1927), attribution scholars have found the most concentrated evidence of Theobald's hand in Act Two") (p. 365).
* **On reconstructions** (pp. 357–58): Greenblatt & Mee's *Cardenio* (2008) preserves "only about sixty-three of the lines published by Theobald … The wording has been changed in eleven of those lines (21%) … ruins the meter of seventeen lines (27%)"; "less than four percent of Double Falsehood"; includes Theobaldisms ('let the gay scene', 'by proxy', 'her charms', 'love is contagious') and skips the Shakespearean 1.2 exchange; Doran "cannot distinguish Theobald from Shakespeare" (having praised the Theobald speech as El Greco-like); "Jackson (2012) and Nance (2013) have demonstrated that the prose is much more authentically Jacobean and Shakespearian than the verse."
* **Critiques**: Alotaibi 2016 (ch. 3–4) re-searches Taylor's parallels in ECCO/LION and finds many "Shakespearean" phrases also in Theobald; argues Taylor cites only Theobald's plays on LION, ignoring his prose (*Censor*, letters), and that the *Cave of Poverty* test is biased. Vickers (unconvinced, general). No published stylometric replication of the appendix data.

### 2.32 Naseem A. Alotaibi, *Lewis Theobald's Double Falsehood: The Authorship Question Reconsidered* (PhD, Liverpool, July 2016), 300+ pp. `[DIRECT — alotaibi-2016-liverpool-phd-double-falsehood.pdf/.txt]`; "'[T]hat Other Great Poet': Double Falsehood, the Arden Shakespeare, and Further Evidence for Fletcher", *Notes and Queries* 68.2 (2021), 184–189 `[ABSTRACT via search]`

### 2.34 *The New Oxford Shakespeare: Authorship Companion*, ed. Gary Taylor & Gabriel Egan (OUP, 2017) `[DIRECT — nos-authorship-companion-2017-archiveorg.txt (OCR)]`
**(a) Marina Tarlinskaja, ch. 23, "The Versification of Double Falsehood Compared to Restoration and Early Classical Adaptations", pp. 385–406.**
* **Method**: stress-profile analysis (which syllable positions lose stress: Elizabethan/Restoration/18th-c. dip on 6 v. Jacobean–Caroline dip on 8); location of strong syntactic breaks (after 4 v. 6/7); enclitic phrases and heavy feminine endings per 1,000 lines; unstressed grammatical monosyllables in position 10; "rhythmical italics"; pleonastic *do* and grammatical inversions; disyllabic *-ion*. Stressing: "In Act 2 and Act 4 the dip in stressing on positions 6 and 8 is almost equal, as in Davenant's adaptation of Shakespeare's Macbeth and the non-Fletcher-derived parts of … The Rivals … In Act 3 and Act 5 … the strongest dip in stressing is on syllable 8, which is what we find in Jacobean drama generally." Enclitics: "In Double Falsehood Acts 1 and 2 and in scenes 3.1 and 3.2 there are 36.4, 33.2, and 52.9 enclitics per 1,000 lines, respectively. * **Conclusion (405–406, verbatim)**: "Theobald's claim has been in large part justified: he had indeed worked from an existing older script." "A counterfeit Shakespeare would not contain signs of an eighteenth-century rewrite in the first half of the play and signs of Fletcher in the second half. If it were an eighteenth-century forgery, as Stern thinks, its creator was remarkably skilful in thinking to use enclitic phrases and heavy feminine endings at all, and impossibly subtle to think to put them in just half of the play, the Fletcher scenes." "Thus, a seventeenth-century manuscript was indeed in Theobald's possession. It was probably based on the lost Cardenio, a collaboration. From what we know of Shakespeare–Fletcher collaborations, one of the co-authors of the lost play was clearly Fletcher. The other is harder to identify solely on internal evidence. There are few signs of Shakespearean verse style in Acts 1 and 2, but his was in any case a style of versification—with its run-on lines and light feminine endings—that was easy for either Davenant or Theobald to rewrite. There are numerous signs of Fletcher's idiosyncratic style. There are signs of an eighteenth-century author in Acts 1 and 2 and scenes 3.1 and 3.2. The versification suggests that what Theobald had in his possession was not the original Jacobean manuscript but a later, post-Restoration rewrite, possibly by Davenant. Thus Double Falsehood seems to contain three layers from different times: Shakespeare–Fletcher, Davenant, and Theobald. The original text was probably the lost Cardenio."

**(b) Giuliano Pascucci, ch. 24, "Using Compressibility as a Proxy for Shannon Entropy in the Analysis of Double Falsehood", pp. 407–416.**
* **Method**: relative entropy via a modified LZ77 compressor (Benedetto–Caglioti–Loreto): each DF scene appended to control sections of 32/16/8/4 KB from candidate authors; lower added compressed length = more similar. Candidates: Shakespeare, Fletcher, Massinger, Davenant, Theobald (p. 410: "William Shakespeare, John Fletcher, Philip Massinger, William Davenant, and Lewis Theobald"); controls include Massinger's *New Way*, *Renegado*, *Bondman*, *Bashful Lover*, Davenant's *Fair Favourite*, Theobald's *Fatal Secret* etc. "over half a million" string comparisons. * **Davenant-v-Theobald-only test** (p. 411): Davenant wins 2.1, 2.4, 3.1 — "Davenant left his fingerprint in 8 per cent of the whole play (144 lines out of 1815), or 21.5 per cent if we judge by the number of scenes he worked on (3 out of 14 in the whole play)"; the ratio "increases significantly once all the plausible candidates are included".
* **Scene-by-scene result (pp. 414–415, verbatim)**: "1.1 Theobald heavily revised Shakespeare / 1.2 Shakespeare / 1.3 Theobald revised Davenant who revised Shakespeare / 2.1 Davenant revised Shakespeare (Possibly slight revision by Theobald too) / 2.2 Shakespeare and Massinger collaborated / 2.3 Theobald revised Massinger / 2.4 Shakespeare and Fletcher and Massinger collaborated (Unreliable results) / 3.1 Davenant revised Shakespeare / 3.2 Davenant revised Shakespeare / 3.3 Davenant revised Fletcher / 4.1 Shakespeare and Fletcher collaborated / 4.2 Theobald revised Shakespeare / 5.1 Theobald revised Davenant / 5.2 Shakespeare and Fletcher collaborated".
* **Interpretation (415–416)**: "Double Falsehood scenes 2.2, 2.3, and 2.4 are the ones that most divide attribution scholars … we are confident of Massinger's contribution here. For scene 2.4 … our results are equivocal, although notably there are no signs of post-Jacobean writing." 3.1 and 3.2 "were so heavily revised by Davenant that they hardly retain their original Shakespearean elements, which in 3.2 could well correspond to lines 39-43, as Stephen Kukowski (1991, 88) suggested." "Although revised by Davenant, Double Falsehood scene 3.3 is the only one in which Fletcher's hand is strikingly apparent, whereas in scenes 4.1 and 5.2 it emerges only in the 4KB-section tests." "Scene 5.2 … seems to be mostly Shakespeare's and to a lesser extent Fletcher's. Our results sketch a slightly different scenario from the nowadays widely accepted belief that Shakespeare's presence is limited to the first half of the play." **Conclusion**: "On the evidence presented here, the possibility that Theobald forged Double Falsehood is eliminated. Theobald had a manuscript of a play containing contributions by Shakespeare and Fletcher, as many studies have shown, and, we believe, contributions by Massinger too. The likeliest explanation, then, is that Theobald had a manuscript of the lost play Cardenio." ### 2.37 Reconstructions — only their claims about what is original
* **Charles Hamilton, *Cardenio, or The Second Maiden's Tragedy* (1994)**: claims the 1611 manuscript play (BL Lansdowne 807) is *Cardenio* in Shakespeare's hand; universally rejected (Middleton's authorship established; "argued, with passion but little rigour" — search summary). Irrelevant to DF except as noise.
* **Stephen Greenblatt & Charles Mee, *Cardenio* (2008)**: a Curious-Impertinent play with a DF play-within; keeps c. 63 Theobald lines, mostly from the Julio–Leonora exchange "in the middle of 1.2", plus 4.1 lines (my earlier notes: 4.1.49–61), reworded 21% (Taylor 2016, 358) — no authorship claims beyond the "lost play by Shakespeare" billing.
* **Bernard Richards, *Cardenio* (Queens' College, Cambridge, March 2009; *Essays in Criticism* 2011; *Quest* 344–51)**: keeps Theobald's text, adds scenes "to explicate the early relations of Julio and Henriquez and to bring into close focus the rape of Violante and the abduction of Leonora from the nunnery in a coffin" [WEB-SUMMARY]; no line-level attribution.
* **Gregory Doran, RSC *Cardenio* (Swan, 2011; Nick Hern text; *Shakespeare's Lost Play: In Search of Cardenio*, 2012)**: "basically Theobald's text with additions" (Shakespeare Blog) [DIRECT]; missing scenes rebuilt from Shelton; "where extra lines were needed, I tried to limit myself to plundering only those Jacobean plays in which John Fletcher had drawn upon Cervantes" (Nick Hern blog) [DIRECT]; Doran's own attribution reasoning (Plays International, Feb 2011) [DIRECT]: "The more I read [Shelton], the more I realised that Double Falsehood did indeed contain Shakespeare and Fletcher because phrases from the Shelton just sprang about … 'This is clearly not just the source but Theobald has got the Shelton open while he's writing.'" — which is, of course, equally an argument that Theobald used Shelton directly. Taylor 2016 (356): Doran singled out the Theobaldian 5.2.251–57 as Shakespearean.
* **Gary Taylor, *The History of Cardenio, 1612–2012* (in *Creation*, 241–316; staged Indianapolis 2012; revised for NOS)**: the only reconstruction with explicit line-level claims — removes the passages listed in §2.31 (5.2.251–57; 5.1.25–37 Henriquez–Leonora; 4.1.212–57; much of 2.1 soliloquy and 2.3; Cibber prose in 2.3), restores 'Cardenio/Fernando/Luscinda', keeps the 1.2 prose and Julio–Leonora verse, 1.1's opening, 5.2.94–101 (Fletcher) etc.; supplies new lines "in the style of the original authors" [WEB-SUMMARY + Taylor 2016 DIRECT].

## 4. Strongest points for each hypothesis

### H1 — Fletcher–Shakespeare *Cardenio* underlies DF
1. External chain: 1613 court payments for a *Cardenno/Cardenna* by the King's Men (Shakespeare's and Fletcher's company) in the very season of their other collaborations; Moseley's 1653 "by Mr Fletcher. & Shakespeare"; the 1718 Tonson assignment; Gildon c.1710/1719 on a manuscript play of Shakespeare's maturity; Theobald's Downes/Betterton manuscript "of above sixty years standing" (Freehafer 1969; Hammond 2010; NOS 583–85).
2. The source is Shelton 1612 specifically — a text "not in Theobald's library catalogue, and which he never used elsewhere, or showed any awareness of" (NOS 585; Taylor & Wagschal 2013; Graham 1916 against Schevill); the title formula "History of Cardenio" is Shelton's phrase (Taylor 2012, 14–15).
3. Two distinct Jacobean styles in the text, with the break at 3.3, measured repeatedly: Bradford 47%/32% double endings; Graham 44.2%/32% feminine endings, weak-and-light endings 3/19; Tarlinskaja enclitics 36.4–52.9 v. 200 per 1,000 lines and disyllabic -ion 13.3/1,000 in 3.3 ≈ Fletcher's H8 13.9; "impossibly subtle" for a forger to plant Fletcher features only in half a play (Tarlinskaja 405–406).
4. Theobald denied Fletcher's presence (Preface) and never mentioned Moseley's entry — "absolutely incompatible with any possibility of Theobald's having set himself deliberately to imitate Fletcher" (Bradford 55; Oliphant 283).
5. Fletcher-specific lexis unknown to Theobald: 'o' my conscience', 'Stand off' (30× extended Fletcher canon, 0× Shakespeare), 'as I have a soul', 'all the miseries' (Jackson 2012); Fletcherian 5.2.94–101 (Taylor 2016 App. B).
6. Positive Shakespeare signals in the first half: 1.2 prose (Nance 2013: 334 words "strongly suggest the presence of Shakespeare more than any other author"); Julio–Leonora verse in 1.2 (Taylor 2016 App. C: unique links to Shakespeare unmatched by any other dramatist); Act 1 pleonastic *do* 16.2/1,000 (Theobald 4.3–4.7) and rhythmical italics 133.6/1,000 "typical of Shakespeare" (Tarlinskaja 402); Boyd & Pennebaker: Shakespeare closest in 39/45 tests on Acts 1–3; Pascucci: 1.2 "Shakespeare", Sh in 4.1, 5.2.
7. Line-end polysyllables — a feature "most unlikely to occupy the attention of a forger" — match Sh 63% / Fl 65% / both 79% v. Theobald 32% (Proudfoot 2012).
8. Theobald's known adaptations (*Richard II*, *Fatal Secret*) show exactly the kind of cutting, regularising and moralising visible in DF (Carnegie 2012; Tarlinskaja's *Fatal Secret* enclitic reduction 127.6 → 41.7); his one avowed imitation, *Cave of Poverty*, "reeks of Theobald" and fails the same LION test that DF's Shakespearean passages pass (Taylor 2013, 2016).
9. Consensus of specialists after 2012: "refuted by more than a dozen scholars" (NOS 585); "none of the speakers endorsed Stern's argument" at UCLA 2014 (Taylor 2016, 354); Hume 2016 — the leading sceptic-minded theatre historian — rejects forgery.

### H2 — Fletcher only, or Fletcher plus someone other than Shakespeare
1. Fletcher's presence is the one thing every stylistic study confirms (Bradford, Graham, Oliphant, Kukowski, Hope B, Jackson, Tarlinskaja, Pascucci 3.3, Matthews & Merriam); Shakespeare's is inferred largely from the external record — Bradford: "The internal evidence is certainly not of great importance … none is close enough to have much weight" (55); Oliphant: "one has to be guided largely by the external evidence and the probabilities" (296); Tarlinskaja: "There are few signs of Shakespearean verse style in Acts 1 and 2" (405–406).
2. Moseley's entry is unreliable and Fletcher-first: the full stop after "Fletcher", "alphabetical order of authorship", Moseley's other false Shakespeare ascriptions (Freehafer 508; Stern 557–58; Bradford 55 "far from reliable"); Moseley's own TNK notice called it Beaumont and Fletcher's.
3. Cervantes/Shelton material is Fletcher's known hunting-ground (Love's Pilgrimage, The Chances, The Coxcomb, Rule a Wife…; Hatcher 1905 via Graham; Stern 558; Alotaibi); Fletcher "the more obviously plot-reliant of the two" (Stern).
4. Whole-play stylometrics reject Shakespeare: E&V 11 discrete rejections (composite probability 1.376E-09), 14 in Scott's tabulation; Matthews & Merriam 1993 "strongly Fletcherian".
5. Every "Shakespearean" verbal parallel has been challenged by a fuller Theobald negative-check (Alotaibi 2016; Stern 2012, 129); the Beaumont check (Jackson) turned up 34 v. 48 three-word links, "trivial in the extreme".
6. Hume's verdict — "little or no unadulterated Shakespeare" — and Jackson's — "scarcely a line of Shakespeare's verse survives intact" — mean H1 and H2 differ only about the lost play, not about DF; the observable text supports "Fletcher + heavy later rewriting" at least as well as "Fletcher + Shakespeare + heavy later rewriting".
7. Pascucci's Massinger detections in 2.2–2.4 (and in H8) — if replicated — would revive Malone's candidate for the non-Fletcher hand.

### H3 — another Jacobean/Caroline play (Shirley; Massinger)
1. Farmer's accentual argument: "aspect" stressed on the first syllable twice, "never the case in the time of Shakespeare" (2nd ed. 26–27) — and a Shirley whose manuscripts Langbaine records as unpublished.
2. Malone's Massinger parallels (*Duke of Milan* "but itself admits no parallel"; "stoop to rise") and eleven lost Massinger plays in the SR; Gifford's independent sense of an Elizabethan/Jacobean hand superior to Theobald.
3. Proudfoot's own data: Massinger's early plays reach "over 20 per cent" line-end-polysyllable matching (higher than every other contemporary tested), explained away as Fletcherian indebtedness; Pascucci 2017 finds Massinger in 2.2, 2.3, 2.4.
4. Nobody has ever run a Shirley test; Stern 2012 and the RenRef reviewer note that Massinger and Shirley "have not been addressed adequately"; Forsythe (via Graham 1920) wanted Daborne, Davenport and William Rowley tried and nobody has.
5. If Moseley's ascription is discounted (H2.2), nothing external ties DF's source to 1613 rather than to a later Fletcher-circle play; the "sixty years" manuscript only reaches back to c.1667.
   (Counter-weight: Bradford found "none of these 'marks of style and manner'" of Shirley; the Shelton-1612 dependence and King's Men 1613 *Cardenno* make a Fletcher–Massinger or Shirley *Cardenio* an unnecessary multiplication of plays; Farmer's accent test ignores Theobald's admitted revision.)

### H4 — Theobald forged it (with or without a Restoration source)
1. No one ever saw the manuscripts; Theobald's account of them is internally inconsistent (Downes copy c.1667 yet "designed" by Betterton for the stage; natural-daughter legend; three-then-four copies), he never printed the play in his 1733 *Works*, never delivered the promised "Dissertation", and the 1770 "Museum of Covent Garden" notice is unverifiable and conveniently ends in the 1808 fire (Stern 563–66; Alotaibi 9–26; Halliwell-Phillipps).
2. The 1653 entry was public (Steevens found the 1613 records only in 1780, but the SR was searchable); Theobald could have learned the title from Gildon/Moseley/Tonson (Freehafer himself says Theobald "learned otherwise, from Gildon and the Stationers' Register") — so "he never claimed Fletcher" cuts both ways.
3. Theobald was a practised imitator and adapter (*Cave of Poverty*; *Orestes* "plagiary" letters to Warburton; *Perfidious Brother* dispute; his *Richard II*), with Shelton available in the 1725 reprint (Stern 577–82; Frazier 1974; Kahan 2004; Alotaibi ch. 3).
4. Theobald's contradictory ownership claims — 1.3.10–14 his own, 3.1.17 "definitely Shakespeare's" — and Malone's "I believe both assertions to have been false" (Lupić 107).
5. Whole-play stylometrics reject Shakespeare (E&V), and the "Shakespearean" parallels dissolve under a full Theobald negative check (Stern 2012; Alotaibi).
6. Every "authenticating" feature (Fletcherian enclitics, Shelton phrasing) is consistent with Theobald "collaborating" with any old Fletcher-circle manuscript, or with a Restoration adaptation he possessed — the weak form of H4 that Hammond's own "palimpsest" model concedes.
   (Counter-weight: the strong form — no old manuscript at all — is now rejected by every quantitative study since 2012, by Hume, and by Stern's own critics as requiring a forger "impossibly subtle" in Jacobean prosody, Shelton phrasing and Fletcher lexis he had no motive to imitate.)

## 5. What each side says would overturn its position

* **H1 proponents** (Taylor, Jackson, Proudfoot, Tarlinskaja, Pascucci, Hammond, NOS): would be overturned by (a) a demonstration that Theobald's own oeuvre contains the Fletcher-specific and Shakespeare-specific features at comparable rates (the Byrne/Stern "negative check" — Taylor's *Cave of Poverty* test is their pre-emptive answer); (b) documentary proof that the 1613 *Cardenno* was not a Cervantes play or not the play Moseley entered (Stern's Cardeña reading); (c) a Massinger/Shirley/other test outperforming Shakespeare on the non-Fletcher scenes with equal corpora (Proudfoot and Jackson explicitly invite it; Pascucci's Massinger result is the nearest thing); (d) discovery of the Restoration text showing a different source play. Taylor 2016 (354) states that refutation of Stern is now so complete that forgery "no longer needs to be addressed" — i.e. only new documents or new negative-check failures would move him.
* **H2 proponents** (Alotaibi; Stern 2012 in part; Hume implicitly): would be overturned by (a) scene-level, equal-corpus tests in which Shakespeare beats Fletcher, Massinger, Beaumont, Davenant *and* Theobald on the first-half scenes (Nance's prose test and Taylor's App. C are the existing attempts; Alotaibi rejects them as corpus-biased); (b) an independent replication of Pascucci's 1.2/4.1/5.2 Shakespeare signals with a different method; (c) any document confirming Moseley's double attribution (e.g. the Tonson 1718 assignment's wording, or a Betterton/Davenant record naming Shakespeare).
* **H3 proponents** (Farmer/Malone tradition; nobody living): would be overturned by direct Shirley/Massinger tests failing (Proudfoot's ">20%" for Massinger is the only data point, and Bradford's negative reading of Shirley the only Shirley test); would be *revived* by replication of Pascucci's Massinger detections.
* **H4 proponents** (Stern 2011; Frazier; Kahan; Alotaibi in weak form): Stern's article frames the question as one of evidence, so a verified pre-1727 witness to the manuscripts (the "Noble Person", Betterton's or Downes's papers, the Covent Garden "Museum" inventory), or a demonstration that Theobald could not have known Shelton 1612 (as opposed to 1725) would end the strong form; Alotaibi concedes that "there is some external evidence, which although it does not confirm Theobald's claims, nevertheless suggests that there might be some truth behind them" (thesis p. 26). The weak form (Restoration-adaptation source) is not falsifiable by stylometry alone — it needs the 1660s text.

## 6. What an investigator should know

1. Every quantitative study since 1910 finds two Jacobean styles with a break at 3.3; Fletcher after that point is the single most secure finding in the whole literature.
2. Shakespeare's presence rests on the external chain (1613 payments + Moseley 1653 + Shelton-1612 source) plus a handful of internal signals (1.2 prose and verse; Act 1 versification; 1.1 opening; Pascucci's 1.2/5.2) — not on any large body of intact Shakespearean verse. Jackson: "scarcely a line … survives intact"; Hume: "little or no unadulterated Shakespeare"; LPD: "Few critics claim that substantial chunks … remain".
3. The strong forgery thesis (Stern 2011) has no current defenders among stylometricians or theatre historians; the NOS lists "more than a dozen" refutations. Treat it as answered, but note that most refutations were written by the *Quest*/NOS group (Taylor, Jackson, Proudfoot, Nance, Pascucci, Tarlinskaja); the independent voices are Hume, Boyd & Pennebaker, Hammond.
4. The live alternative is not forgery but H2: a Fletcher (± Massinger/Beaumont) play, or a Fletcher–Shakespeare play in which Shakespeare's share is unrecoverable. Alotaibi 2016/2021 is the only sustained modern case; nobody has run Shirley or a proper equal-corpus Massinger test.
5. The consensus transmission model is three layers — Jacobean original → Davenant-era adaptation (c.1663–67; Downes/Betterton) → Theobald 1727 (+ Cibber in 2.3 per NOS). Tarlinskaja and Pascucci independently locate the Davenant layer in Acts 2 and 3.1–3.2; this is why "Shakespeare's" first half looks eighteenth-century in verse profile.
6. Theobald's hand is densest in Act 2 (2.3 and 2.4 "wholly his" — Oliphant; Proudfoot; Taylor), in 4.1.212–57, 5.1.25–37 and 5.2.251–57 (Taylor 2016) — the very passages Doran and Greenblatt–Mee admired as "Shakespearean".
7. Boyd & Pennebaker 2015 is the most-cited but methodologically weakest pro-Shakespeare study (act-level units, no Davenant/Massinger candidates, unverifiable "psychological signature"); no replication exists.
8. The only truly hostile modern audit of the pro-Shakespeare parallels (Alotaibi) shows that Theobald's *full* corpus (prose, letters, translations) must be the negative check; Jackson, Proudfoot and Taylor used mainly his plays.
9. Corpus imbalance is endemic: Jackson 50 Fl / 39 Sh / 15 Th; Proudfoot 15 / 13 / 9; Boyd 33 Sh / 9 Fl / 12 Th.
10. Pascucci 2017 is the outlier on both ends: it eliminates forgery but finds Massinger (2.2–2.4) and "mostly Shakespeare" in 5.2; unreplicated.
11. Elliott & Valenza's 11 rejections say only that DF *as a whole* is not solo Shakespeare — which nobody claims; their half-play test was announced (2010) but never published.
12. Hope 1994's actual auxiliary-*do* figures were not obtainable here; the book must be consulted (pp. 89–100).
13. Not accessible in full: Stern 2011, Hammond 2010/2012/2014, all *Quest* and *Creation* chapters, Kukowski, Pujante, Muir, Frazier, Metz, Kahan, Lupić, Folkenflik, Tarlinskaja 2014/2023, ODNB; their content above is second-hand (chiefly via Alotaibi's thesis, Taylor 2016 and the NOS Companion, all read directly).
14. No new documentary evidence and no new quantitative study of DF has appeared 2018–2026 that I could find; the field is static since NOS 2017.
15. Reconstructions (Doran, Greenblatt–Mee, Richards) make no defensible line-level authorship claims; Taylor's unadaptation is the only one built on explicit attribution arguments and is itself contested (Alotaibi).
16. Reference works now converge on "adaptation of a Fletcher–Shakespeare *Cardenio* via a Restoration version" (Arden, NOS, RSC, Wikipedia, LPD), with Britannica still calling the hoax "plausible".
17. If the project wants a decisive new test, the gap is clear: equal-sized, scene-level comparison of DF 1.1–3.2 against Shakespeare, Fletcher, Massinger, Beaumont, Davenant *and* the complete Theobald corpus, with the 1660s adaptations of TNK/H8 (*The Rivals*) as calibration — no such study exists.
