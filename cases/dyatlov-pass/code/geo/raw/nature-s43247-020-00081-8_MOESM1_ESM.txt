Summary:  
The authors investigate an unsolved accident from the last century and provide physical 
explanations to attribute this accident to an avalanche. They show that under certain conditions the

digging of a platform for a tent, just above the victims which were severely but to lethally injured. 
More generally, this case study is used to support the development of new analytical (coupling of 
loading and sintering) and numerical methods (Material Point Method) in the field of avalanche.  
 
Major comments:

exciting but still with sound science. To my knowledge, the main novelty in terms of methodology is

Response 
In our simulations, we indeed simulated the body using a hyperelastic model (St-Venant Kirchhoff) 
whose parameters are calibrated based on crash test experiments performed in the automotive industry 
(elastic modulus calibration to reproduce chest deflection). This choice allowed us to simulate the 
body as a bulk without representing individual bones which would require a resolution incompatible 
with the scales of avalanche dynamics. Concerning hyper-elasticity and MPM, let us reply in two 
steps:  
1) hyper-elasticity is designed for materials in which the elastic deformation can be very large. In 
addition, the existence of the strain energy density function made hyper-elasticity easy to implement 
in FEM dynamic simulations using finite strain theory. As a consequence, it has been largely adopted 
as well in MPM algorithms used for simulating large deformation problems and which are also based 
on the discretization of the weak form of the momentum balance equation. Hence, hyper-elasticity is 
very well simulated by MPM, but the question of the referee may also concern the accuracy of the 
solution, in terms of stresses, which we address below. 
2) historically, MPM is known to have stress inaccuracies, in particular due to discontinuities induced 
by linear shape functions which were originally used. Since the early development of MPM by Sulsky 
and co-authors, a lot of progress has been done, in particular with respect to shape functions. In fact, 
most recent MPM models use higher order shape functions (iMPM) which prevents this issue. In our 
work, we use cubic B-spline shape functions which guaranties the continuity of the gradients at the 
nodes (C2 functions). We also have convergence under mesh refinement, but we must accept that the 
convergence rate in MPM is not as good as with FEM as shown in many studies (e.g. Vaucorbeil et al.

Introduction 28 
During the night of February 1, 1959, nine Russian hikers died under unexplained 29 
circumstances during a skiing expedition in the northern Ural Mountains. The group had 30 
decided to set up their camp on the slope of the Kholat Saykhl (Fig. 1a); the name means “Dead 31 
Mountain” in the local Mansi dialect. Something unexpected happened after midnight that 32 
caused expedition members to cut the tent suddenly from the inside and escape towards a forest 33 
1 km downslope (Fig. 1b), without appropriate clothes, under extremely low temperatures 34 
(below −25°C, see supplementary material SM3), and in the presence of strong katabatic winds 35 
induced by the passing of an arctic cold front1. 36 
Twenty-six days to two months after the trage dy, search teams found bodies in the forest and 37 
on their way back to the tent. According to the 1959 Soviet criminal investigation, "a compelling 38 
natural force" led to the death of the Dyatlov group. However, the nature of this force has not 39 
been identifie d. The mystery arises from numerous unexplained observations. While 40 
hypothermia was determined to be the main cause of death, four hikers had severe thorax or 41 
skull injuries; two were found with missing eyes and one without tongue; some were almost 42 
naked and barefoot; traces of radioactivity were found on some of their clothes; and signs of 43 
glowing orange spheres floating in the sky were reported that night1. 44 
Several theories have been proposed to explain this incident, including infrasound- induced 45 
panic, a nimals, attacks by Yetis or local tribesmen, katabatic winds, a snow avalanche, a 46 
romantic dispute, nuclear-weapons tests, etc. The originally popular avalanche theory has been 47 
questioned due to several contradictory pieces of evidence1: 1) no obvious signs of an avalanche 48 
or debris were reported by the search team that arrived 26 days later; 2) the average slope angle 49 
above the tent location was not sufficiently steep  for an avalanche (lower than 30°); 3) the 50 
hypothetical avalanche released during the night, at least nine hours after the cut was made in 51 
the slope; and 4) the thorax and skull injuries were not typical for avalanche victims. 52 
  
3 
 
In 2015, the Investigative Committee of the Russian Federation  (ICRF) re -opened the  53 
investigation and in 2019 concluded that a snow avalanche was the most probable cause of the 54 
accident2,3. The results of this investigation have been challenged recently by the office of the 55 
Prosecutor General of the Russian Federation, who in 2019 started its own investigation and in 56 
July 2020 came to same conclusion as ICRF . Both investigation have not , however, provided 57 
scientific explanations for the four counterarguments listed above  and therefore keep being 58 
challenged by the relatives, public and researchers. In particular, a 2019 Swedish- Russian 59 
expedition disagreed with the ICRF conclusions, instead proposing that the dire ct impact of 60 
katabatic winds on the tent was the main contributing factor. 61 
Based on the significant amount of published material 1-9, it seems that previous investigations 62 
lack an important ingredient: a quantifiable physical mechanism that can reconcile th e 63 
avalanche hypothesis with seemingly conflicting evidence. Identifying such a mechanism may 64 
provide new insights into the nature of storm-triggered snowpack instabilities, which is another 65 
important motivation for this work. 66 
Here, we show that —even though the occurrence of an avalanche at this location is unlikely 67 
under natural conditions—the combination of four critical factors allowed the release of a small 68 
snow slab directly above the tent. These factors include (Fig. 1c ): (1) the location of the tent 69 
under a shoulder  in a locally steeper slope to protect them from the wind; (2) a buried weak 70 
snow layer parallel to the locally steeper terrain, which resulted in a thinning snow slab; (3) the 71 
cut in the snow slab made by the group to install the tent; (4) strong katabatic winds that led to 72 
progressive snow accumulation due to the local topography (shoulder above the tent) causing a 73 
delayed failure. Furthermore, the possible construction of a parapet1 above the cut (a classical 74 
safety procedure to protect the tent from the wind, Fig. SM3) could have accelerated the failure 75 
process. The proposed physical mechanism couples the onset of dynamic  shear-fracture 76 
propagation in the weak snow layer with wind-induced snow transport. Our model shows that 77

the conditions for an avalanche release were met after a delay of 7.5 to 13.5 hours from the 78 
moment the hikers made the cut in the slope , in agreement with the forensic evaluation of t he 79 
time of death. Dynamic avalanche simulations suggest that even a relatively small slab could 80 
have led to severe but non- lethal thorax and skull injuries, as reported by the post -mortem 81 
examination1. 82

Results 91 
Local topography 92 
The mountain slope at the location of the tent is highly irregular. Around 100 m above the tent, 93 
there is a shoulder which separates a rather flat plateau and a steeper slope below (Fig. 2a). This 94 
slope consists of 4-6 m high steps1 (Fig. 1c and Fig. SM2) and the tent was installed below one 95 
of them, where it was easier to make a cut in a locally flatter slope . The choice of the tent 96 
location was also likely driven by the fact that the larger scale shoulder  would protect them 97 
from the strong winds. In reality, as we show below, this choice of location could have 98 
contributed to the accident: small scale topograph ic variability resulted a locally steep weak 99 
snow layer while the larger shoulder contributed to significant wind-driven snow accumulation 100 
above the tent, eventually leading to an instability. 101 
Slope angle and friction 102 
Major arguments against the avalanche hypothesis include insufficient signs of the occurrence 103 
of an avalanche (no apparent deposit or crown fracture) and the relatively mild slope (~23° +/− 104 
2°). It appears, however, that the ground surface in the vicinity of the estimated tent location 105 
was steeper  (up to 30°)  than the average snow slope . Subsequent snowfalls smoothed the 106 
irregular terrain to the observed average 23° slope. This implies directly that the slab above the 107 
layer of depth hoar was thinner uphill, which has three main consequences: 1)  It results in the 108 
average slope of the buried weak layer being about 28°, increasing the probability of slab release 109 
(Fig 2b). 2) It reduces tensile support at the top of the slab, considerably limiting the avalanche 110 
volume. 3) It makes it more difficult to trace avalanche signs, especially 26 days after the 111 
incident. 112 
While a 28° slope may be perceived as being too mild to cause an avalanche —based on the 113 
implicit rule of thumb that a minimum of 30° is required 10—in reality, the critical slope angle 114 
  
6 
 
can be as low11 as 20° (Fig. 2b), provided the dynamic friction angle (sometimes called crack-115 
face friction11) of the snow is sufficiently low. In fact, field measurements have shown that the 116 
dynamic friction angle of snow can be as low as 15°, especially for very low temperatures12. In 117 
particular, the buried crystals of depth hoar, which were reported by the invest igation on the 118 
site (see SM3), tend to exhibit rather low friction values13 (Fig. 2c). 119 
Additional load due to wind-transported snow 120 
In spite of the fact that the 28° inclination of the buried weak layer was higher than the angle 121 
of friction, the snowpack did not fail for at least nine hours after the slope was cut (see SM1), 122 
due to cohesion in the weak layer. In principle, overcoming c ohesion does not necessarily 123 
require additional loading. Recent work on delayed snow avalanches 14 has proposed a release 124 
mechanism driven by rate -dependent processes in the snow slab and the weak layer 10, which 125 
can develop under constant loads. It cannot, however, accommodate a nine-hour delay, due to 126 
the relatively short extent of the slab. It follows that the true mechanism must involve additional 127 
loading of the slope. Given the extremely low temperatures and strong katabatic winds, it is 128 
unlikely that anyone would have climbed above the tent during the night, disturbing the weak 129 
layer. In the absence of significant snowfall, the only way to accumulate additional load is 130 
through wind transport15.  131 
Snow accumulation above the tent resulted from katabatic winds and the presence of a shoulder 132 
located above the tent (Fig. 2a). A possible construction of a small snow parapet by the Dyatlov 133 
group (classical safety measure for snow camping, Fig. SM3), could contribute to additional 134 
loading.  135 
Below, we present an analytical model—which reproduces the forensic estimate of delay—for 136 
a thinning snow slab that is gradually loaded by wind- transported snow above the cut in the 137 
slope. Subsequent numerical modeling confirms that the observed injuries of the Dyatlov group 138 
members are consistent with the failed-slab dynamics. 139

Impact on a human body 203 
In addition to explaining the delay, our proposed mechanism provides the pre -failure slab 204 
geometry, which can help in understanding how a relatively small slab caused the severe but 205 
non-fatal injuries reported. We address this question by combining a novel numerical model 206 
  
11 
 
with existing data for human -thorax injuries from impact tests p erformed by the automotive 207 
industry17,18. 208 
Three-dimensional numerical simulations based on the Material Point Method (MPM) and 209 
finite-strain elastoplasticity19 (see Methods) show that this small -slab avalanche impacted the 210 
hikers lying on the tent floor and filled the excavated space but did not have a significant runout, 211 
consistent with the reported lack of clear avalanche signs (Fig. 5). The predicted length of 5.0  212 
m for tensile failure of the slab is in agreement with the analytical model. The simulated snow 213 
slab reached a velocity around 2 m/s upon impact  (see Extended Data Fig . 1, SM5 and  214 
supplementary videos 1-2). At this velocity, an impact on a human thorax of a typical snow 215 
block with a volume of 0.125–0.5 m 3 and density 400 kg/m 3 results in a maximum thorax 216 
deformation between 28% and 34%, corresponding to the lower range of values reported from 217 
crash tests by Kroell et al.18 for a 10 kg mass impacting the thorax at a speed of 7 m/s  (Fig. 5, 218 
SM5 and supplementary videos 3-6). According to the Abbreviated Injury Scale18 (AIS), these 219 
deflections would mostly lead to non- fatal thoracic injuries from moderate to severe, in 220 
agreement with the autopsy report of the Dyatlov-incident criminal investigation. Such injuries 221 
are not usually ob served in avalanche victims, because impacts rarely occur against stiff 222 
obstacles. In the Dyatlov case, the victims were trapped between the falling slab and the tent 223 
floor, which was placed on compacted snow reinforced by skis1. 224

Discussion 234 
Significant progress in snow and avalanche research over the past two decades19-23 has allowed 235 
better understanding of avalanche dynamics and of the processes related to snow-slab avalanche 236 
release24-28. Nevertheless, no mechanism similar to the one inspired by the Dyatlov mystery has 237 
been explored in the literature, and its physical quantification required new theoretical 238 
developments. 239 
In our analytical model, these developments include a snow slab with a spatially variable 240 
thickness and its evolution due to sintering of the wind- transported snow, which affects the  241 
instability of a buried weak snow layer. This is  highly relevant for the study of natural storm -242 
triggered slab avalanches 29 because its application is not limited only to wind blowing snow 243 

  
13 
 
events but can also account for additional loads due to a snowfall . The variable slab geometry 244 
resulting from irregular local topography and the cut made in the slope  play a critical role in 245 
determining whether or when an instability will occur. Our numerical simulation of the impact 246 
of a snow avalanche on a human body constrained by an obsta cle combine s advanced 247 
elastoplastic constitutive models with large -deformation dynamic numerical analysis (MPM) 248 
and biomechanical modeling of the human body. This opens new perspectives for research on 249 
the effects of snow avalanches on human health and safety. 250 
Needless to say that our models are based on a number of assumptions, which can be justified 251 
for this particular case study and relaxed for future research. For example, given the very low 252 
reported temperatures, we assumed a brittle behavior for the w eak layer which allows 253 
neglecting the effects of the process zone 30. Moreover, weak layer volumetric collapse 19, 22, 23 254 
did not have to be accounted for in our approach because this layer remains completely intact 255 
before the onset of instability. Furthermore, the analytical model assumes a 2D geometry which 256 
in this case can be justified by the fact that the length of the shoulder  controlling snow 257 
deposition is much larger than the length of the tent. Yet, the 2D profile of the deposited snow 258 
has been simplified for the sake of obtaining a closed-form solution. Another important source 259 
of uncertainty lies in the dependency of the wind deposition flux on the average wind velocity. 260 
The available research15 shows a very wide range of measured deposition fluxes for a relatively 261 
narrow range of the average wind velocity. Nevertheless, the range of wind velocity back-262 
calculated using the analytical model and the  forensic estimation of the delay is in good 263 
agreement with the range reported in nearby weather stations during the night of the accident. 264 
Concerning the numerical models, because our main focus was the global thorax response, the 265 
skeleton and individual ribs were not analyzed. Focusing on the thorax impact, the MPM 266 
simulation is initiated at the onset of slab release and the weak layer is not explicitly modeled. 267 
In spite of these simplifications, both the analytical and numerical models independently 268 
  
14 
 
predicted a similar size of the failed slab, providing additional validation for the new 269 
mechanism. In addition, while our simulations show that in principle, the observed injuries 270 
could have resulted from the avalanche impact, the impact-induced deformations of the thorax 271 
would be rather sensitive to the size of the disintegrated slab blocks (Fig. 5) and thus to the 272 
relative positions of the bodies with respect to the cut and slope direction. Given this 273 
uncertainty, it is also possible1 that the thorax injuries were the result of a later snow impact in 274 
a very steep ravine where the bodies of the victims, escaping the avalanche area, were found. 275 
Solving the Dyatlov Pass mystery is an enormous task, which is far beyond the scope of this 276 
paper. We hope, however, that our work may contribute to determining the plausibility of the 277 
avalanche hypothesis. More importantly, it allows the quantification of conditions that can help 278 
to prevent similar incidents. Clearly, for a cut in the snow slope to cause a delayed slab release, 279 
it requires a relatively rare combination of: i) a sufficiently steep, weak layer at the base of the 280 
snowpack, ii) a cut in the slope and 3) significant snow accumulation after the cut due to wind 281 
transport. However, once these conditions are present, the occurrence of a delayed release 282 
requires fairly common values of geometrical and mechanical parameters (see Fig. 4), and 283 
Dyatlov-related investigations have indeed reported a non- negligible number of similar 284 
accidents1 (see SM2). This implies that building a tent even on a relatively mild slope (less than 285 
30°) can be dangerous and should not be recommended when combined with a cut in the slope. 286 
Instead, digging a snow cave may be a safer solution, as confirmed by the increasing use of this 287 
practice for winter camping in recent decades31.  288 
In conclusion, our work shows the plausibilit y of a rather rare type of snow slab instabilities 289 
that could possibly explain the Dyatlov Pass incident. Yet, we do not explain nor address other 290 
controversial elements surrounding the  investigation such as traces of radioactivity found on 291 
the victims’ garments, the behaviour of the hikers after leaving the tent, locations and states of 292 
bodies, etc. While possible explanations are given in multiple published sources 1-9 as well as 293 
  
15 
 
by both the Investigative Committee and the Prosecutor General of the Russian Federation, we 294 
believe that this will always remain an intrinsic part of the Dyatlov Pass Mystery. 295

Modeling the impact of the avalanche using the Material Point Method 547 
We simulate the snow slab dynamics and snow block impacts  using the model recently 548 
developed by Gaume et a l.19. This model uses the Material Point Method, finite strain 549 
elastoplasticity and a Cohesive Cam Clay constitutive relationship to simulate snow and 550 
avalanche mechanics. We recall here the main characteristics of the model. 551 
Numerical method 552 
The mass and momentum balance equations are solved using the Material Point Method 553 
(MPM)35 and finite strain elasto -plasticity. MPM is a hybrid Eulerian -Lagrangian method 554 
which is well suited to study problems involving large deformations, collisions and f ractures. 555 
It is thus suitable to study the impact of a snow slab on a human body. “ Particles” are used to 556 
track position, velocity and deformation gradients and a fixed background mesh is used to solve 557 
the balance equations. Here we used the Affine Particle In Cell (APIC) method for the particle 558 
to grid and grid to particle transfer 36 which allows an exact conservation of momentum and 559 
angular momentum. In addition, we use cubic B -splines as shape functions which guaranties 560 
the continuity of the gradients at the nodes.  More details about the MPM model and finite strain 561 
elastoplasticity can be found in Gaume et al.19. 562

Comment 
1) The authors have taken into account my previous comments and have managed to simplify and 
evaluate the analytical model, which makes the methodology clearer and sound. 
 
However, the answer to one of my comment (n° 1) is not fully convincing. I questioned the use of the 
term "delay" in the title and throughout the paper. In this paper, the authors refer to delay as the time 
between the cut in the snowpack and the avalanche release. This might be misleading since the 
triggering factor is snow accumulation and not the cut in the slope. If the mountaineers would have 
made no cut in the slope, a progressive loading by snow accumulation would still potentially trigger 
an avalanche during the night after they set up. Here, the snowpack changes in time so the release 
probability also evolves. If we caricature, one may as well define a delay between the beginning of the 
winter season and the avalanche release... I still understand that the Dyatlov incident lies in a narrow 
range of snow conditions (stable enough enough so that the avalanche does not release when cutting a 
pit, instable enough so that limited accumulation leads to snow failure) but this 
needs proper clarification in the text. 
 
For instance, in the abstract, the words "a physical mechanism for a delayed slab avalanche caused by 
progressive snow accumulation" are misleading as the delay is not defined yet and one may expect a 
new complex mechanism, which is, in the end, the well known loading by wind-drifted snow. I 
suggesting defining the delay directly in the abstract. Moreover, the paper is not all about "this delay" 
but also explaining the limited injuries, the relatively low slope in the release area, the role of the 
parapet in snow accumulation. So I would reword the title to "The mechanism of the Dyatlov Pass 
avalanche", which does not reduce the interest and quality of the paper. 
 
Except this comment, and to my opinion, the paper can be accepted as it is. 
 
Pascal Hagenmuller
