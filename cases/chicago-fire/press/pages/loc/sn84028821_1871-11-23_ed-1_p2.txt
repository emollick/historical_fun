a

Our nation has increased in wealth
and favor her products and manu
facturers secure her commerce with
the remotest parts of the world.
Her-people, happy under the benign
influences of equitable government,
enjoy sufficiency and often times op
ulencc;the common lot- of all
higher than other standards among
nations, and very few among our
number are reduced to beggary or
objects of poverty. But with the
increase or wealth and resources
has sprung up a spirit of prodigali
ty; a desire to excel our neighbors
whether our means warrant or not;
and multitudes disregarding their
interests and advantages, plunge
madly into the strife for rank and
position, to which they arc wholly
unequal; carelessly squandering the
inherited earnings of hours of toil
and labor.
The day when each one carved
his way to honor and fame by the
strength of his own right arm,
is not a myth of the past: it is one
of the most unyielding realities
the presentjCorroborated by the his
tory of the eminent of our own
times. Most frequently from the
lowest ranks, encountering every
difficulty that poverty could throw
in their way.they toiled nobly stead
ily, upward, with firm and self reli
ant hearts, until they attained
their present position. Labor jurat
omnia. Wealth cannot buy the
laurel on the poets brow, or secure
the wisdom of the cultivated mind.
It may place the means within our
reach, but without unremitting ex
ertions the prize still eludes our
grasp.
The frivolity of the age is mani
fested in the fickleness of the gen
eral mind. The desire for change
is constantly uppermost, and the
taste for the new and novel needs
frequently to be satisfied. It is not
confined to style and dress, but
found in every service and position.
Congregations tire of the old-fogy
doctrinal sermons of their aged pas
tors, prefering the diffuse.and wordy
modern rhetoric;while the general
tendancy, is, to be unmindful of the
customs of our forefathers, and
leave ttie good old fashioned ways,
for new and untried expedients.
But in hardly any manner is the
frivolity of the age more noticable
or injurious than in the thoughtless,
careless waste of time. By all
means improve your leisure if you
would improve your fortune. The
golden sands Of time run swiftly, si
lently; merrily as the rippling stream
they glide along; unnoticed by many;
by many unimproved or unthought
of, until the great vista of .Eternity
opens before them; then their true
importance is felt, when the soul
about to appear before its Maker,
account for the manner in which
each one of these unheeded moments
were spent and improved.
The truant boy, unlearned as
what is his true advantagc,and seek
ing the painted, butterfly of pleas
ure near shady streams, and moss
covered stones, is not the only spend
thrift of his time. Many are the
persons-, and many arc the ways
wasting our leisure moments, and
some who consider the time between
one scene of festivity and the next,a
dreary waste, a dull almost unbear
able blank in existence, scdelously
employ all arts and artifices for kill
ing time.
Reading is considered, at once an
interesting and profitable way
spending our leisure, and this wo
hold is eminently true,whcrc its sub
ject is one of merit and importance.
But literature has sadly degenerated
in this its day of plenitude, it has
exerted a great power for good, but
it may be perverted to exert a cor
respondingly great and mighty in
fluence for cviL The mass of liter
ature, though there may be many
bright and illustrious exceptions, is
most corrupt, its tendency down
ward, its influence evil. Many peo
ple lose a great deal of time bv read
ing, for they read frivolous and idle
works; such as absurd romances and
novels, where characters that never
existed arc insipidly displayed; the
oriental ravingof the Arabian Night!
and the flimsey yellow-backed
pamplets of fairy tales or blood and
thunder stones ot the border; and
all this sort of idle, hurtful matter.
Publications gratifying the vicious
tastes of new, and bunday issues,
eagerly perused for their animad
version of religious things are alarm
ingly increasing; while all classes
and ranks of newspapers and pen
odicals,arc filled with senseless stor
ies of love and romance. These
light and frivolous, nay.harmful and
injurious articles are sought for by
all the lighter part ot the popula
tion,excite a deplorably morbid state
of the mind.
But let it not be understood that we
pass censure without distinction, on
all works of fiction. Many are the
deserving works .of this class, which
includes Shakespere and Byron, as
well as the bold imageries of Milton
Dante and Bickersteth. Some
the most striking and beautiful de
scriptions were penned by Walter
bcott some of the most plaintive
passages and accurately drawn char
acters by the genius of Dickens;
and these will ever be models es
teemed by true lovers of the beauty
and purity of the English lan
guage
Hut the generally poor, and vaga
bond loafers, who spend the most of
their time on store boxcs,meditative-
ly whittling the corners, or frequent
ing snug lounging places, are the
most unmitigated, irreclaimable
abuses of their time and talents.
Were they employed at any avoca
tion, never so humble, or poorly re
warded, this class would soon want
a representation. But while the sun
rises and sets, and the stars contin
ues in their course, men will run to
seed at the elbows, and want the
necessary wherewithal to satisfy-!
their need. The theatre will still
hold its fascination for thousands
and the stale jokes and demoniac
laugh of the circus clown, for the
admiration of rustics. The maiden
will still spend hours at her toilet,
the loquacious will prate till oppor
tunity passes forever from their
power, and lotteries will still pluck
those who have drawn but doubtful
prizes in the lottery of life.
