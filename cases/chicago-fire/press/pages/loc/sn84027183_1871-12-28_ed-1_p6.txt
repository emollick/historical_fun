The Condition or Paris.
Gen. Geo. W. Clark writes to the
Register
from Paris that the only
Chicago relief fund raised in that
city was by Americans temporarily
sojourning there. General Clark
continues:
"There is no comparison between
the calamities which have befallen
the cities of Chicago and Paris. The
latter, today, in a material and com
mercial point of view, is about as
well ofF as it ever was. All the prop
erty destroyed here during the siege
and Communal insurrection has
been estimated at $100,000,000, and
that was nearly all Government
property. It consisted of palaces,
churches, theatres, monuments, etc.
This estimate also embraces the vil
lages in the environs of Paris, which
were destroyed, but which were no
essential part of the city. The beauty
of the city has been sadly marred,
but its wealth not materially di
minished. The individual losses
Were comparatively light, except in
the interruption of business and the
high price of provisions during the
seige. To»see the gayety of the pop
ulation now, as they fill the places of
amusement, and throng the boule
vards of an evening, one can hardly
realize that a few months ago this
was the scene of a bloody revolution,
while the city as surrounded by a
foreign foe.
The impression that Paris is in
ruins is a very erroneous one. It
contains a third more people and
twice the wealth of the whole State
of Iowa, but not half as many
Christians."
