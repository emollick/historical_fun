IN CAMP, MUSKOKA, Canada, Oct. 16. 
Twelve days our lives, our fortunes and 
our sacred camp equipments have heroical-
ly struggled log.-ther in the narrow cou-
fines of four birch bark caDoes to accomplish 
the laborious ascent of fifty or sixty miles 
of the Muskoka river. 
Tbay have been days of toil and drudg-
ery. 
Like ourselves, tha days have been 
toil-worn, toil-strung and toll-wasted,—self-
explaining and expressive are those com-
pound, qualifying adjectives, and precisely 
adapted to the circumstance? of our present 
situation. 
"With a tireless wing and a dauntless breast, 
Without a home or a hope of rest, 
Lite the Petrel wheeling over foam and spray 
Upward and onward we press our way. 
The clear sky has smiled upon us with a 
brilliant, cheerful October sun or frowned 
by turns with forbidding storms and angry 
clouds. 
The climate has alternated in com-
fortable weather arid in excessive cold. 
Til<» Muskoka is a noble brook ; it is a wild, 
a romantic, gently flowing, log-obstructed 
tortuously 
winding rivulet. 
Amid what 
northern regions it coils the weird solitude 
ia its serpentine embrace no manner of man 
knoweth. 
"River—t'.y bed is rock, the wilderness 
thy home." So broad and grand and mag-
nificent is its shaded pathway through the 
forest that when the challenge is sent across 
from towering pines upon opposite banks 
to join at mid stream th ir storm tossed 
crests in the solemn death crash that calls 
forth the echoes of its mighty solitudes ; 
the mirth and disdain of the wild and serene 
Muskoka for the variety and mockery of 
their effort are unbounded. 
A fallen tree upon its banks makes about 
as much show as the "fly upon the ele-
phant's back" that we have heard about, 
though it may have kissed the breeze when 
standing at 200 feet or more above the wa-
ters that reflected its symmetrical trunks. 
(Not the elephante—the tree, sir 1 the tree!
