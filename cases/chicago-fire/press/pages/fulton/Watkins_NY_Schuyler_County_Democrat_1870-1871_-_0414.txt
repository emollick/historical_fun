The singular suicide of two young 
girls at Lewiston Me., a few days ago, 
was telegraphed in a brief paragraph 
stating the mere fact. 
Additional 
particulars of the tragedy, furnished 
by the Lewiston Journal of the 4th 
inst., are reproduced below. 
About 12$ oc'clock P. u. Friday Mr. 
Cyfus Cobb, who lives on Court street 
just at the Auburn end of the bridge, 
looking from the rear window of his 
store and house, saw two females, 
apparently young ladies, sitting on 
the Auburn shore of the river, on the 
very verge of West Pitch. Mr. Cobb's 
daughter also noticed the girls, and 
thought it very strange that they 
should be sitting so near a very dan-
gerous place. 
The more noticahle 
became the fact when she saw them 
jump up and trip lightly and careless-
ly down the precipitous, rocky and 
slippery bank, where they removed 
their outer garments. After removing 
their outer garments, including their 
hats, the girls returned to the spot 
where they had been sitting, and re-
sumed their seats. In a few moments 
not far from one o'clock, the train 
from Bangor came thundering by, 
the track being but 200 or 300 feet 
from where they sat. Miss Cobb 
says that while the train was passing 
that point, she saw the girls rise, 
each throw her arms around the 
other's waist, and in this embrace, 
they, with apparently one consent, 
leaped from the shore into the Falls. 
Miss Cobb turned to her father: 
"Father, they have jumped together 
into the Falls." 
Late in the afternoon Miss Starbird, 
of Auburn, and others, identified the 
clothing left by the suicides on the 
West Pitch Stone, as belonging to 
Ada Brown, of Bucktield, and Anna 
Wood, of Hartford, 
young 
girls 
fourteen and sixteen years of age. Miss 
Starbird says that both girls were at 
home in Hampshire, St. 
Auburn, 
Thursday evening, and appeared in 
excellent spirits. 
Anna Wood left 
No. 4 Bates Block on Thursday, 
where she had been since Saturday. 
When she left she said she was going 
to work in a gentleman's family in 
this city. 
The landlady paid her 83 
cents, her due. Ada Brown worked 
in the Androscoggin Mill. 
While 
Miss. Wood was at No. 4, Ada Brown 
called frequently, and Miss Brown 
and herself went out together several 
evenings. 
Mrs, Brown came 
here 
five weeks since. Her father, John 
Brown is a farmer residing in Buck-
field. 
Her landlady says that Miss 
Wood appeared oheerful when she 
left her house, though she had com-
plained of sickness, caused by eating 
lobster. Ada Brown's oldest sister 
has been at work in this city, and is 
frantic with grief. 
She left in the 
afternoon to carry the sad news to 
her parents. 
We understand that both the Wood 
and Brown girls were at Ada Brown's 
room on the Bates coi-poration Thurs-
day night, reaching there at 10 p. M. 
Friday morning the older sister of 
Ada carried Ada's breakfast to her 
room,! where both the girls then were. 
She left Ada with the understanding 
that she would be in the mill at 8£ 
o'clock. 
Ada did not go in the mill 
as promised. As she did not make 
her appearance at dinner, the older 
sister became alarmed, and going out 
learned of the sad suicide of the two 
girls, who she at once surmised might 
be Ada and Anna Wood. 
The cause 
of the sad suicides is only conjectur-
ed as being the "old story," but will 
be more definitely known when there 
shall be an inquest over the bodies 
on their discovery.
