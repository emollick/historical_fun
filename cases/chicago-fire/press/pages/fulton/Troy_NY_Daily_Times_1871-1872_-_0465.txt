CBAKLBSTOWN, IncL, Nov. 28.— Availing my-
self of tbe opportunity to visit the scene of the 
murder, I accepted the kind invitation of Dr. 
Combs, this morning, and visited with him his 
patients, the Parke girls, who, strange as it may 
seem, are still alive and able to converse intel-
ligibly. 
Tbey are at the bouse where their 
father, mother and younger brother were killed, 
and as carefully cared for a J kind friends and 
near relatives possibly can. 
Tbey each occupy a 
long bed in opposite corners of the room, and 
can see and converse with each other if they 
wish. 
Neither seems to realize that they are la 
much danger. 
Wbeo we reached the house, 
Helen, tbe youngest, aged fourteen years, was 
sitting up in bed and eating, though hex injuries 
are by far the worst, and all physicians who have 
examined her head declare her recovery impossi-
ble, and look for her death at any moment. Her 
skull received several blows of the ax with both 
pole and blade, crushing it in like an egg shell. 
A portion of the skull over the left eye is gone, 
and the brain protrudes from the opening as 
large as a common-sized goose egg. They com-
plained very little to-day of pain in their heads. 
Eveline; tbe eldest, who is nearly eighteen, is 
entirely rational, and talks freely. I saw the 
bandages removed from her bead by tbe physi-
cian, revealing the sknll in as terrible a condition 
as that of her sister. There is scarcely a possibil-
ity that either can recover, though Eveline's 
chances are by far the best, as a skillful exami-
nation has revealed the fact that the thin mem-
bracce is*hot broken, and consequently no brain 
has escaped, though a glancing blow with the 
blade of the ax made an ugly displacement of 
tbe skull. In all probability, however, both will 
die within the next thirty days. On our arrival 
Helen's pulse was one hundred and twenty and 
regular, with s o unnatural warmth, but some 
little fever. 
Eveline's pulse one hundred and 
sixteen and quite regular. 
The only survivor of 
the family, beside the injured girls, is a son, who 
bsd married but s few days previously, snd wss 
still stopping at the house of his father-in-law. 
Had himself and wife been at home and oc-
cupied tbe apartments he usually did, they 
would most likely have shared the same common 
fate. 
In company with a neighbor 1 visited the den 
of Squire Taylor, but a trifle over a mile from 
tbe Parkes bouse. The property, a substantial 
log house of four rooms, was in the charge of a 
neighbor, (Mrs. Taylor,) who is beyond a doubt, 
in the minds of tbe entire community, as guilty 
as her husband, and disappeared. 
On entering 
the house, I found tbe floors of both upper and 
lower rooms strewn with goods a foot deep, of 
almost every conceivable description, some of 
them of a nature that warrants the conclusion 
that they were not only stolen, but that this 
house is a resort for an organized gang of petty 
thieve* and a general depot for stolen goods. 
Among the goods that I particularly examined 
was a» set of genuine solid silver table-spooos 
and forks, an elegant military overcoat and 
cape, entirely new, with buttons cut off, and 
costing not less than $50, an original package of 
toweling, entirely new, black crape shawl, a large 
figured white silk handkerchief that could not 
have cost less than $5 at wholesale; yards of un-
made goods, consisting of water-proof ladies' 
cloth ; lace, flannels, and such goods as second 
class clothing abounded in considerable quanti-
ties. 
Fine vases, fine annuals with the names 
erased and others substituted; also numerous 
fine articles of ladies' wear. 
There were also 
strewn on tbe floor letters from Cincinnati and 
Philadelphia wholesale business men to others 
in relation to business purely, dating back to 
1862- also private letters, evidently from white 
people, showing that old man Taylor has carried 
on this kind of business for years. 
Previous to 
the authorities taking possession of the property, 
these were all singly packed in boxes and kept 
in tbe second story, and hid from observation 
as much as possible. 
Taylor has a son-in-law 
and daughter In Chicago, who just before the 
Chicago fire dated a letter from uear 250 South 
Clark street, and who every now and then sent a

last sent here to Taylor was seized by the offi-
cer, and contained, besides children's under-
wear an elegant lap-robe, the estimated value 
of which was $100, and a very fine set (com-
plete) of chinaware.
