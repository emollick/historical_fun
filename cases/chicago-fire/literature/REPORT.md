## 0. Fixed points and open points

**Agreed by every source consulted (primary and secondary):**

1. The fire started in or at the barn behind Patrick and Catherine O'Leary's cottage, 137 DeKoven Street, West Division, on Sunday evening 8 October 1871. The Board of Police and Fire Commissioners: "The Board find that the fire originated in a two-story frame barn in the rear of No 137 DeKoven street the premises being owned by Patrick Leary." [chicagology/fire052.txt, transcribing the Chicago Evening Post of 12 Dec 1871]
2. The Board did not determine a cause: "There is no proof that anybody had been in the barn after nightfall that evening. Whether it originated from a spark blown from a chimney on that windy night or was set on fire by human agency we are unable to determine. Mr. Leary, the owner, and all his family prove to have been in bed and asleep at the time. There was a small party in the front part of house which was occupied by Mr. McLaughlin and wife, but we failed to find any evidence that anybody from part of the house went near the barn that night." [chicagology/fire052.txt; same wording in Bales p. 60, 143 and 295 (Google Books snippets, gbooks/BALES_SNIPPETS_BY_QUERY.txt) and Fedler 1985 p. 22 citing "Police And Fire," Chicago Tribune, Dec. 12, 1871, p. 4]
3. The cow-kicks-lamp story was in print on 9 October 1871, in the Chicago Evening Journal extra, i.e. within a day of ignition and long before any reporter "confessed" to inventing it (Section 1.2 and Section 7).
4. The O'Learys swore, on 15 October 1871 and again at the inquiry in November, that they were in bed (Section 1.4–1.5).
5. Weather and fuel, not the ignition source, explain why a barn fire became a city fire: drought since July, a strong south-west wind ahead of a Plains low-pressure system, and a wooden city (NWS Chicago 2021 page, Section 8.4).

**Genuinely open:** who or what ignited the barn. The candidates argued in the literature are: Mrs O'Leary with a lamp (the 1871 press, Musham 1940); Daniel "Peg Leg" Sullivan with pipe or lamp (Bales 1997/2002; also 1971 Chicago Association of Commerce, per Smith); Dennis Regan (raised as a possibility by Bales); a McLaughlin party guest fetching milk (1871 rumour, Andreas's "second theory", Ahern 1921); boys or young men smoking/gambling (Andreas's "third theory"; Cohn's 1944 bequest story); spontaneous combustion of fresh hay (Ahern 1915/1921 and "Big Jim" O'Leary); a chimney spark (the Board's own alternative wording); an incendiary/Communard (1871 press); a cometary fragment (Donnelly 1883, Waskin 1985, Wood 2004). No professional historian consulted (Musham excepted, and Musham's confidence is idiosyncratic) claims the cause is known; see Section 12.

## 1. The 1871–1885 documentary baseline (primary texts, quoted from the fetched OCR/transcriptions)

### 1.1 What the first Chicago paper said (Chicago Evening Journal extra, Monday 9 October 1871)

The original extra was not obtainable (no digitised copy found on loc.gov, archive.org or Google Books). Its origin sentence is quoted identically by three independent fetched texts:

* Musham 1940, p. 152: "The first story in the Chicago press, reported in the Evening Journal of October 9, was as follows: **The fire broke out on the corner of De Koven and Twelfth streets about 9 o'clock on Sunday evening, being caused by a cow kicking over a lamp in a stable in which a woman was milking a cow.**" [musham_1940_part3_commentary_cause.txt, OCR p. 152]
* Bales 2002, annotated bibliography p. 320: "'The Great Calamity of the Age!' Chicago Evening Journal — Extra, 9 October 1871, p. 1. While Chicago is still burning, the Journal writes that the fire was caused 'by a cow kicking over a lamp in a stable ...'" [gbooks/BALES_SNIPPETS_BY_QUERY.txt, query "Evening Journal", p. 320]; and p. 52: "... lamp in a stable in which a woman was milking." Although this newspaper contained no illustrations ..." [same file, query "lamp", p. 52]
* Carl Smith 2020: "The Chicago Evening Journal, the only local newspaper to publish on Monday, October 9, reported that the fire began with 'a cow kicking over a lamp in a stable in which a woman was milking.' Since then this story and the fire have been inseparable." [web/americanheritage_olearys_full.txt]
* Fedler 1985 (p. 26 of the ERIC PDF): "The Evening Journal was published in Chicago that Monday, and it immediately reported that, 'The fire broke out...at about 9 o'clock on Sunday evening, being caused by a cow kicking over a lamp in a stable in which a woman was milking.' Its story -- not a story in The Herald -- seems to have been the first to mention Mrs. O'Leary's cow." [web/fedler_1985_ERIC_ED257108.txt, PDF page 26; OCR imperfect]

Note that the Journal's sentence names no one; it is "a woman ... milking", not "Mrs O'Leary".

### 1.3 The Chicago Tribune's own first account (Wednesday 11 October 1871)

Chicago Tribune, 11 Oct 1871, p. 1 (its first post-fire issue): "A fire in a barn on the West Side was the insignificant cause of a conflagration which has swept out of existence hundreds of millions of property ..." and, under THE WEST SIDE: "At 9:30 a small cow-barn attached to a house on the corner of DeKoven and Jefferson streets, one block north of Twelfth street, emitted a bright light, followed by a blaze, and in a moment the building was hopelessly on fire. Before any aid could be extended the fire had communicated to a number of adjoining sheds, barns and dwellings, and was rapidly carried north and east, despite the efforts of the firemen." (OCR: "AIICSO a aDjall cow-barn attached to a hoiirv on Lhe corner of DtsKovvn and Jeflersou streets ... emitted a bright light, followed by a blaze") [ca/tribune_1871-10-11_p1.txt]. The Tribune's first issue therefore gives no cause at all — a point Bales makes (p. 78, quoting Ahern's 1915 admission that "No newspaper was printed the morning of Oct. 9, but when the papers were able to get out 'extras' the cow and lamp story was given as the cause of the holocaust" and adding "This is one more reason why Ahern's credibility is suspect") [gbooks, query "lamp", p. 78].

Chicago Tribune, 27 Oct 1871, p. 2, "How It Burned": surveying the "causes assigned", the paper mocks the "petroleum" limestone theory and "the wrath of God in general, leaving petroleum walls pine pavements, the Fire Commissioners, and the O'Leary cow all out of the account." [ca/KEY_tribune_1871-10-27_oleary_cow_out_of_account.txt]. Fedler 1985 cites the same article for the claim that "There have been not less than 900 causes assigned for the Chicago conflagration" [fedler, PDF p. 23, fn 45].

### 1.4 The Chicago Times of 18 October and the O'Learys' answer (Tribune 19 and 20 October)

The Chicago Times of 18 Oct 1871 was not obtainable (not on Chronicling America). Its text is quoted by Fedler from microfilm: Franc Wilkie's story reported "Flames were discovered in a small stable in the rear of the house on the corner of DeKoven and Jefferson streets" and described "an old Irish woman" who "was apparently about 70 years of age, and was bent almost double with the weight from many years of toil, and trouble, and privation", who when cut off from county relief "swore she would bring revenge on a city that would deny her a bit of wood or a pound of bacon", and — in the Times' "her side of the story" — "On Sunday night, about 9½ o'clock, she took a lamp in her hand and went out to have a look at her pet. Then she took a notion the cow must have some salt, and she sat down the lamp and went in the house for some. In a moment, the cow had accidentally kicked over the lamp, an explosion followed, and in an instant the structure was involved in flames." [web/fedler_1985_ERIC_ED257108.txt, PDF pp. 9–10, citing "The Fire," Chicago Times, Oct. 18, 1871, p. 1]. Fedler adds a footnote that the microfilm is "virtually unreadable" in places and that quotations elsewhere "are likely to contain slight variations" [PDF p. 10]. Carl Smith 2020 quotes the same Times passages ("she would be revenged on charity that would deny her a bit of wood or a pound of bacon"; "any one of the vermin which haunt our streets") [web/americanheritage_olearys_full.txt]. Musham 1940 quotes the "salt" version at length from the Lakeside Memorial (1872), p. 22, as one of his four O'Leary "confessions" [musham..., p. 151].

Chicago Tribune, 19 Oct 1871, p. 2 — the only contemporaneous interview with Mrs O'Leary found in a fetched original: "The Cow that Kicked Over the Lamp. A reporter of The Tribune called upon Mrs. Leary, of No. 137 De Koven street, in whose barn originated the fire, which destroyed the city of Chicago on last Sunday night and Monday morning. It was the intention to interview her [as to the] cause of the fire, and ascertain all about that cow which kicked over the lamp. She said that this story was false; she did not go into the barn with a kerosene lamp; she did not milk at night; and she did not know how the fire started. She was asleep when the alarm was given, and was awakened by a neighbor, who rapped on the door and told her that the stable was on fire. When asked how her house, which is not even scorched, was saved, she replied that there was a hydrant on each side of it, and a hundred of her friends filled buckets with water and threw it on the sides and roof of the building." (OCR regularised; raw in file) [ca/KEY_tribune_1871-10-19_cow_interview.txt]

Chicago Tribune, 20 Oct 1871, p. 2, "HOW IT ORIGINATED. Statements and Affidavits as to the Starting Point of the Great Fire" — Michael McDermott's letter and the affidavits sworn before him on 15 October [ca/KEY_tribune_1871-10-20_mcdermott_affidavits.txt (OCR) and chicagology/fire052.txt (clean transcription); Andreas 1885 II: 708 reprints them]. McDermott: "A great deal has been published respecting the origin of the great fire, which all reports have settled down on the head of a woman, or as the Times has it, an 'Irish hag' of 70 years of age. Admitting for a moment that an Irish hag of 70 years was by an unforeseen accident the cause, yet there was a great want of charity in the epithets used by the Times of the 18th. The following facts are stubborn things, and will cause the public to look for the cause in other sources, and perhaps attribute it to the love of plunder, Divine wrath, etc.: ... Mrs. O'Leary is neither haggard nor dirty." The O'Leary affidavit: "they had five cows, a horse and wagon, on all of which they had not one cent of insurance. She milked her cows at 4½ p. m. and 5 a. m., as Mrs. O'Leary peddled the milk. Mrs. O'Leary fed the horse beside the fence at about 7 o'clock p. m., and then put him in the barn. She had no lamp in the yard or barn that night or evening. Patrick O'Leary testifies that he was not in the barn during that day or night; left the feeding of the cows and horse [to] his wife and daughter: that both were in bed when awakened by Denis Ryan, of No. 118 DeKoven street". Sullivan's affidavit: "Denis Sullivan being duly sworn before me testifies that he was at Patrick O'Leary's house, No. 137 DeKoven street, on Sunday night the 8th, of October, 1871, from about 8½ to 9 o'clock at night, during which time Mr. O'Leary and wife were in bed, that he went a few lots east of O'Leary's on the opposite side of DeKoven street, until about half-past 9 o'clock, when he saw the fire. He went across the street and cried 'fire' 'fire,' and went into O'Leary's barn, where he found the hay in the loft on fire. He then attempted to cut loose the cows and horse, but failed to save anything but a half burned calf. He then came to O'Leary's and found them out of bed. Denis Ryan alarmed them during his time at the barn." [chicagology/fire052.txt]

Two things in these affidavits drive all later argument: (i) the O'Learys were "awakened by Denis Ryan [Regan]", while at the inquiry Mrs O'Leary said Sullivan woke them (Andreas's footnote: "The discrepancy in this statement is not commented on by the commissioners" [ia/andreas_v2_pp701-716_burning_of_chicago_origin.txt, line 1478 = II: 708–709]); (ii) Sullivan places himself "a few lots east of O'Leary's on the opposite side" — the position Bales tests against the tract books (Section 3).

### 1.5 The inquiry (23 November – early December 1871) and the Board's report (published 11–12 December)

The inquiry transcript (c. 1,100 handwritten pages, Chicago History Museum) was not obtainable here (chsmedia.org down; images.chicagohistory.org Cloudflare-gated). Its contents are known through Andreas 1885 (who summarised the "half-a-dozen" witnesses bearing on the origin), Musham 1940 (pp. 154–155), and Bales 2002 (who transcribed it; Appendix C annotates Mrs O'Leary's testimony, and Google Books snippets of pp. 230–233 show his annotated transcript).

Andreas, History of Chicago, II (1885), p. 708 (archive.org OCR): "The Origin of the Fire must ever remain a mystery ... While the city was still enveloped in flames, some now-forgotten tongue declared that Mrs. O'Leary was engaged in milking a cow, when the unruly animal kicked over a lighted lamp, and so ignited the straw and refuse on the barn floor This story was sent throughout the world, and men unhesitatingly accepted it as the correct explanation. ... But even the existence of the spot upon which the alleged lamp stood, the finding of a broken lamp in the ruins, and the evidence of reputable citizens that Mrs. O'Leary really illuminated her humble home with kerosene, are not sufficient to save the theory from discredit. There is no proof that Mrs. O'Leary is responsible for the fire. An official investigation of the disaster resulted in no definitive conclusion. Some fifty witnesses were interrogated, and, from among the number, half-a-dozen, perhaps, gave testimony which bore directly on the subject. Taking these in their proper sequence, it is learned That Dennis Sullivan discovered the fire at about 9:30 p. m.*; that the O'Leary family was then in bed; that Mrs. O'Leary was in the habit of milking her cows not later than five o'clock p. m.; that a family named McLaughlin lived in the O'Leary house, and was enjoying a social party in honor of the arrival of a relative from Ireland; that this family indulged in the use of beer, but had no whisky or milk punch; that none of the McLaughlins visited the barn that evening for the purpose of obtaining milk; and, in substance, that not one word of evidence was adduced before the commissioners to prove how or by whom the fire originated." [ia/andreas_v2_pp701-716_burning_of_chicago_origin.txt; page located between the OCR page markers 707 and 709]

Andreas, II: 701, "Cause of the Fire": "The precise cause of the fire is now a mystery, and must ever remain so, unless the knowledge at present withheld through fear or pride shall hereafter be revealed, as those who may know the cause manifest a decided aversion to the subject. ... Four theories are advanced relative to the origin of the great fire: First. That Mrs O'Leary visited her cows after dark, carrying a lighted kerosene lamp, which was kicked over by the historic cow, setting fire to the surrounding rubbish. This was the first theory advanced, and the one most tenaciously adhered to ... Second. That some of O'Leary's neighbors surreptitiously visited the barn, also with a lighted lamp, for the purpose of obtaining fresh milk to make punch or oyster stew, and met with a vigorous protest from the disturbed cow, which resulted in the conflagration. Third. That some boys were enjoying a moment of stolen pleasure in the barn, with pipes or cigars, and carelessly let fire fall among the inflammable substances on the floor. Fourth. That the fire was the deliberate work of an incendiary. These pages contain the results of a patient and impartial research among the records, and a painstaking examination of living witnesses, but the admission is made, that a satisfactory conclusion as to the origin of the fire has not been reached. Certain points of valuable evidence, however, are here given to the public for the first time. Among these are the following: The fire started as early as 8:45, instead of 9:30, as has been asserted by nearly all writers. A woman's scream was heard in or near the O'Leary barn just before the fire broke out, but no evidence is obtainable to prove by whom it was uttered. A broken kerosene lamp was found in the ruins of the O'Leary barn the day after the fire." [same file, lines 46–103 = II: 701]

Andreas on the Board's report (II: 711): "The foregoing report states that Sullivan saw the fire 'not later than twenty-five minutes past nine,' but this testimony, while tending to exonerate the watchman in the Court House, is contravened by later and trustworthy statements. ... The report appears to be little more than an attempt to quiet the too loud expressions of dissatisfaction with the Department." [same file, lines 1738–1752]

Andreas's 1884–85 witness statements (II: 714–715): James Dalton, whose cottage stood "four feet above the ground, on cedar posts" immediately east of the O'Learys, "At about fifteen minutes to nine o'clock, on the night of the fire, ... heard a woman's scream of terror. The sound came from the direction of the O'Leary premises"; Joseph Dushek, 153 DeKoven, "first discovered the fire at about 8:45" and "Just after the fire, while looking through the ruins of the O'Leary barn, I found an oil lamp, of the usual pattern ... The oil-holder, however, had been broken. The globe and chimney were gone."; Mrs McLaughlin "manifested so decided an aversion to being interviewed that little information was obtained In fact, she threatened to 'prosecute' those engaged in writing the history, if her name were used"; and (II: 715) "Dennis Sullivan had just returned to his own house, across the street from O'Leary's, from the vain attempt to save something from the barn. Everything was destroyed except a calf, which was placed in the lot next to Sullivan's." [same file, lines 2320–2420, 2499–2503]

Note the two competing time-lines already in 1885: the Board/Sullivan "20 to 25 minutes past 9" versus Andreas/Holden's "as early as 8:45". Musham adopts 8:30–8:45; Bales (p. 193) cites the Evening Journal's own "chronological table" placing the barn fire at 9:30 P.M. [gbooks, query "Evening Journal", p. 193]. The 45-minute gap matters for every reconstruction of who was where.

### 1.6 The Board's report as printed (Evening Post, 12 December 1871)

Full transcription in [chicagology/fire052.txt]: "The Board of Police as required by law have investigated the origin of the fire of October 8th and 9th. We have heard the sworn testimony of fifty-one witnesses and had the same taken down by a stenographer and subsequently transcribed. As written out it fills about 900 pages of foolscap paper. ... Where the Fire Started. The Board find that the fire originated in a two-story frame barn in the rear of No 137 DeKoven street ... The fire was first discovered by a drayman, by the name of Daniel Sullivan who saw it while sitting on the sidewalk on the south side of DeKoven street, and nearly opposite premises. He fixes the time at no more than 20 to 25 minutes past 9 when he first noticed the flames coming out of the barn. Who Started It. There is no proof that anybody had been in the barn after nightfall that evening. Whether it originated from a spark blown from a chimney on that windy night or was set on fire by human agency we are unable to determine. ..." The Evening Post's own preface is sarcastic: "Of course they have nothing new to disclose or even to suggest for this is an age when all valuable information of public affairs is first harvested and conveyed by that enterprising avant courier, the press". Fedler (PDF p. 22) adds the Board's other conclusions from the Tribune's printing: "The firemen and their officers were sober, and did all that men could do."

Why no finding? The primary texts themselves give the reasons later authors elaborate: the Board's remit and interest were the Department's conduct ("The Work Achieved"), the only witnesses who could know (the O'Learys, Sullivan, Regan, the McLaughlins) all swore they were elsewhere, and Andreas already read the report as "an attempt to quiet ... dissatisfaction with the Department." Bales's chapter 5 title asks "The Inquiry — Charade or Cover-Up?" (Section 3.8).

## 2. H. A. Musham, "The Great Chicago Fire, October 8–10, 1871" (1940/1941)

**Reference.** H. A. Musham, "The Great Chicago Fire, October 8–10, 1871," *Papers in Illinois History and Transactions for the Year 1940* (Springfield: Illinois State Historical Society, 1941), pp. 69–189 (the paper runs from OCR p. 69; Part III "Commentary" begins p. 147; "The Origin of the Fire" pp. 147–158; "The Alarm for the Fire" pp. 158 ff.). Full text obtained from archive.org (`ia/papersinillinois1940illi_djvu.txt`; cleaned extracts `musham_1940_ocr_clean.txt`, `musham_1940_start_of_fire_section.txt`, `musham_1940_part3_commentary_cause.txt`). Musham was the son of the foreman of "Little Giant No. 6", the first company at the fire ("9:16 p.m., Foreman Musham, Little Giant No. 6" [p. 158]), and relies on his own unpublished "Notes on the Chicago Fire" and "Notes on the Weather at the Time of the Chicago Fire" (fns. on pp. 96, 150, 153) — the maps in the paper are his.

**Claim about the cause.** Musham is the one modern author who affirms the cow story. His narrative (Part II, "The Start of the Fire", p. 96): "Sometime between 8:30 and 8:45 p.m. Mrs. O'Leary went out to her barn with a kerosene table lamp in her hand, possibly to milk a cow or to attend one that was ailing. She placed the lamp on the floor. The cow became frisky and kicked it over with her right hind foot, breaking the bowl. The oil ran over the floor and the burning wick set it on fire. In an instant the interior was a mass of flame and smoke. Mrs. O'Leary gave a loud scream which was heard by the neighbors but they paid no attention to it. Patrick O'Leary ran out of the house to see what had happened." [musham_1940_start_of_fire_section.txt, pp. 96–97]. His conclusion (p. 158): "when all the evidence in the case is fairly considered, there can be but one conclusion and that is: That the Great Chicago Fire of October 8-10, 1871 was started in the barn of Mr. and Mrs. Patrick O'Leary, 137 De Koven Street, by one of their cows kicking over a lighted kerosene lamp between 8:30 and 9:00 p.m. on Sunday, October 8; that it was brought about by an accident; and that Mrs. O'Leary was in the barn at the time." [musham_1940_part3_commentary_cause.txt, p. 158]

**Evidence cited and method.** He lists five candidate causes (pp. 147–148): "(1) incendiaries; (2) boys smoking in the O'Leary barn; (3) spontaneous combustion of the hay in the O'Leary barn; (4) neighbors visiting the O'Leary barn with a lighted kerosene lamp to get some milk for making milk punch or an oyster stew; (5) and Mrs. Catherine O'Leary's visiting her barn with a lighted kerosene lamp, possibly to milk a cow or to attend an ailing one." (citing Luzerne, *The Lost City*, 185–96, and Andreas II: 701). He eliminates the first four in turn:

* Incendiaries: "no information worthy of belief has been uncovered to this day proving that this was the case, but there were stories and rumors to the effect that the city was fired by a secret terrorist organization, an offshoot of the Paris Commune of 1871" (p. 148).
* Boys smoking: "It is not likely that boys would take the trouble to enter a barn after dark to smoke when they could do so just as well in the alley or behind one of the high fences, without fear of detection. In the absence of positive evidence to the contrary this theory can be rejected." (p. 148) — Bales p. 97 repeats this reasoning almost verbatim.
* Spontaneous combustion: "the hay was fresh, dry and small in amount, it had been in the barn for only a few hours before the fire started, and the day had been unusually dry and warm. The barn was loosely built ... There was enough circulation of air throughout the structure to make a dust explosion impossible. This suggested cause of the fire can therefore be rejected without further comment." (p. 148)
* Milk-punch neighbours: he quotes the Higginson-papers "Irish cook" story ("They wanted to make a little punch but were out of milk, and some one suggested that they go over and milk Mrs. O'Leary's cow") and rejects it: "Mrs. McLaughlin indignantly denied that she or any of her guests had been in the barn ... This suggested cause for the origin of the fire can also be rejected in the absence of positive evidence." (p. 149)

For the fifth cause his evidence is four alleged O'Leary "confessions" plus the Journal sentence and the lamp remains (pp. 150–153):
1. Robert S. Critchell, *Recollections of a Fire Insurance Man* (1909), p. 81 — fetched original (archive.org): "On the following evening, about eight o'clock Sunday night, the far-famed and historical lady, known as Mrs. O'Leary, went out into her barn to milk her cow to get some milk to make milk punch with; the cow kicked over the lamp, set the barn afire and the result was the great Chicago conflagration of 1871. This story of the origin of that fire is strictly true. I never saw the cow, but I did interview the old lady and was informed by her in rather an ungracious way that the story was true." [ia/critchell_recollections_1909_djvu.txt, p. 81]
2. Jacob J. Schaller (Musham's own notes): a nine-year-old milk boy to whom, "The next morning", Mrs O'Leary allegedly said "that she had company the night before and she wanted some milk to make Tom and Jerry for her friends, she took her lantern and went to the barn ... the cow becoming resentful at being milked a second time on the same night, raised up her right hind leg and let go, giving a light kick which hit the lantern and knocked it over" (pp. 150–151).
3. The Lakeside Memorial (1872), p. 22 — the "ailing cow" version.
4. The Chicago Times "salt" story (via Goodspeed, pp. 156–57) — the "front steps ... My poor cow" version (pp. 151–152).

Musham's own summary of their inconsistencies (p. 152): "Here are four statements said to have been made by Mrs. O'Leary, and on four separate occasions, and two others supporting them. The Critchell and Schaller statements confirm each other on the main points except in one particular. Critchell says that she took a lamp to the barn while Schaller says that it was a lantern. ... These stories disagree in several particulars. They all overlook the fact that she had five cows instead of one, and a calf and a horse. ... There can be but one thing to do relative to the main point and that is to accept it as being true."

Lamp remains (p. 153): "There was a lamp in the barn that night. The remains of one were seen by Phillip Sharkey in the ruins of the barn on the morning of October 9, and by Joseph Dushek of 153 De Koven Street, just after the fire. The chimney and globe were missing. Further, on Tuesday morning, a broken lower part of a small glass lamp was found under a broken plank of the floor by S. H. Kimball, which may have been part of the lamp found by Dushek. No remains of any other kind of a lamp or lantern were found in the ruins though they were well examined by the curious and souvenir hunting public. It is probable that the lamp seen was that taken by Mrs. O'Leary to the barn."

On the affidavits and inquiry testimony (pp. 153–156) he says: "These statements, and those sworn to before Notary McDermott, are all in the nature of an attempt to prove an alibi, and in them are discrepancies. They state that Mrs. O'Leary could not have been in the barn when the fire started because she was in bed. The statement sworn to before Notary McDermott states that Rogan waked the O'Learys up, but that made before the Board of Police declares that Sullivan did, and Rogan does not say that he waked them at all. If the O'Learys were in bed and asleep at 8:30 p.m. and for the next half hour, they were certainly very good sleepers to sleep through a party, with fiddling and dancing going on within a few feet of them, and through all the noise and excitement attending the discovery of the burning of their barn. The burning barn was seen by the neighborhood as early at 8:40 p.m. and had even then attracted the attention of a number of people." (pp. 155–156). Why she denied it (p. 156): "She was afraid of the crowd and was in great fear of being arrested as an incendiary. There had recently been much talk in the city about incendiaries and what would happen to them if they were caught."

**What Musham concedes.** "Whether it was lighted when it was taken to the barn or lighted afterward cannot be ascertained." (p. 150); "For whom was Mrs. O'Leary milking a cow after regular milking hours on that fateful evening? ... his or her name cannot be determined from the information at hand." (pp. 152–153); the four confessions "disagree in several particulars" (p. 152); the Schaller story rests on his own unpublished notes; Critchell's book was published 38 years after the event. He also concedes the department's first alarm failed and that the courthouse watchman struck the wrong box (pp. 158 ff.).

**How later authors treat Musham.** Kogan & Cromie 1971 rest their cow narrative entirely on him ("according to contemporary records cited in a monumental and detailed monograph about the Great Fire by Howard A. Musham in 1941" [web/uchicago_mag_2021.txt]). Bales p. 61 quotes his conclusion and answers it: "Musham supports this statement by claiming that on the morning of October 9 ..." [gbooks, query "kerosene", p. 61] (the rest of the sentence is cut by the snippet). Bales's index entry for the cow theory runs to pp. 52–68 and 75–80, i.e. his chapter 2 is a point-by-point rebuttal of Musham's four "confessions" (Critchell, Schaller, Lakeside, Times) as late, second-hand or fabricated (p. 91 on the 1903 Callahan tale: "apocryphal"; p. 80: neighbourhood children may have "concocted" the story).

## 4. Robert Cromie, *The Great Chicago Fire* (1958; reissued 1994) and Herman Kogan & Robert Cromie, *The Great Fire: Chicago, 1871* (1971)

**References.** Robert Cromie, *The Great Chicago Fire* (New York: McGraw-Hill, 1958; Rutledge Hill Press, 1994, ISBN 978-1-55853-264-9; Google Books ids YnR5AAAAMAAJ [1958], uSYdMwEACAAJ, ISGxoAEACAAJ — all "no pages" view). What follows is from (a) Herman Kogan's 1971 centennial essay reprinted by the University of Chicago Magazine in 2021, which is the argument of the Kogan–Cromie book in short, and (b) other authors' citations of Cromie.

**Kogan 1971 on the cause** [web/uchicago_mag_2021.txt]: "Whatever the specific cause, it began in the barn behind the home of Patrick and Catherine O'Leary on De Koven Street ... One such controversy still revolves around the question: Did Catherine O'Leary's cow really start the fire? Did she indeed kick over a lamp that set afire dry hay stored in the barn? In the hours just after the big blaze began, there seemed to be little doubt that this was precisely what happened. In fact, Mrs. O'Leary herself, according to contemporary records cited in a monumental and detailed monograph about the Great Fire by Howard A. Musham in 1941, admitted as much." He then retells Musham's Critchell and Schaller stories and the "salt" story, and: "As the hours of disaster continued ... Mrs. O'Leary did little talking—indeed, remained silent until official inquiries in November. And by that time she virtually denied everything she had said earlier. One can hardly blame her ..." Other theories: "The old Chicago Times ... ran a front-page story in the weeks after the fire alleging that agents of the Paris Commune had been dispatched to set the city ablaze. Others—especially one of the more notable O'Leary offspring, 'Big Jim' O'Leary, a multimillionaire gambling boss on the city's South Side by the time he died in the mid–1920s—insisted the cause was spontaneous combustion. Still others claimed that small boys had been smoking in the hayloft or that one or the other of the O'Leary neighbors had come sneaking into the barn looking for milk for punch or for whiskey and had dropped a lantern." His verdict: "In retrospect and from this historical perspective, it is really not important, intriguing as the mystery evidently continues to be, to determine the specific cause. For the tragic irony is that however the fire started, it could very easily have been stopped, early except for some errors, the most perplexing of which involved the sounding of the alarm." The 1971 essay also records: "The fire, according to the best authorities, started between 8:30 and 8:45 p.m." and the Quinn/"cigar butt" anecdote.

**Cromie 1958 as cited by others.** Fedler 1985 cites Cromie p. 9 for the O'Leary family/neighbourhood (fn 2) [web/fedler_1985_ERIC_ED257108.txt, PDF p. 27]. Bales cites "Cromie, p. 15" among sources on the fire department (p. 43 n.) [gbooks, "City Council", p. 43]. Wikipedia cites the 1994 edition for "In 1893 the reporter Michael Ahern retracted the 'cow-and-lantern' story, admitting it was fabricated, but even his confession was unable to put the legend to rest" [wiki/GCF_precise_start_section.wikitext] — i.e. Cromie is the source of the widely repeated "1893" date for Ahern's retraction (Bales's 1893 Ahern piece is "The Fire Fighters", Inter Ocean, 9 Oct 1893, which in the snippets concerns the watchman and alarms, not a confession; see Section 7). **Concession/claim:** on the fetched evidence Cromie's position is that the legend is a reporter's fabrication and the cause unknown; the 1971 Kogan–Cromie book leans (via Musham) to the cow story being the earliest and best-attested account while declining to decide. Both are popular narrative histories without the tract-book or transcript work.

### 5.7 Fred Fedler, "Mrs. O'Leary's Cow and Other Newspaper Tales about the Chicago Fire of 1871," paper, AEJMC 68th convention, Memphis, August 1985, 29 pp. (ERIC ED257108) [web/fedler_1985_ERIC_ED257108.pdf / .txt] — obtained in full

Directly on point (the press origin of the legend), and cited by Wikipedia. **Claims:** "Chicago's great fire of 1871 started in the O'Leary's barn, but Mrs. O'Leary was in bed at the time. Because of a sore foot, Mrs. O'Leary had gone to bed early that night. The tale about her cow seems to have been created by a reporter who wanted to make his story about the fire more interesting." (PDF p. 3). **Evidence:** microfilm of the Chicago, Milwaukee, New Orleans, Louisville, New York, Boston and Atlanta papers; the Board's report as printed in the Tribune of 12 Dec 1871. He shows the Evening Post extra (Monday) and its Tuesday issue "reported that the fire had started in a small barn. The Post commented, 'How those flames originated we do not know, though [rumor] has it that they were the result of incendiarism, and also that they sprang from carelessly used pipe or cigar.'" (PDF p. 8); that the Times of 18 Oct (Wilkie) was "obviously fraudulent" (p. 9); that a genuine-seeming Journal interview had Mrs O'Leary say "I always milked my cows by daylight, and never had a lamp of any kind or a candle about the barn. It must have been set afire. Two neighbors at the far end of the alley saw a strange man come up about half-past 9 in the evening." (p. 11 — the "mysterious stranger", Bales pp. 63–64); the New York World of 13 Oct had the "salt" story five days before the Times (p. 10); and, decisively for Section 7, that "Five sources, all apparently reliable, say a newspaper reporter created the story" but "Despite the claims, The Herald seems to have been one of the last newspapers to mention Mrs. O'Leary's cow, not the first. On Monday, The Herald reported, 'The fire started in a row of two-story wooden tenements on Deckoven Street.' ... The Herald did not mention a cow until Wednesday. Even then, it failed to mention Mrs. O'Leary." (pp. 24–25). His list of suspects (pp. 23–24) adds two Patrick O'Leary versions from F. E. Coyne's *In Reminiscence* (1941), "Tramps sleeping in the barn", "an adult had stepped into the open barn to light a pipe or cigar while sheltered from the strong wind", and "three men trying out a new terrier had been chasing rats in the O'Leary's barn, and one of them dropped a lighted match", and records that "Big Jim O'Leary admitted that the fire started in his family's barn. But O'Leary blamed tramps or neighborhood boys who were smoking. O'Leary said his mother blamed a neighbor's carelessness (probably the McLaughlin's)." **Concessions:** "Regardless of the story's source, it was a fanciful tale." and the alternative that "Several reporters may have heard a picturesque rumor, started by someone else, and all those reporters may have included it in their stories." (p. 26) — i.e. he cannot show who first said it.

### 6.2 The story as it now circulates (Wikipedia, JewAge)

The deleted stand-alone Wikipedia article read, in full (oldid 613162809):

> "'''Louis M. Cohn''' ([[Wrocław|Breslau]], [[Prussia]] March 10, 1853 - 1942) was a [[Chicago]] importer who claimed to have been in [[Catherine O'Leary|Mrs. O'Leary's]] barn the night the [[Great Chicago Fire]] started there in 1871. … According to Cohn, on the night of the fire, he, then 18, was gambling in the O'Learys' barn with one of their sons and some other neighborhood boys. When Mrs. O'Leary came out to the barn to chase the kids away around 9:00 on October 8, they knocked over a lantern in their flight, although Cohn states that he paused long enough to scoop up the money. … Following his death, Cohn bequeathed $35,000 which was assigned by his executors to the [[Medill School of Journalism]] at [[Northwestern University]]. Its only reference was the Hyde Park Media reprint of DeBartolo. JewAge (`sources/web/jewage_cohn.txt`) reproduces this text verbatim, citing Wikipedia. The present *Great Chicago Fire* article ("Precise start" section, `sources/wiki/GCF_precise_start_section.wikitext`) says:

### 6.3 What DeBartolo actually reported — 8 Oct 1997

Exact quotations from `sources/web/tribune_debartolo_1997-10-08.txt`:

- Framing: "All we know for certain is the approximate time and place: About 9 p.m. Sunday, Oct. 8, 1871, in or around a small shed that bordered the alley behind 137 DeKoven St. Everything else we've heard about the Chicago Fire's origin–the cow, the comet, and most recently, Daniel 'Peg Leg' Sullivan–is 'interesting, but ultimately unprovable theory,' says Carl Smith, a Northwestern University professor and expert on the Chicago Fire."
- On Bales and the Council: "Ald. Edward M. Burke, though, seems so convinced Peg Leg did it that last month he introduced a resolution in the City Council exonerating Catherine O'Leary and her bovine of all blame. Monday, the Council's Police and Fire Committee passed the resolution and sent it to the full Council for consideration. The resolution was based on research by amateur historian Dick Bales, a Wheaton attorney. … Bales theorized Sullivan, a neighbor of Mrs. O'Leary, lied about where he was standing when he initially saw the blaze. That's led some to conclude he was in the barn actually causing the trouble."
- The claim: "But before officially censuring Peg Leg, we should at least consider one other possible suspect: The only credible individual ever reputed to have admitted, in effect, 'I did it.' His name was Louis M. Cohn. Although fire experts like Bales and Smith have never heard the name– and, says Bales, 'I've read absolutely everything' — gambling historians have."
- Provenance: "Cohn's claim first surfaced Sept. 28, 1944 when his $35,000 estate, bequeathed in his name to Northwestern University's Medill School of Journalism, was ceremoniously handed over to Kenneth Olson, dean of the school. - The press-release paragraph (the only primary-ish text of the "confession"): "A less flattering boast is dispatched in the last paragraph, which begins: 'Mr. Cohn had an interesting connection with the origin of the Great Chicago Fire.' He asserted that he and Mrs. O'Leary's son, in the company of several other boys, were shooting dice in the hayloft . . . by the light of a lantern, when one of the boys accidently overturned the lantern, thus setting the barn afire. Mr. Cohn never denied that when the other boys fled, he stopped long enough to scoop up the money.'"
- Vital data: "According to his Cook County death certificate, Cohn would have been 18 years old at the time of the fire. He was born March 10, 1853 in Breslau, Prussia (part of modern-day Germany), also the birthplace of his unnamed father. He died in 1942 a few weeks before his 89th birthday, succumbing in Passavant Hospital after a lengthy bout with kidney cancer. He survived his wife, Bertha, by many years, had no children and was buried in Rosehill Cemetery."
- The will: "Cohn's recorded nine-page will, signed five months before his death, contains no reference to the fire. There is, however, reason to question the document's authenticity. It seems Cohn didn't properly sign or date what was represented as the will's last page. Instead, he signed all nine pages in their margins, but didn't affix his signature to the bottom of the final page to confirm that the preceding pages contained all he had to say." … "It is entirely possible that out of civic concerns, ethnic pride or a sense of benevolent protectiveness, Cohn's friends thought it best to keep his reputed admission, true or not, as private as possible."
- 1940s press: "In 1942, the day after Cohn's will was admitted to probate, the Tribune briefly acknowledged its scholarship provision. Two years later, the Sun-Times reported that the estate went to Medill, ending its two-paragraph story with this kicker: Cohn 'claimed to have been present in the barn of Mrs. O'Leary on the night of the Chicago Fire.' The Tribune, which essentially founded Medill 23 years earlier, made no mention of the gift or the fire."
- Wykes 1964: "Cohn's alleged involvement in the disaster was not publicly acknowledged again until gambling historian Alan Wykes' 1964 book, 'The Complete Illustrated Guide to Gambling.' In a chapter headed 'Seven Come Eleven,' Wykes reports Cohn's $35,000 gift to Northwestern, adding that the estate was handed over 'together with the full story of the `truth' about the Chicago Fire.' Wykes explains that Cohn's alleged admission is unverified, 'but, true or not,' he writes, 'it has taken its place in the colorful history of craps.' In his retelling of Cohn's claim, Wykes also significantly expands upon it. 'In his will,' the author writes, 'Cohn added a postscript to his story in the form of a deadpan comment that could have been made only by a man with the unswerving single-mindedness of the dedicated gambler: `When I knocked over the lantern, I was winning.'"
- Placing Cohn: "Chicago's 1870 census registered 32 Cohn households. The male heads of three of them reported Prussia as their birthplace. All lived within walking distance of the O'Learys. One, a cigarmaker who resided with another male and three females at 343 1/2 Park Ave., was even named Louis." Based upon church records, the lad would have been almost 9 years old."

### 6.7 Internal checks on the story

1. **Cohn's age.** Death certificate: born 10 March 1853, Breslau → 18 in October 1871 (DeBartolo 1997). Consistent with "then 18" in every retelling.
2. **"Mrs. O'Leary's son."** Fetched sources give the sons' births as Cornelius 1860 and James Patrick 1863, both baptised at Holy Family (`sources/web/glessner_olearys_2021.txt`: "Two sons, Cornelius (known as 'Puggy') and James Patrick, followed in 1860 and 1863 respectively"); DeBartolo: James "almost 9 years old" (1997) / "only about 9 in 1871" (1998); Irish America: "James, age 9 at the time"; Carl Smith (American Heritage excerpt): five children "ranging from a fifteen-year-old to an infant". So the craps partner of an 18-year-old would have been an 8–9-year-old or an 11-year-old. (Wikipedia's *James Patrick O'Leary* article gives his birth as 1869, which would make him two years old in 1871; that date conflicts with Glessner's parish-record account and DeBartolo's "church records".)
3. **Who knocked the lantern.** The 1944 press release blames "one of the boys"; Feinberg never heard Cohn admit it; only Wykes's 1964 "postscript" ('When I knocked over the lantern, I was winning') makes Cohn the culprit, and the recorded will contains no such text (DeBartolo 1997, 1998; Feinberg).
4. **Mrs. O'Leary "chasing them away around 9:00".** This contradicts her sworn inquiry testimony that she was in bed (Section 1.6, Section 3), and no 1871 witness placed her in the yard. It does, however, cohere with the timing of the outbreak that everyone knew by 1944.
5. **Boys in the barn — the 1871 record.** The Board asked about it. Bales p. 230 (inquiry transcript): "Q. Were boys in the habit of congregating in a barn in the vicinity, do you know? A. No sir. I did not hear it." Mrs. McLaughlin was pressed to identify "three boys" who left her house (Bales p. 233). Fedler's 1985 survey of contemporary press lists, among 1871 theories, that "a group of young men who had been playing cards tried to get some milk" and that "young boys enjoying a pipe or cigar started the fire" — i.e., a gambling/boys story existed in 1871 independently of Cohn, and Bales (p. 109) treats such tales as "just one of several stories concocted by neighborhood children in the first few hours after the …".
6. **Big Jim's own statements.** In the fetched texts James "Big Jim" O'Leary never described a craps game he attended; Fedler: "Big Jim O'Leary admitted that the fire started in his family's barn. But O'Leary blamed tramps or neighborhood boys who were Smoking"; the 1971 landmark report: "Years after the fire, Jim O'Leary, son of Catherine, stated that it was positively caused by spontaneous combustion."

### 7.2 What Ahern said — in the texts obtained

**1915 (Tribune, 21 Jan 1915, transcription):**
> "At the time of the fire I was police reporter for the Morning Republican, which later became the Inter-Ocean. Johnny English was THE TRIBUNE's police reporter and Jim Haynie was on the Times. Both of them are dead. I am the only police reporter living who helped to cover the greatest fire in the world's history. It was Jim Haynie, I think, who 'faked' the story about the cow kicking over the lamp. No newspaper was printed the morning of Oct. 10, but when the papers were able to get out extras the cow and lamp story was given as the cause of the holocaust."
> "In after years I met O'Leary and we talked about the fire. He told me that in his opinion it was caused by spontaneous combustion of hay. A couple of weeks before the fire O'Leary had put a ton of green hay in the barn loft. It was an unusually warm, dry spell of weather. Everything was parched. The hay, while curing, took fire from the terrific heat that beat upon the roof, and that was what laid Chicago in ashes."

### 7.4 Fedler's analysis (1985)

From `sources/web/fedler_1985_ERIC_ED257108.txt` (OCR; PDF pp. 24–26):

> "A Reporter's Hoax? Five sources, all apparently reliable, say a newspaper reporter created the story about Mrs. O'Leary's cow."

The five (Fedler's notes 48–52): John McPhaul, *Deadlines & Monkeyshines* (1962), p. 63 — "The legend of Mrs. O'Leary had its start the night of the fire … a correspondent for The (New York) Herald wrote a story that quoted Mrs. O'Leary … 'she had gone to the shed to give salt to an ailing cow, and the animal had kicked over a kerosene lamp'"; Wendt, *Chicago Tribune: The Rise of a Great American Newspaper*, p. 236 — "The correspondent of The Herald said the fire began when Mrs. O'Leary went out to milk her cow, which kicked over a Lantern"; Collier's Encyclopedia vol. 6 (1979), pp. 213–214 — "according to a reporter's fabrication that gained universal acceptance"; Tom Burnam, *The Dictionary of Misinformation* (1975), p. 161 — "The fourth source identifies the reporter: Michael Ahern. Moreover, it adds that Ahern 'admitted that he made up the whole story to add color to his account of the fire'"; Silas Bent, *Ballyhoo* (1927), pp. 98–99 — "The last survivor of the group which reported the Chicago fire of 1871 admitted, shortly before his death, that Mrs. O'Leary's cow had not kicked over the lantern. The fire had started from spontaneous combustion in a hayloft, but a fretful Bossie seemed to the reporters a more picturesque origin of the disaster." Fedler: "The fifth account suggests that several reporters, not Ahern alone, helped create the story."

Fedler's test against the New York Herald file (notes 53–56):
> "Despite the claims, The Herald seems to have been one of the last newspapers to mention Mrs. O'Leary's cow, not the first. On Monday, The Herald reported, 'The fire started in a row of two-story wooden tenements on Deckoven Street.' Its story was skimpy, probably because the fire destroyed the telegraph lines leading into Chicago. Because the lines were destroyed, it would have been difficult, perhaps impossible, for Ahern to transmit his story to The Herald that night. The Herald did not mention a cow until Wednesday. Even then, it failed to mention Mrs. O'Leary. Instead, The Herald reported that the fire started when a boy went to a stable to milk his cow. … That story, like most of the others published by The Herald, seems to have been provided by The Associated Press, not by its own correspondent in Chicago. The Evening Journal was published in Chicago that Monday, and it immediately reported that, 'The fire broke out...at about 9 o'clock on Sunday evening, being caused by a cow kicking over a lamp in a stable in which a woman was milking.' Its story -- not a story in The Herald -- seems to have been the first to mention Mrs. O'Leary's cow. Newspapers in other cities began to publish similar stories that Tuesday. Some admitted copying the Evening Journal."

Fedler's own hedges: "Several reporters may have heard a picturesque rumor, started by someone else, and all those reporters may have included it in their stories. Or, Michael Ahern may have worked for the Evening Journal, or for some other Chicago daily, and supplemented his income by serving as a correspondent for a New York daily. … Regardless of the story's source, it was a fanciful tale."

### 7.5 The print record versus the confession (cross-reference Section 1)

The Library of Congress pages fetched for this dossier independently confirm Fedler's chronology: the Washington *Evening Star* of 10 Oct 1871 already printed "THE ORIGIN OF THE FIRE was in a stable, where a woman took a kerosene lamp at milking time" (`sources/ca/KEY_eveningstar_1871-10-10_origin_woman_kerosene.txt`), and the AP "boy … milk his cow … kerosene lamp" version ran nationally on 11 Oct (New-York Tribune, Wheeling, NY Herald — Section 1.2). Bales's bibliography (p. 320) dates the *Journal* extra to 9 Oct 1871: "'The Great Calamity of the Age!' Chicago Evening Journal — Extra, 9 October 1871, p. 1. While Chicago is still burning, the Journal writes that the fire was caused 'by a cow kicking over a lamp in a stable …'". Ahern's own paper, the *Republican*, is not credited with the story by anyone in the fetched material; no *Republican* or *Times* issue of 9–11 Oct 1871 was obtainable.

**Consequences.** (1) Whatever Ahern, English or Haynie did, the cow-and-lamp explanation was in print in Chicago on 9 Oct and nationally by 10–11 Oct, before any reporter could have "added color" over subsequent days. (2) Ahern's 1915 text itself concedes this ("when the papers were able to get out extras the cow and lamp story was given as the cause"). (3) The "confession" is at most a claim to have *joined* a rumour; Bales (p. 80) prefers neighbourhood children as the rumour's source and (p. 75) an anonymous *Journal* reporter as its first printer. (4) The dating of the "confession" in the secondary literature is unstable (1893 / 1911 / 1915 / 1921), and the 1911 article's authorship was disputed at the time by a colleague who claimed to have ghost-written it (Smith, gcf).

## 8. The comet theory (Donnelly 1883 → Waskin 1985 → Wood 2004) and the Peshtigo/Michigan fires

But there's a hitch with Biela's comet: It appeared in 1872, a year after the Chicago fire. A researcher directed me to another shooting star, Comet Encke … But other experts also cast doubt on the whole idea of such fire starter comets. Meteors, they note, also are called 'dirty ice' and tend to cool too much in their fall through earth's atmosphere to set much ablaze."

### 8.3 Robert Wood, "Did Biela's Comet Cause the Chicago and Midwest Fires?" (AIAA 2004-1419, Planetary Defense Conference, February 2004)

Not obtained (arc.aiaa.org abstract page: HTTP 403; the pdf.aiaa.org preview link in Wikipedia is dead). Wikipedia's *Great Chicago Fire* article ("Precise start") summarises: "At a 2004 conference of the Aerospace Corporation and the American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics, engineer and physicist Robert Wood suggested that the fire began when a fragment of Biela's Comet impacted the Midwest. Biela's Comet had broken apart in 1845 and had not been observed since. Wood argued that four large fires took place, all on the same day, all on the shores of Lake Michigan …, suggesting a common root cause. Eyewitnesses reported sighting spontaneous ignitions, lack of smoke, 'balls of fire' falling from the sky, and blue flames. According to Wood, these accounts suggest that the fires were caused by the methane that is commonly found in comets." The *Biela's Comet* article calls it "an unpublished 2004 scientific paper" and the whole idea "A fringe theory".

**Rebuttals as cited by Wikipedia (the cited works themselves not fetched):**
- "Meteorites are not known to start or spread fires and are cool to the touch after reaching the ground, so this theory has not found favor in the scientific community." — Mica Calfee, "Was It A Cow Or A Meteorite?", *Meteorite Magazine* 9:1 (Feb 2003); NASA, "Meteorites Don't Pop Corn" (27 July 2001).
- "Methane-air mixtures become flammable only when the methane concentration exceeds 5%, at which point the mixtures also become explosive, a situation unlikely to occur from meteorites. … Methane gas is lighter than air and thus does not accumulate near the ground; any localized pockets of methane in the open air rapidly dissipate."
- "if a fragment of an icy comet were to strike the Earth, the most likely outcome, due to the low tensile strength of such bodies, would be for it to disintegrate in the upper atmosphere, leading to a meteor air burst like the Tunguska event." — M. Beech, "The Problem of Ice Meteorites," *Meteorite Quarterly* 12:4 (Nov 2006), pp. 17–19.
- "The specific choice of Biela's Comet does not match with the dates in question, as the 6-year period of the comet's orbit did not intersect that of the Earth until 1872, one full year after the fire, when a large meteor shower was observed." *Biela's Comet* article: "on 27 November 1872, a brilliant meteor shower (3,000 per hour) was observed radiating from the part of the sky where the comet had been predicted to cross in September 1872."
- Earth Magazine (`sources/web/earthmagazine_peshtigo.txt`): "While massive impactors such as the Chicxulub bolide … are thought to have ignited widespread fires …, small rocky meteorites are generally poor conductors of heat. NASA debunked that particular ignition method in 2001, noting that there has never been a historically documented case of a small rocky meteorite igniting a fire."

### 8.4 Peshtigo and the Michigan fires — the accepted meteorological explanation

- NWS Green Bay, "The Great Midwest Wildfires of 1871" (Tom Hultquist, NWS Twin Cities; `sources/web/nws_grb_peshtigofire2.txt`): "The actual cause of the fire was never determined, but weather conditions across the entire region during the summer and fall of 1871 produced conditions conducive to large, rapidly-spreading fires should one ignite." … "The fire in Peshtigo resulted from a number of factors, including prolonged drought, logging and clearing of land for agriculture, local industry, ignorance and indifference of the population, and ultimately a strong autumn storm system occurring in the presence of conditions supportive of a large, rapidly-spreading fire." … "A large upper-level ridge was present across the region from July through September …" … Fort Embarrass observers: "Dense smoke was observed for several days leading up to the fire, and temperatures in the 70s were reported on October 8 … a strong low pressure system over the central plains …, which would have produced strong southwesterly winds across the region. These winds, in combination with the warm temperatures and dry conditions, likely led to the rapid spread of pre-existing fires and any new fires that ignited."
- NWS Chicago, "The Great Chicago Fire of 1871" (150th-anniversary page, 8 Oct 2021; `sources/web/nws_lot_1871fire.txt`): "In the 3-month period leading up to the fire (July 7 - October 8), only 3.55 inches of precipitation were recorded. This was almost 8 inches below the average … the 2nd driest stretch from July 7th through October 8th on record"; "The month of September on its own was exceptionally dry … only 0.74 inches"; "From September 29th through October 8th, Chicago saw no precipitation at all"; "October 8th was by far the most favorable for the spread of fires. Temperatures were very warm for early October with high temperatures in the low to mid 80s across most of northern Illinois … relative humidity ranged from around 35% to 40%"; "A strong low pressure system moving across the Great Plains was responsible for producing strong winds across the Midwest on October 8th with weather observers in the region estimating that southwest winds peaked at around 35 to 45 mph, including within the city of Chicago"; 9 Oct: "a cold front … would reach the Chicago area sometime during the late afternoon or evening and bring relief in the form of rain and cooler temperatures … (the weather observer in Evanston, IL had reported a total of 0.10" of rainfall the following morning)"; "The rain eventually ceased sometime on the morning of October 10th."
- Earth Magazine (Peshtigo): "During the late 1800s, logging and farming practices created an abundance of slash … From 1870 to 1871, the Midwest was engulfed in drought … Historical records mark the date of the last soaking rain before the fire as July 8 … In early October, a cyclonic weather front formed over the Great Plains, creating westerly winds that headed toward Peshtigo."
- Illinois Fire Service Institute guide (`sources/web/uiuc_peshtigo_guide.txt`): "A combination of drought, logging practices, and a strong southwesterly wind created a firestorm that devastated the town of Peshtigo and burned around 1.5 million acres of land in early October, 1871."
- Wikipedia *Peshtigo fire*: infobox cause "Small embers from slash and burn agriculture were caught up in drafts from unusually high winds during a period of extremely dry drought-like conditions."; "These fires, and many other fires of the 19th century, had the same basic causes: small fires coupled with unusually dry weather."; "In any event, no external source of ignition was needed. There were already many small fires burning in the area as part of land-clearing operations and similar activities after a tinder-dry summer. … All that was necessary to trigger the firestorm, plus the other large fires in the Midwest, was a strong wind from the weather front which had moved in that evening." (Gess & Lutz, *Firestorm at Peshtigo*, 2002/2003; MPR 2002.) *Great Fires of 1871*: infobox cause "Controlled burns, railroad sparks, etc."; the Michigan fires "were said to be caused by land clearing fires that grew out of control due to the 'slash'".
- Bales p. 111 is cited by Wikipedia for the same conclusion ("A common cause for the fires in the Midwest in late 1871 is that the area had had a dry summer …").

### 9.2 Spontaneous combustion of green hay

## 12. Where current scholarship stands (2020–2026)

1. **No professional historian since 2020 endorses a specific ignition mechanism.** Carl Smith (Northwestern; *Chicago's Great Fire*, 2020): the cause "may never be known" (AP/ABC7/NBC, Oct 2021); the theories are "interesting, but ultimately unprovable" (1997) and the Cohn story "unprovable and refutable" (2006); his 2011 online essay: "It is highly unlikely that we will ever have definitive knowledge of the cause of the fire" and the O'Leary legend's truth is "unresolvable". Julius L. Jones (Chicago History Museum, lead curator of *City on Fire: Chicago 1871*, 2021): "The cow-kicking-a-lantern myth has been dispelled; no one really knows how it started." John Russick (CHM): "We don't know it was an accident." Libby Mahoney (CHM, 2006): "We will never know." Tim Samuelson (2025): "Nobody is sure what started the fire." The National Weather Service (Chicago, 2021 page; Green Bay): "The actual cause of the fire was never determined." Smith's 2020 book itself was not obtained; his treatment of the cause is attested here only through the *American Heritage* excerpt, the 2011 essay, the 2021 press interviews and the C-SPAN conversation (which, in the saved captions, discusses the O'Leary "myth" and sourcing problems rather than a cause).

2. **What is settled.** (a) The fire began in or immediately beside the O'Leary barn, 137 DeKoven St., between roughly 8:30 and 9:30 p.m. (every source; the Board's report; Bales's timeline discussion pp. 179–181, 192–193). (b) Mrs. O'Leary was not milking; the O'Learys were in bed; the cow story is a press invention of 9–11 Oct 1871 and a case of nativist scapegoating (Smith, Jones, Russick, the 1997 Council resolution, Fedler, Bales, Sawislak). (c) The scale of the disaster is explained by the July–October drought (3.55 in. of rain 7 Jul–8 Oct; September 0.74 in.), SW winds of 35–45 mph on 8 Oct, wooden construction, and alarm/response failures (NWS 2021; Musham; Bales's chapters on Goll and Schaefer; Steinberg 2021). (d) The comet theory is rejected by astronomers and fire scientists.

3. **Bales's Sullivan thesis: influential, not consensual.** It carried the City Council (28 Oct 1997 resolution), the popular press (Mental Floss, Time, Irish America, Newsweek/AP), and many museum-adjacent tellings; Bales stated in 2021 "I am 100% convinced Daniel Sullivan started the fire." Against it stand the mock-trial verdict at John Marshall Law School (1997/98), the Chicago Public Library's critique (as recorded by Wikipedia), Smith's "unprovable", DeBartolo's rival suspect, and Bales's own written limits (Section 3.10: "No one knows what really happened in the barn on October 8, 1871"). No peer-reviewed re-examination of the tract-book/sight-line argument after 1997 was found; the Harry E. Pratt Memorial Award for the 1997 article is attested only by the publisher's author blurb (AbeBooks listing of the McFarland edition: "Bales' initial conclusions concerning the cause of the Great Chicago Fire earned him the Illinois State Historical Society's Harry E. Pratt Memorial Award") and by search-engine summaries, not by an ISHS document.

4. **The Cohn theory** remains a journalistic proposal (DeBartolo 1997, 1998; Potash 2006) taken seriously by *Time* (2015) and dismissed by Smith and Bales; the 2021 *Forward* treatment could not be read.

5. **The Ahern "confession"** is still repeated in 2021–2025 popular coverage (Emery; Glessner House; Saturday Evening Post; Block Club) with inconsistent dates (1893/1911/1915/1921), but the specialist literature (Fedler 1985; Bales 2002) regards it as unreliable and, given the 9–11 Oct 1871 print record, at most a claim to have amplified an existing rumour.

6. **Sesquicentennial framing (2021).** Coverage and the CHM exhibition shifted emphasis from "whodunit" to scapegoating, inequality, preparedness and weather; Russick's remark that blaming the cow "is a way to say it was an accident" captures the current interpretive stance. Wikipedia's *Great Chicago Fire* article (Sept 2026 text) presents the cow legend, Bales, DeBartolo and the comet in sequence, with the comet rebutted at length; the stand-alone Wikipedia biographies of Sullivan and Cohn were both merged away in June 2014 ("WP:ONEEVENT").
