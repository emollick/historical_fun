# "Ourang Medan": the US/UK press record and the English-language chain, 1940–1965
Of the files cited below, `proceedings_mmc_1952-05_we_sail_together.txt`, `Vol9_No5_May1952_text.txt` and the CIA letter texts are in `sources/transcriptions/`, and `chronology.md` is `sources/us-uk-press_chronology.md`; working copies of the other transcriptions, OCR texts, PDFs and images are not included in this folder.

## 1. Headline findings

1. **The earliest English-language printing is the Yorkshire Evening Post (Leeds), Thursday 21 November 1940, p. 5**, "MYSTERY S O S FROM DEATH SHIP / Unwounded Bodies Found on Deck / EXPLOSIONS DEFEATED BOARDING PARTY", an **Associated Press** despatch datelined **"TRIESTE, Thursday"**, sourced to an unnamed **"merchant marine officer who went to the rescue of the steamer Ourang Medan off the Solomon Islands last November"** (i.e. November 1939). The Daily Mirror (London) ran a shortened AP version the next day, Friday 22 November 1940, p. 11, "CREW DIES IN S O S MYSTERY" ("said an officer of the rescue party yesterday ... A ship of the British Merchant Marine was first to arrive on the scene. — Associated Press"). Paijmans adds the Hampshire Telegraph, 29 Nov 1940, "Story of Weird Sea Tragedy". The Mirror text was reprinted verbatim in The China Mail (Hong Kong), 27 Feb 1941, p. 9. **Silvio Scherli is not named in any 1940 British text**; the only link to him is the Trieste dateline. Full transcriptions: yorkshire_evening_post_1940-11-21_transcription.txt, daily_mirror_1940-11-22_transcription.txt (clipping images: skittish_image-28/29/30/32/33/34/35.jpeg, daily_mirror_1940-11-22_clipping.jpg; China Mail OCR: china_mail_1941-02-27_extract.txt).

2. **The first American printing is Hearst's Sunday supplement The American Weekly, 10 October 1948, pp. 24-25, "Secrets of the Sea" by Win Brooks** (illustrated by John Floherty Jr.), trailed on 3 Oct 1948 p. 31. I have the complete text (Chicago Herald-American copy on archive.org; Albany Times-Union copy on fultonhistory; Atlanta Constitution teaser). Brooks credits **"a Holland newspaper, Elseviers Weekly, last January"** and says the story "appeared again in the London press" a month later; his source is "an Italian officer on a vessel which answered a distress call", unnamed; the rescue ship is unnamed. **This is where the Strait of Malacca first enters the legend — as the ship's departure route from Medan in "late May 1947"** — while the ship is still lost in June 1947 in the South Pacific "400 miles southeast of the Marshall Islands". Files: american_weekly_1948-10-10_secrets_of_the_sea_transcription.txt (clean), american_weekly_1948-10-10_secrets_of_the_sea_ocr.txt, american_weekly_1948-10-03_teaser_ocr.txt, albany_times_union_1948-10-10_3308/3309.pdf (+_text.txt), atlanta_constitution_1948-10-03.pdf.

3. **The Coast Guard text is verified from the USCG's own PDF**: Proceedings of the Merchant Marine Council, vol. 9 no. 5, May 1952 (CG 129), p. 107, in an **unsigned editorial feature "We Sail Together"** (pp. 107-109) that strings together five "mystery ship" anecdotes before turning to the Titanic and the SOLAS conventions. **It credits no source.** It is the **first text anywhere to place the disaster in "February 1948" in "the Straits of Malacca"**; it does **not** name the rescuing vessel (no Silver Star, no City of Baltimore); and it coins the much-quoted "Sprawled on their backs, the frozen faces upturned to the sun with mouths gaping open and eyes staring, the dead bodies resembled horrible caricatures. Even the ship's dog was found dead." Textual parallels ("we float"/"We are floating"; "approximately 50 miles from her indicated position"/"50 miles from the position given"; "No. 4 hold"/"No. 4 Hatch"; dog; "I die"/"I am dying") show it derives from Brooks's American Weekly piece; the "February 1948" is best explained as a misreading of Brooks's "printed ... last January. A month later it appeared again in the London press". Eleven other issues (Mar, Apr, Jul–Dec 1952; Jan–Mar 1953) contain no follow-up, letter or correction. File: `sources/transcriptions/proceedings_mmc_1952-05_we_sail_together.txt`; PDF Vol9_No5_May1952.pdf; other issues in pmmc_issues/.

4. **The chain from the Coast Guard into forteana is now documented link by link**: Proceedings (May 1952) → Robert V. Hulse, "Death at Sea", Fate vol. 6 no. 6 (June 1953), pp. 73-74, which cites the Proceedings → M. K. Jessup, The Case for the UFO (1955), pp. 88-89, which quotes Fate (mis-dated "June 1954") → Frank Edwards, Strangest of All (1956), pp. 70-72 (and Edwards's Winnipeg Free Press column, 27 June 1964) → C. H. Marck Jr.'s letter to the CIA, 5 Dec 1959 (copies the Fate/Jessup wording) → Vincent Gaddis, Invisible Horizons (1965), pp. 125-126 (Ace) → Ivan Sanderson, Uninvited Guests (1967) and Invisible Residents (1970), which quotes Gaddis in full → Sparks Journal (Society of Wireless Pioneers), Nov 1978 → Roy Bainton, "A Cargo of Death", Fortean Times 125 (Aug/Sep 1999). Every one of these English texts up to 1978 says February 1948, Straits of Malacca, unnamed rescuers "from Dutch Sumatra and British Malaya". **None of them names the Silver Star.**

5. **The "Silver Star" (and "City of Baltimore") entered the story in German, in Otto Mielke's 32-page pulp "Das Totenschiff in der Südsee. Dampfer 'Ourang Medan'" (Anker-Hefte: Seefahrt in aller Welt, Nr. 1, Arthur Moewig Verlag, Munich, 1954; reprinted 1979)**, and reached English only through Bainton (1999), who got it from Theodor Siersdorfer of Essen. Wikipedia's statement that "by 1948 ... the City of Baltimore and the Silver Star picked up several distress messages" is unsupported by any 1948 text. **Lloyd's Register settles the ship's identity**: LR 1947 (Steamers) and LR 1953: "Santa Juana (ex Silver Star-47, ex Santa Cecilia-46) ... 6507 [GRT] 1942 Federal [SB, Kearny NJ] ... Grace Line Inc." — i.e. a real Grace Line C2 freighter carried the name Silver Star in 1946-47, on the Grace Line's US–South America trades; nothing connects her to the Pacific or a rescue. (A second vessel, the Canadian Park tanker Silver Star Park (1943) → Santa Cecilia (II) (1945) → Ilha Grande (1951), explains the "Silver Star Park ... Brazil" muddle in blogs.)

6. Supporting sources located and saved: Bainton 1999 (Wayback copy of the NESWA reprint) and the near-identical Journal of Borderland Research v. 55 q2 (1999); **Theo Paijmans, "Death Ship: The Final Voyage of the Ourang Medan", Fortean Times 402 (Feb 2021), pp. 32-37, with 31 footnotes** listing every early printing he traced (Italian, Austrian, French, Dutch, British, American, German, Russian) — the single most useful secondary source; FT letters column June 2021; the CIA letter in both releases (2003 and 2021); Revista da Semana (Rio) 27 May 1950 (Portuguese translation of the Elsevier text, signed Wolf Kneip, "narração de Silvio Scherli"); the Australian UFO newsletter Panorama (1964); Schiff Classic 5/2019 (Peter H. Block's German retelling, which reproduces Mielke's SOS texts and names the survivor "Jerry Rabbit").

## 2. The 1952 Coast Guard text

* **Citation**: "We Sail Together" [unsigned], Proceedings of the Merchant Marine Council, United States Coast Guard, Washington, vol. 9, no. 5 (May 1952), pp. 107-109; the Ourang Medan paragraph is on p. 107, col. 3. Official PDF: https://www.dco.uscg.mil/Portals/9/DCO%20Documents/Proceedings%20Magazine/Archive/1952/Vol9_No5_May1952.pdf (the USCG site returns 403 to automated clients; I used the Wayback Machine capture of 9 Dec 2025: http://web.archive.org/web/20251209204040/https://www.dco.uscg.mil/Portals/9/DCO%20Documents/Proceedings%20Magazine/Archive/1952/Vol9_No5_May1952.pdf). The archive.org item "proceedingsofmer40857unit" is the October 1954 issue (vol. 11 no. 10), not 1952.
* **Exact wording** (p. 107): "Perhaps one of the most perturbing sea dramas occurred in February 1948. Radio silence was broken with an urgent S. O. S. from the S. S. Ourang Medan, a Dutch vessel, then proceeding through the Straits of Malacca. The strange distress call, transmitted in Morse Code, eerily read, "S. O. S. from Ourang Medan * * * we float. All officers, including the Captain, dead in chartroom and on the bridge. Probably whole of crew dead * * *." A few confused dots and dashes later two words came through clearly. They were "I die." Then, nothing more. Later the Ourang Medan was found adrift approximately 50 miles from her indicated position. When the vessel which had stumbled across her sent a boat over to investigate, the sailors swarming aboard the Ourang Medan found a sight seldom seen. There wasn't a living person or creature on board. There were dead men every where. Bodies were strewn about the decks, in the passageways, in the charthouse, on the bridge. Sprawled on their backs, the frozen faces upturned to the sun with mouths gaping open and eyes staring, the dead bodies resembled horrible caricatures. Even the ship's dog was found dead. Yet, the bodies seemed to bear no sign of injury or wounds. Then, when a fire was discovered in No. 4 hold, she had to be abandoned. A few minutes later an explosion followed and the Ourang Medan sank. To this day no explanation has been offered as to what might have happened to the unfortunate ship's company."
* **Context**: the paragraph sits between anecdotes about the Spanish ship "Fell Antonia" (found off Ceylon "just before the end of the 19th century"), a nameless Dutch derelict found by the "Phoebus" in 1907 ("recorded in the Netherland Board of Trade files"), a Tasmanian schooner-ketch found on the Lacepede Archipelago ("the shipping files in North-west Australia"), the American schooner "Star", and the Titanic. The essay's point is that mysteries "make people think" about safety at sea. No author, no source, no reader's letter or correction followed in the issues I could check (Mar, Apr, Jul, Aug, Sep, Oct, Nov, Dec 1952; Jan, Feb, Mar 1953 — all downloaded from Wayback and searched for "Ourang", "Medan", "death ship"). **Not checked**: June 1952 and Apr–Jun 1953 (not in the Wayback Machine; the live USCG site blocks automated access).
* **Where the Coast Guard got it**: the wording tracks Win Brooks's American Weekly piece of 10 Oct 1948 (see §4), not the Elsevier/Locomotief originals (no missionary, no 22 dead, no Chinese troop-transport past) and not the 1940 AP text. The distinctive additions — "February 1948", disaster "in the Straits of Malacca", "frozen faces upturned to the sun", "horrible caricatures" — first appear here and were then quoted as the Coast Guard's own report by Hulse (1953), Jessup (1955), Marck (1959), Gaddis (1965) and Bainton (1999, who could not trace the document).

We made wild guesses at what had happened . . . mutiny . . . piracy . . . prisoners revolt. "At the end of 16 hours we sighted a big ship on the horizon. "The captain decided to search the officers' quarters but we heard an explosion in the ship's hold. A column of smoke belched from the second hatchway. "We rowed back to our ship. The explosions were repeated at short intervals so we withdrew to a safer distance. Soon nothing was left but the blazing hulk of the Ourang Medan. — Associated Press."

**Does the 1940 version credit Scherli or an Italian?** No. Neither British text names any person. The YEP's informant is "a merchant marine officer" (nationality unstated) who brought the account "to Trieste"; the Mirror's is "an officer of the rescue party" and the Mirror says the first ship on the scene was "of the British Merchant Marine". The AP evidently picked the story up in Trieste from the local press (Il Piccolo delle Ore Diciotto, 16 Oct 1940, by Silvio Scherl-Scherli, per Paijmans n. 29; two Vienna papers had already reprinted it on 20 Oct 1940, one citing "Piccolo di Trieste", n. 28). So the Trieste dateline is the fingerprint of Scherli, but the attribution to him is inferential, not stated.

Verdict: the 1948 Dutch text is a re-write of the same template — the two-stage SOS (doctor by short wave first, then the officers-dead message that breaks off), the flagless listing derelict, the second officer's body on the bridge, the unwounded dead "at their posts", the explosion from a hatch that ends the search, the night-long fire and morning sinking. 1948 adds the 22 dead, the dog, the radio operator at his key, "I am dying", the fourth hatch and the 50-mile error, and later (Feb 1948) the survivor and the missionary; it drops the warship and the "prisoners' revolt" speculation, and moves the scene 1,300 miles north and the date forward by 7½ years.

### 3.3 Where the Strait of Malacca and the Silver Star first appear
* **Strait of Malacca**: first in Brooks, American Weekly, 10 Oct 1948 (route out of Medan, "late May 1947"; the loss is still in the South Pacific in June). First as the scene of the disaster: Proceedings, May 1952 ("then proceeding through the Straits of Malacca", "February 1948"); thence Hulse/Fate June 1953, Jessup 1955, Edwards 1956, Marck 1959, Gaddis 1965, Sparks 1978, Bainton 1999.
* **Silver Star** (and City of Baltimore): first in Mielke, Das Totenschiff in der Südsee (1954) — in German, with the date 27-28 June 1947 and a South-Sea/Marshalls setting; first in English in Bainton, Fortean Times 125 (1999), who had it from Siersdorfer/Mielke and himself noted that Lloyd's showed the Silver Star renamed. Not in the Proceedings (1952), not in Fate/Jessup/Edwards/Gaddis/Sanderson. The rescue vessel is unnamed in every version before 1954 except Scherli's own Il Piccolo original (an "American torpedo boat W716", per Paijmans) and 7 Jours 1941 ("an American destroyer").

## 5. The book/magazine chain
* **Gaddis, Invisible Horizons (1965)**: the book is lending-only on archive.org, but its full-text index confirms the wording, and Sanderson (Invisible Residents, 1970, pp. 141-142) reprints Gaddis's account "in full" with permission (gaddis_invisible_horizons_1965_via_sanderson_excerpt.txt). Gaddis: "On a morning in early February, 1948, ... steaming through the Straits of Malacca between Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula, bound for Jakarta ... Suddenly Dutch and British marine radio listening posts picked up SOS calls ... 'Their frozen faces were upturned to the sun,' stated a report in the Proceedings of the Merchant Marine Council ... Minutes later the boilers of Ourang Medan exploded ... we may consider carbon monoxide or other toxic fumes from a smouldering fire or a leak in the boiler system as the cause". His only cited source is the Proceedings; a full-text search of his book for "Silver Star" returns nothing.
* **Jessup 1955, Edwards 1956, Marck 1959**: see §4. Jessup also aired the case in the Liverpool Echo, 30 Aug 1955, p. 4 ("Kidnapped into Space? This could explain the Mary Celeste", per Paijmans n. 18 / Wikipedia).
* **Bainton**: "A Cargo of Death", Fortean Times 125 (cover-dated Aug 1999; the NESWA reprint says "September 1999 issue, page 28"), pp. 28-31 — saved from the Wayback Machine (bainton_cargo_of_death_1999.txt); a slightly earlier version is in the Journal of Borderland Research v. 55 q2 (1999) (jbr_1999_v55q2_djvu.txt). Siersdorfer of Essen, and notes that Lloyd's showed the Silver Star "had changed owners and had another name ... Santa Cecilia". His later books (The Mammoth Book of Unexplained Phenomena, 2013) repeat this.
* **Mielke 1954**: no copy or scan located; Google Books has only a catalogue record (id LyxcHAAACAAJ). What it says is known from Bainton, Paijmans (n. 13, with the 1979 reprint title) and the German blog/magazine retellings: an American steamer Silver Star (Grace Line, with tonnage, engine power and captain's name) receives the SOS on 27 June 1947 together with the "City of Baltimore" (a ship's radio station) and Radio Sydney; the ship found on 28 June at 179° W 20° S; cargo 15,000 crates of sulphuric acid and potassium cyanide plus nitroglycerine; hydrogen-cyanide gas; the survivor (named "Jerry Rabbit" in Peter H. Block's Schiff Classic 5/2019 retelling, which reproduces Mielke's SOS texts "SOS-SOS-SOS + from ship ourang medan + the ship with sw get urgent dh medico +" and "SOS-SOS-SOS + ourang medan 179 grad west 20 grad sued + wir treiben + dritter offizier tot auf brücke + kapitän und ingenieur tot + wahrscheinlich alle im maschinenraum tot + teil der mannschaft ...") reaches Taongi in a lifeboat and dies after telling a missionary. Mielke gives no sources (Bainton). Note that Mielke's SOS is the Elsevier SOS, not the Coast Guard's — the German line descends from Elsevier, the American line from Brooks/Coast Guard.
* **Fortean Times 402 (Feb 2021), Theo Paijmans, "Death Ship", pp. 32-37** (paijmans_FT402_2021-02_death_ship_ocr.txt): the definitive modern study; concludes Scherli "crafted a template attractive enough to resell to different newspapers and magazines for eight long years", altering the rescue ship and the year each time; found Scherli's 1954 letter to the American Tobacco Company offering to sell "research material" for $780; notes a real Rotterdamsche Lloyd steamer "Medan" (1910-1930). FT June 2021 letters: Norman Darwen on the sharks/towline embellishments in Boar & Blundell, The World's Greatest Ghosts (1986).

## 6. A real ship? the Silver Star
* No 1947-48 US press item about a real crew found dead in Asian waters was found (Chronicling America, fultonhistory, archive.org full text). Every 1948 printing traces to Elsevier/Scherli.
* **Silver Star**: Lloyd's Register of Shipping 1947 (Steamers) lists "Santa Juana (ex Silver Star-47 ...) 6507 [tons] 1942 Federal ... Grace Line Inc"; LR 1952/53/54 and 1968/69: "Santa Juana (ex Silver Star-47, ex Santa Cecilia-46)". Shipbuildinghistory.com: Federal Shipbuilding, Kearny NJ, hull 233, MC hull 189, C2-S-B1 "Santa Cecilia", delivered Aug 1942, "sold private 1947, scrapped 1971". Grace Line took delivery of a new C2-S-AJ4 "Santa Cecilia" (North Carolina SB hull 239) in July 1946, which is why the 1942 ship became "Silver Star" in 1946 and "Santa Juana" in 1947. So Mielke's "Silver Star, owned by Grace Lines of New York" was a real name in 1946-47; whether she was ever in the Pacific is unknown (Grace Line's trades were New York/Pacific coast–South America), and Bainton reports that Grace Line never answered enquiries for her log. Blog claims that "the Silver Star was really the Silver Star Park, in Brazilian waters" confuse her with the Canadian Park tanker Silver Star Park (1943) → Santa Cecilia (II) (1945) → Ilha Grande (1951). Silver Line's British "Silverstar" is a different, later ship (LR 1969: Silver Star, 12,929 tons, John Brown 1950 — irrelevant).
* "City of Baltimore": no 1947 US merchant ship of that name identified (the Baltimore Mail liner became USS Heywood in 1940); in Block's retelling it is only a radio station acknowledging the SOS.

## 9. Surprises
1. The 1940 AP text dates the incident to **November 1939** ("last November"), which matches the 7 Jours (1941) version's "13 November 1939" and shows the Solomons/1939 setting was the original; Scherli's 1948 rewrite is therefore a re-dating of a story already eight years old.
2. The 1940 SOS asks for a **warship** and the boarders speculate about "mutiny ... piracy ... prisoners revolt" — Vienna's Kronen Zeitung headline "SOS vom Meutererschiff" (mutiny ship) is the same version. Neither "I die" nor the dog exists before 1948.
3. The Daily Mirror says a **British** merchant ship was first on the scene; the American Weekly says an **Italian** officer; Il Piccolo an **American** torpedo boat; 7 Jours an American destroyer; Mielke the American Silver Star. The rescuer's nationality was the most freely re-sold variable.
4. The Coast Guard's "February 1948" and "Straits of Malacca" look like a compression of Brooks's article (Malacca as route; "January ... a month later") rather than any independent report; and the Coast Guard, not Scherli, wrote the "frozen faces upturned to the sun" line that Gaddis, Jessup and Marck later quoted as official testimony.
5. The American Weekly reached the story via London ("A month later it appeared again in the London press", "London reports", "Lloyd's says") — so a February 1948 British printing almost certainly exists.
6. The CIA letter is not evidence of official interest: Marck was copying Jessup's book, prodigies and all.
7. A Portuguese-language version (Rio, May 1950, "narração de Silvio Scherli") and a Hong Kong reprint (1941) show how far the two templates travelled before the Coast Guard ever printed it.
