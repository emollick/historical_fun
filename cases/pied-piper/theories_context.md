# Hameln, 26 June 1284: the scholarly explanations and the historical context

> **Reading note.** Statements in this file about 26 June 1284, the 130 children, the east gate, the Calvary and the mothers' search describe the tradition as first attested c. 1430–50 (Lüneburg note) and later, not established facts of 1284. The report's verdict is a ranking of explanations, not a resolution: emigration ranks first at 42 in 100, the six alternatives together carry 58. See README.md and report.html.

Research memo. Method: web search in German and English; full text of Jürgen Udolph's 1997 article (Niedersächsisches Jahrbuch für Landesgeschichte 69, pp. 125–183, scanned PDF on adw-goe.de) was downloaded and extracted locally and is used throughout as the best available research history; Lübben's Middle Low German dictionary (Gutenberg #61948) and Grimm's DWB (DWDS) were consulted directly; the weekday was computed. Items marked **[unverified]** could not be checked against a primary or scholarly text. Wikipedia is used only as a pointer unless stated otherwise.

Working conventions: "Kinderauszugssage" = the child-exodus strand (attested from the lost Marktkirche window c. 1300 through the Lüneburg manuscript c. 1430/50); "Rattenvertreibungssage" = the rat-expulsion strand, first combined with the exodus in Froben Christoph von Zimmern's chronicle (1559–65) and in Johann Weyer (1563/68). The two-strand model goes back to Willy Krogmann (1934) and is common ground for every theory below.

## PART A — The theories

### A.1 Eastern colonization / emigration (Ostsiedlung)

**Prehistory.** Udolph (1997, p. 127 n. 8) notes that the emigrant-recruiter reading is old: already a 1704 miscellany ("Auserlesene Anmerkungen...") took the piper for a recruiter of colonists for Transylvania (cited after C. Soetemann, Nordost-Archiv 74, 1984, p. 10). The Grimms' Deutsche Sagen no. 245 (1816) end with the Transylvanian variant. Emily Gerard, *The Land Beyond the Forest* (1888, p. 30), reports the Transylvanian Saxon folk belief that they are the lost children of Hameln, emerged from the "Almescher Höhle". The 20th-century scientific versions are:

**(a) Wolfgang Wann — Moravia/Olmütz (1949; printed 1984).** Wann, an archivist from Troppau (Opava), *Die Lösung der Hamelner Rattenfängersage*, Phil. Diss. Würzburg 1949 (typescript); popular version "Von der Westfälischen zur Mährischen Pforte", *Der Klüt* 1948, pp. 46–57; posthumous edition by W. Scherzer, *Der Rattenfänger von Hameln. Hamelner Landeskinder zogen aus nach Mähren*, München 1984. Argument: the German settlement of the Olmütz episcopal lands in the last third of the 13th c. was organised by Bishop Bruno von Schauenburg (Bishop of Olmütz 10 Sept 1245 – 18 Feb 1281), a count of Schauenburg-Holstein whose family seat was the Schauenburg near Rinteln on the Weser, 25 km from Hameln; Bruno was King Ottokar II's adviser. Wann claimed (i) Low German place names in the Olmütz bishopric (Braunsberg, Brunswerde, Bruntál "Brunos Tal", Füllstein, Hotzenplotz district, etc.), (ii) a settlement *Hamlingen/Hamlíkov* near Olmütz derived from Hameln, (iii) personal names in Olmütz records matching Hameln burghers (Hamel/Hamal, Hämler, Hamlinus, Leist, Fargel, Ketteler, Stockvisch), (iv) that the emigrants were "the lower strata" (so Frenzel/Rumpf summarising Wann), i.e. "Kinder" = young burghers. Reception: adopted enthusiastically by Heinrich Spanuth (1951, p. 120: "Ich bin davon überzeugt, daß er ... den Nachweis ... geführt hat"), cautiously by Kurt Ranke; still cited by Brockhaus (19th ed. 1992) and Meyers (1977) as the standard explanation. **Objections (Udolph 1997, pp. 128–148, 179):** almost every Moravian place-name equation is wrong: Bruntál/Freudenthal is not named after Bruno; Hamlíkov (1385 Hamlicov; extinct) is a Czech formation, most probably from *Hamlík*/German "Hammel" (Hosák–Šrámek I, 239), not from Hameln — Dobbertin had already confused it with a different site; the "Weser" family names are common Low German names with no diagnostic value; F. v. Klocke (1940) showed that Westphalian inflow to Olmütz land began c. 1240 and that "after Bruno's death [1281] no noteworthy further inflow from Westphalia" occurred — i.e. the Olmütz colonisation was essentially finished by 1284. Udolph's verdict: "Von seinem Material bleibt fast nur im Bereich der Personennamen Belastbares übrig" (p. 148). L. Weinerth, *Was hat die Rattenfängersage von Hameln mit Olmütz zu tun?* (1971) is a Sudeten-German popularisation.

**(b) Heinrich Spanuth (1873–1958).** Hameln pastor/teacher; in 1936 found the Lüneburg manuscript (the c. 1430/50 Latin prose note on Heinrich von Herford's *Catena aurea*); doctoral thesis at 78: *Der Rattenfänger von Hameln. Vom Werden und Sinn einer alten Sage*, Diss. (masch.) Göttingen 1951; printed Hameln 1951 (repr. 1985); also *Geschichte der Stadt Hameln* (1955). Position: supported Wann's Moravia thesis but kept Transylvania, Bohemia and Pomerania open. Not an independent onomastic argument. (His son G. Spanuth, "Neuer Deutungsversuch des Exodus Hamelensis", *Forschungen und Fortschritte* 32, 1958, pp. 367–368, and 33, 1959, p. 380, proposed instead that the children sank "in den Koppen" while dancing on a bridge — see A.3.)

**(c) Hans Dobbertin (Eldagsen) — Pomerania / Rügenwalde-Kopahn / Prussia (1955–1996).** Key works (all verified in the NdsJb index and Udolph's bibliography): "Wohin zogen die Hämelschen Kinder (1284)?", *Niedersächsisches Jahrbuch für Landesgeschichte* 27 (1955), pp. 45–122 (also separately, Hildesheim 1955); "Neues zur Hamelner Rattenfängersage", *Fabula* 1 (1957/58), pp. 144–155; "Der Auszug der Hämelschen Kinder (1284): Ein Vermißtenschicksal der Kolonisationszeit wurde zur Volkssage", *Jb. f. Volkskunde der Heimatvertriebenen* 4 (1958), pp. 35–68; "Westdeutsche Burg-, Städte- und Ritternamen wurden zu ostdeutschen Ortsnamen", ibid. 5 (1959), pp. 121–160; "Livland- und Preußenfahrten westdeutscher Fürsten, Grafen und Edelherren im 13. Jh." (1962); "Die Hamelner Glasbildinschrift als Hauptquelle der Rattenfängersage", *Zeitschrift für Volkskunde* 62 (1966), pp. 29–42; *Quellensammlung zur Hamelner Rattenfängersage*, Göttingen 1970; "Berichtigungen und Ergänzungen zur Hamelner Kinderausfahrt (1284)", NdsJb 49 (1977); *Beiträge zur Hamelner Kinderausfahrt (1284)*, 2nd ed. Eldagsen 1981; "Personen- und Landesgeschichtliches zur Rügenwalder Gertrudkirchensage und zur Hamelner Kinderausfahrt (1284)", *Jb. f. ostdeutsche Volkskunde* 28 (1985), pp. 77–103; "Abschließendes zur Rattenfängersage", ibid. 29 (1986), pp. 245–273; "Urkundliches ... zum Kolonistendorf Spiegelberg bei Allenstein", *Preußenland* 25 (1987), pp. 35–39; "Hamelns älteste Quellen zur Kinderausfahrt", NdsJb 62 (1990), pp. 311–315; *Quellenaussagen zur Rattenfängersage*, Hameln 1966, enlarged ed. 1996. Argument: the "Kinder" were young Hamelners recruited as colonists; the "piper" mirrors the colourfully dressed *locator*; the leader was **Count Nicolaus von Spiegelberg** of Coppenbrügge, whose family had Slavic/Pomeranian kin ties (mother from the house of Schwerin; father Moritz I married Margarethe of Mecklenburg-Werle in 1248); "bi den Koppen" was identified with **Kopahn/Kopań**, a Johanniter manor ("1270 bis 1294 urkundlich als Johanniterhof (Cuppen, Kopan, Cvpan)") on the coast next to Rügenwalde (founded 1270 by Wizlaw II of Rügen on the Darlow hill, moved to the present site 1312); the party allegedly embarked there and was lost at sea — the Kopań Wikipedia article (after Dobbertin) even gives "22 July 1284" as the day they were "last seen" from the hill at Kopahn. A later variant, developed with Alfred Cammann ("Der Rattenfänger von Hameln und der Untergang in Masuren 1284–1984", *Jb. f. ostdeutsche Volkskunde* 27, 1984, pp. 1–58; "700 Jahre ... und die These vom Ende in Masuren", *Preußenland* 22, 1984), relocates the catastrophe to Prussia/Masuria, tying it to a colonist village *Spiegelberg* (Polish Spręcowo) north of Allenstein and to a Hameln bailiff "Johann von Alfeld, 1283 in Masuren als Vasall des Grafen Nikolaus von Spiegelberg verschollen" (Dobbertin, letter cited by Cammann, JbOstdVk 27, 1984, p. 48). **Evidence base:** (1) PUB II/2 (Prümers 1885) no. 1277: charter of Duke Bogislaw IV of Pomerania, Usedom, 13 Oct 1283, calling Count Konrad of Gützkow and Count Nicolaus of Spiegelberg "consanguinei nostri" (verified via de.wikipedia "Konrad von Gützkow" and "Grafen von Spiegelberg"; the Mecklenburg genealogical study on mvdok.lbmv.de could not be opened — bot wall); (2) the Pomeranian branch of the Eversteins (Hameln's former Vögte) at Naugard "seit 1274" (de.wikipedia "Everstein"); (3) place names Spiegelberg near Pasewalk (Uckermark) and near Allenstein. **Objections:** (i) The Koppen = Kopahn equation is rejected by Udolph (1997, pp. 161–162): *Koppe* is simply a variant of *Kuppe* "hill-top", one of dozens of such names; the Kopahn/Kopań name is Slavic (*kopa* "hill"); "Es ist unzulässig, aus der zufälligen Ähnlichkeit ... Folgerungen zu ziehen"; Dobbertin himself admitted (Beiträge 1981, p. 8) that it is uncertain whether the Johanniter *Kopan* (which was endowed at Gollnow near Stettin) is Kopahn at Rügenwalde at all; the Lüneburg manuscript does not contain "Koppen" (Frenzel/Rumpf 1966, p. 59). (ii) Nicolaus von Spiegelberg is last attested 13 Oct 1283; de.wikipedia's genealogy gives "Nikolaus I. († 1283)" — if that is a real death date (rather than 'last mentioned'), he cannot have led anyone in June 1284 **[unverified either way; W. Hartmann, "Die Grafen von Poppenburg-Spiegelberg", NdsJb 18 (1941), pp. 117–191, is the authority to check]**. (iii) The shipwreck, the date 22 July, and the Gertrud-chapel legend of Rügenwalde (the chapel is 15th-century) are constructions; note that 22 July is Richard Verstegan's (1628) date for the event ("22 July 1376") — the coincidence suggests Dobbertin borrowed the day. (iv) Kopań is first documented 1333 (de.wikipedia). (v) Udolph, while agreeing with Dobbertin on the NE direction, says Dobbertin made "schlimme Fehler" with names (e.g. Altfelde bei Marienburg ≠ Alfeld/Leine; Altschaumburg; Piepenburg 1320 *Pipenborg* has nothing to do with a piper).

**(d) Jürgen Udolph — Prignitz / Uckermark / (Vor)Pommern (1997).** "Zogen die Hamelner Aussiedler nach Mähren? Die Rattenfängersage aus namenkundlicher Sicht", NdsJb 69 (1997), pp. 125–183 (full text used; also popularised in taz 27 Jan 1998, Time/Independent 1998). Method: only transferred *place* names (not family names, whose value he dismisses) can locate the destination; he uses the Brandenburgisches Namenbuch (Wauer, Prignitz 1989; Uckermark in prep.) and Enders' Uckermark studies. Findings (pp. 157–180): *Hamelspringe* (Süntel, 1163 Hamelspring) → *Hammelspring* near Zehdenick, Uckermark (1375 *Havelspryng* in the Landbuch of Charles IV — I confirmed the entry "Hammelspring (sonst Havelspring). Ukm." in Fidicin's edition; 1438 *Hamelspringe*); the Hamelspringe knightly family (Conrad II) probably acted as settlement entrepreneurs north of Berlin; *Beverungen* (Weser) → *Beveringen* near Pritzwalk (Prignitz) → *Beweringen* near Stargard (Pomerania); *Dahlhausen* near Beverungen → Dahlhausen (Prignitz); the Hindenburg family (from the Beverungen area) → *Hindenburg* (Altmark 1283; Uckermark 1269 near Prenzlau and near Templin; Pomerania near Naugard, 1317 *territorium Hyndenburg*); *Spiegelberg* near Pasewalk and near Allenstein; *Rutenberg* (Uckermark 1300) from the Lower-Saxon Rutenberg family. Conclusion (Schlußwort, pp. 180–181): "nicht Pommern das eigentliche Ziel war, sondern die Prignitz und die Uckermark"; first wave over the Altmark, settling north of Berlin, only later across the Oder; transfers to Moravia are "verschwindend gering"; contacts with Moravia depended on Bruno and "erloschen" after 1281; "Wenn es einen Rattenfänger gegeben haben sollte ..., dann hätte er die Hamelner ... nur nach Brandenburg, in die Prignitz, die Uckermark oder nach Pommern geführt." He treats the piper as a late second strand and does not himself argue the 130 or the date. (Reports that he later extended the family-name comparison to Polish telephone books and pointed to Starogard (Stargard) belong to the 1998 press coverage.) **Objections:** none published in peer-reviewed form that I could find; the obvious ones are (i) transferred names prove Weser-area settlement in the NE in general (12th–14th c.), not a specific 1284 departure from Hameln; (ii) the earliest attestations (1269–1375) do not pin any village to 1284; (iii) no document links Hameln to any of these villages.

**(e) Other variants.** Teutonic Order/Prussia: implicit in Dobbertin/Cammann's Masuria variant and in Dobbertin 1962 ("Preußenfahrten"); contextually attractive because the great Prussian revolt ended in 1283 and systematic village-founding by the Order began right after (see B.3). Transylvania: rejected by all specialists — M. Kroner ("Der Rattenfänger von Hameln und die Siebenbürger Sachsen", *Südostdeutsche Vierteljahrsblätter* 33, 1984, pp. 169–172) states that Transylvanian-Saxon origin research finds no traces leading to Lower Saxony; A. Ostermeyer (Jb. Museumsverein Hameln 1982/84, pp. 59–60) likewise; Udolph: no Weser names there. Radu Florescu, *In Search of the Pied Piper* (2005), a popular book, combines a "German military leader" leading youths on a Baltic colonising voyage lost in a shipwreck (i.e. Dobbertin) with a real rat plague. **Mutual exclusivity:** (a) Moravia and (d) Prignitz/Uckermark are mutually exclusive as destinations; (c) and (d) share the NE direction but differ on leader (Spiegelberg), route (sea vs. land via Altmark) and outcome (catastrophe vs. successful settlement). All share the same reading of "Kinder" and "piper".

### A.2 Children's crusade / pilgrimage / procession

**Proponents.** The 1212 crusade link is attributed in popular literature to G. W. Leibniz **[unverified: no Leibniz text located]**; the 1911 Britannica (s.v. Hameln) records "some trace the origin of the legend to the Children's Crusade of 1211" and, as a separate theory, "a dancing mania which seized upon some of the young people of Hameln who left the town on a mad pilgrimage", with a bibliography of Sprenger (1861), Meinardus (*Der historische Kern der Hameler Rattenfängersage*, Hannover 1882 = ZHVNds 1882, pp. 256–304), Jostes (*Der Rattenfänger von Hameln. Ein Beitrag zur Sagenkunde*, 1885), Langlotz (1888), Baring-Gould (1868). Which of these authored the "mad pilgrimage" reading I could not confirm **[unverified; Meinardus or Jostes most likely]**. de.wikipedia: "Mehrere Historiker nehmen an, die Sage ... sei vom Kinderkreuzzug inspiriert worden."

**Objection (standard, de.wikipedia and hameln.de):** the crusade is 1212; every early Hameln witness (window c. 1300, the 1384 "hundred years" memory, Lüneburg MS, 1556 gate stone "ante annos 272", house inscriptions) puts the event in 1284. A structural point cuts both ways: Peter Raedts (*Journal of Medieval History* 3, 1977) showed that the 1212 *pueri* were largely young adults and the poor — exactly the philological reading of "Kinder" used by the emigration school (see B.4).

**Later "Kinderfahrten".** The best-documented mass youth pilgrimages are those from Germany and Switzerland to Mont-Saint-Michel 1456–1459: Hektor Mülich (Augsburg) reports groups of two or three hundred boys from the Rhine region in 1457; the Lübeck council chronicle records children wanting to go to "St. Michael's Mount" c. 1456; a Lucerne petition of 1457 is signed "wir bilgerkind ... von Zürich, Baden und ... Einsiedeln" (article "Die Kinderwallfahrten aus Deutschland und der Schweiz zum Mont-Saint-Michel 1456–1459", *Zeitschrift für schweizerische Kirchengeschichte* 63, 1969, pp. 221–332; author not captured). No scholar I found links them to Hameln except as a typological parallel; they post-date the Lüneburg manuscript, so they cannot have shaped the earliest layer, though they might have coloured 15th–16th-c. retellings. No Rome/Aachen pilgrimage explanation appears in the German specialist literature.

### A.3 Dancing mania / St Vitus / St John's dance

**Comparanda.** Erfurt 1237: Hecker (*Die Tanzwuth*, 1832; Engl. *Epidemics of the Middle Ages*, "more ancient dancing plagues") says "upwards of a hundred children ... at Erfurt ... proceeded dancing and jumping along the road to Arnstadt", many dying or left with tremor — no source cited; the elaborate version (15 June 1237, >1000 children, Löbertor, route via Waltersleben–Eischleben–Ichtershausen–Rudisleben, parents fetching them next day) is Ludwig Bechstein's *Deutsches Sagenbuch* (1853), i.e. a Sage; Martin Wähler, "Der Kindertanzzug von Erfurt nach Arnstadt im Jahre 1237" (journal article, details not retrievable — JPortal bot wall) is the specialist study; the medieval chronicle entry itself **[unverified]**. Utrecht/Maas 1278: again Hecker ("on the Moselle Bridge at Utrecht, on the 17th day of June, A.D. 1278, ... two hundred fanatics began to dance ... the bridge gave way, and they were all drowned"), garbled geography, no source; modern accounts (Bartholomew 2001) repeat "about 200 people on a bridge over the Meuse". Aachen: the great St John's dance began 24 June 1374 (en.wikipedia "Dancing mania", after Blom 2009). Gregor Rohmann, *Tanzwut* (2013, Habil. Frankfurt) treats "Tanzwut" as a culturally constructed illness concept, not mass hysteria — which weakens any biomedical reading.

**Who links them to Hameln?** Hecker does not mention Hameln. The link appears (i) in the 1911 Britannica's "dancing mania ... mad pilgrimage" theory; (ii) in G. Spanuth 1958 (children sunk "in den Koppen" while dancing on a bridge — an obvious transfer of the 1278 bridge story; dismissed by Udolph because "Koppen" is absent from the Lüneburg text); (iii) as "Tanzwut (Veitstanz)" in Kroner's 1984 list of the seven standard explanations; (iv) in M. Rumpf, "Die Hamelner Rattenfängersage medizinisch gedeutet", in Humburg (ed.) 1985, pp. 29–37 (medical reading; content not seen); (v) en.wikipedia, citing R. W. Marks 2005 and Schullian 1977. **Objections:** the Hameln sources describe an orderly departure behind a musician, not compulsive dancing or a return; the well-attested epidemics are 1374+ (Rohmann); the 13th-century cases rest on late or uncited reports; none produces 130 permanently "lost" persons.

### A.4 The battle of Sedemünde(r), 1259/1260

**Proponent.** Christoph Friedrich Fein, garrison preacher at Hameln (1742–61), *Die entlarvete Fabel vom Ausgange der Hämelschen Kinder* (Hannover 1749): the legend disguises the defeat of the Hameln townsmen by the Bishop of Minden; the piper is the recruiter who "lured 130 sons of the city" into an ambush at Sedemünder on **28 July 1259**; Fein therefore re-dated the exodus from 1284 to 1259. The 1911 Britannica still lists "the defeat of the Hameln men at Sedemünde in 1259 and their capture by the Bishop of Minden" among the theories; Meinardus (1882) discussed it; Kroner 1984 lists "Erinnerung an die großen Verluste Hamelns in der Schlacht von Sedemünde (1260)".

**Facts.** In 1259 the abbot of Fulda sold his rights over town and Stift Hameln to Bishop Wedekind of Minden (for 500 marks of silver: de.wikipedia "Schlacht bei Sedemünder"); the burghers refused homage; the citizen army was crushed at Sedemünder (a later-deserted village south of the Deistertor of Münder, i.e. NE of Hameln) on 28 July 1260 ("manchen Quellen zufolge 1259"), survivors taken captive; in autumn 1260 Dukes Albrecht I and Johann of Brunswick-Lüneburg brokered a settlement; 1268 Albrecht acquired the Vogtei; 1277 he confirmed the town's rights (Stadt Hameln, "Geschichte der Stadt Hameln", official PDF; de.wikipedia "Hameln"). Literature: A. Gercke, *Sedemünder – das ältere Münder im Sünteltal* (1975).

**Why it is dated wrong / rejected.** (i) Date: the battle is 28 July 1259/60; the exodus is 26 June 1284 in every witness, and Hameln itself computed 272 years in 1556 and "100 years" in 1384; no source supports Fein's arithmetic ("supporters have never been able to explain why the legend was then dated so precisely to ... 1284, 25 years later" — de.wikipedia). (ii) The battle was public, well remembered and documented; a town does not mythologise a battle into a children's abduction within a century while its own records keep the true date. (iii) "130 sons" is Fein's invention. The theory survives only as antiquarian history (17th–19th c.).

### A.5 Plague, ergot, sinkhole, murder, Death

**Plague.** Objection identical to A.2: "Pestepidemien im mittelalterlichen Europa setzten erst 1347 ein" (de.wikipedia). The medical version: D. Wolfers, "A Plaguey Piper", *The Lancet* 285/7388 (April 1965), pp. 756–757 — piper as plague allegory (children as prime victims); article content not accessed. A local epidemic in 1284 is not attested for Hameln. The Hameln Stadtarchiv's exhibition "Rattenfänger, Pest und Ratten" treated plague/Totentanz as later cultural context, not as the 1284 cause.

**Ergotism.** Only popular/online literature (2020s blogs) proposes rye ergot in "the damp Weser valley" as the trigger of a dancing exodus; no scholarly proponent found. Rumpf 1985 (medical reading) is the only specialist candidate **[content unverified]**.

**Landslide/sinkhole/bog on the "Koppen" — Woeller, Hüsam.** Waltraud Woeller (Humboldt University Berlin): "Zur Sage vom Rattenfänger zu Hameln", *Wiss. Zeitschrift der Humboldt-Univ. Berlin*, Ges.-u. sprachwiss. Reihe 6 (1956/57), pp. 135–145, and "Zur Entstehung und Entwicklung der Sage vom Rattenfänger von Hameln", *Zeitschrift für deutsche Philologie* 80 (1961), pp. 180–206; reported as her 1957 Habilitation. She proposed the "Teufelsküche" in the Ith near Coppenbrügge (a partly swampy hollow with unstable rock) as the end point, admitting she could not explain how 130 people could die there at once. Gernot Hüsam (b. 1941, Coppenbrügge; museum founder in Burg Coppenbrügge, 1983): identifies "Calvaria/Koppen" with the Koppenberg/Heerburg at Coppenbrügge; papers "Das Reimgebet im Hamelner 'Passionale'" (1994), "Der Begriff 'Calvaria' in der Rattenfängersage" (1995), "Das Aquarell aus der Mörsperger Reisechronik" (1996); book *Neuestes zur Rattenfängersage – Hamelns fast gelöster Kriminalfall* (novum Verlag, 2024, ISBN 978-3-99130-491-3). Thesis (2024 reading sample; piedpiper.international summary): Hameln youths gathered around St John's night 1284 at a pre-Christian Wodan sanctuary (the skull-shaped Teufelsküche) on the Ith; the Spiegelberg counts, who had acquired the Coppenbrügge lordship c. 1281 and enforced Christian order "mitunter auch durch Gewalt", eliminated them ("Was the end at the Koppenberg a planned murder?" — Hüsam 1990); rock faces found in 1987 and hidden imagery (three stags) in the 1592 Mörsperg watercolour are his "evidence"; he treats the rhymed Latin prayer in the Hameln Passionale as composed in 1284 (scholars regard the short "bad Latin" rhyme as later and no original survives — Dewezet report). de.wikipedia files this as a "weniger stark vertretene Theorie" (pagan sect leader, ritual dances near Coppenbrügge, Bergrutsch/Erdfall). **Objections:** no archaeological finds of 130 bodies; the counts of Spiegelberg's only 1280s documentation places Nicolaus in Pomerania; the "evidence" is iconographic over-reading; Woeller herself conceded the mechanism problem. Udolph (pp. 161–162) shows "Koppen" is an ordinary hill word; the only sober identification is F. C. Müller's: "Hügel ostwärts des mittelalterlichen Hameln ... im Zuge von Eisenbahn- und Industriebauten eingeebnet", with Wann citing a 1356 field-name attestation "Die sogenannten Koppen" **[1356 attestation not verified]**.

**Earthquake.** No proponent found (nor any 1284 earthquake in the region).

**Murderer/paedophile (20th c.).** William Manchester, *A World Lit Only by Fire* (1992), asserts without evidence that the piper was a psychopathic paedophile who "used them in unspeakable ways", misdating the event to 20 June 1484; Jeremy duQuesnay Adams' review (*Speculum* 70, 1995, pp. 173–74) calls the book full of gratuitous errors. Hüsam's "planned murder" (1990) is the local variant. Fanny Rostek-Lühmann (*Der Kinderfänger von Hameln. Untersagte Wünsche und die Funktion des Fremden*, 1995; 1992 essay online) offers a psychoanalytic reading: the Passionale prayer blames the "rei" = parents; the stranger is the projected executor of a wish to be rid of children — a reading of the *legend*, not the event. She counts "insgesamt fünfundzwanzig verschiedene Theorien" (1992, p. 45).

**Piper as Death / psychopomp.** A widespread interpretive gloss (en.wikipedia "natural causes": the piper as symbolic Death, Totentanz), not a historical theory: the Danse macabre iconography is 15th-century (Paris 1424; Lübeck 1463), so it can at most explain the 16th-century shaping of the piper (Zimmern's devil-figure) rather than 1284. The earliest depiction (1592 drawing of the 1572 window) shows a "Schalmeipfeifer" in parti-coloured dress — the costume of a *Spielmann*, and also, as Udolph and the Hameln museum stress, of the *Lokator* who advertised with music in market places.

### A.6 Folkloric readings, motif migration, handbooks

- **Krogmann 1934** (*Der Rattenfänger von Hameln. Eine Untersuchung über das Werden der Sage*, Germanische Studien 158, Berlin) and "Der Rattenfänger von Hameln", *Rheinisch-westfälische Zs. f. Volkskunde* 14 (1967), pp. 130–150: foundational literary-critical genealogy of the texts; established the late (1559–65) entry of the rat strand; Udolph notes Krogmann "die Namen kaum berücksichtigte".
- **Aetiological reading of Koppen/Calvarie/Bungelosenstraße.** The Lüneburg MS's "quasi ad locum calvarie vel decollacionis" makes "Calvary" the town's execution place; the 1602/03 Rattenfängerhaus inscription "to calvarie bi den koppen verloren" and the Hochzeitshaus (1610–17) "bei den Koppen" tie the loss to the hills east of the Ostertor; the drum-less street is the last place the children were seen. Krogmann, Woeller, Lauffer (O. Lauffer, "Altertumskundliche Beiträge zur Erklärung der Sage von den Hämelschen Kindern", 1949) and Gehrts ("Zur Rattenfängersage", ZfdPh 74, 1955) treat these as sediment of legend formation (Bergentrückung motif: the mountain as realm of the dead — Rostek-Lühmann citing the Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens).
- **Migration of the rat-catcher motif.** Parallels collected by D. L. Ashliman (pitt.edu): Lorch/Rhine "Die Zauberpfeife" (Hefner & Wolf 1850); Korneuburg (Bermann 1864, set in 1646); Brandenburg hurdy-gurdy man (Kuhn & Schwartz 1848); Newtown, Isle of Wight (Elder 1839/Jacobs); Erfurt 1237/1257 (Sommer 1846; Grässe 1868). All are post-1550 literary or folk variants; the rat-catcher is a 16th-century wandering motif attached to the older Hameln exodus (Zimmern 1565; Weyer 1563/68; Hondorff 1568).
- **Wolfgang Mieder,** *The Pied Piper: A Handbook* (Greenwood 2007; German: *Der Rattenfänger von Hameln. Die Sage in Literatur, Medien und Karikatur*, 2002): a source and reception compendium; supports the emigration reading (documents Hameln-area settlers in Transylvania — a claim the German specialists reject, see A.1e); in 1985 (in Humburg's volume, p. 113) he declared that after Krogmann, Wann, Spanuth and Dobbertin "die historische Erforschung als abgeschlossen erklärt werden kann".
- **Museum/Hameln volumes.** Norbert Humburg (museum director), *Der Rattenfänger von Hameln. Die berühmte Sagengestalt in Geschichte und Literatur, Malerei und Musik, auf der Bühne und im Film* (Hameln 1984, 2nd ed. 1990); Humburg (ed.), *Geschichten und Geschichte. ## PART B — Historical context

### B.1 Hameln c. 1284

- Lordship: Hameln grew out of the Fulda proprietary church founded 802/812 by Count Bernhard and Christina; Fulda's Benedictine monastery/collegiate Stift St. Bonifatius (documented 1054/1074); "um 1200 als Stadt genannt"; Fulda exercised lordship through its Vögte, the **counts of Everstein**. 1259: abbot sells his rights to the Bishop of Minden; burghers refuse; 28 July 1260 (or 1259) defeat at Sedemünder; autumn 1260 Welf mediation; 1268 Duke Albrecht I of Brunswick(-Lüneburg) obtains the Vogtei; 1277 privilege confirming the town's rights ("den vollen Besitz der bürgerlichen Freiheiten"). Hameln then counted as one of the four major Welf towns; Hanse member 1426–1572. The "Mühlending" court before the Wesertor, presided over by the ducal Vogt, and the Weser weir/millstone trade shaped the economy (Stadt Hameln PDF "Geschichte der Stadt Hameln"). In 1284 itself the Welfs forced the Eversteins to sell Burg Everstein (de.wikipedia "Everstein") — the year was one of Welf consolidation on the Weser. Sources: O. Meinardus (ed.), *Urkundenbuch des Stiftes und der Stadt Hameln* I (to 1407), Hannover 1887 (Hathi/archive.org; not searched here); C. Meckseper, "Zur mittelalterlichen Topographie von Hameln", NdsJb 52 (1980), pp. 203–217; K. Lübeck, "Das Fuldaer Eigenkloster Hameln", NdsJb 16 (1939); Stadtarchiv Hameln, *Studien zur Hamelner Geschichte* 3 (OAPEN; blocked 403).
- Population: no scholarly estimate for 1284 was found. Secondary figures: "approximately 2,000 inhabitants" as a Welf town after 1277 (de.wikipedia "Schlacht bei Sedemünder", unsourced); "kaum 2000 Einwohner" in 1344 (juedische-geschichte-hameln.de, unsourced; it derives c. 120 Jews = >5 %); 2,398 in 1689. At ~2,000, 130 persons = 6.5 % of everyone, and — if "Kinder" = unmarried young people aged c. 14–30 (roughly a quarter of a medieval town) — something like a quarter of the town's youth: a demographic wound consistent with a locator's recruitment of a village-founding group (a Waldhufen/Anger village of 40–64 Hufen needs 40–64 households) and with the town's century-long memory. **[All figures secondary.]**
- Governing families: not recoverable from the web sources used; the Urkundenbuch (Meinardus 1887) is the place to look (a Hameln dean is attested 1274; council/Bürgermeister appear in 13th-c. charters). **[gap]**

### B.2 The regional nobility

- **Counts of Spiegelberg** (de.wikipedia "Grafen von Spiegelberg"; Hartmann 1941; Schnath, *Die Herrschaften Everstein, Homburg und Spiegelberg*, 1922; Mooyer 1856): Moritz I (c. 1215 – after 1289), m. 1248 Margarethe of Mecklenburg-Werle; acquired in 1281 the lands forming the Grafschaft Spiegelberg with Burg Coppenbrügge as seat (a county of only five villages; the older Burg Spiegelberg near Lauenstein/Ith); **Nicolaus I († 1283 per Wikipedia)**, witness in Bogislaw IV's charter of 13 Oct 1283 at Usedom as "consanguineus" of the duke (PUB II/2 no. 1277); Moritz II (1256–1309). Whether Nicolaus is attested after Oct 1283 is the crux for Dobbertin **[unverified]**. Colonist villages named Spiegelberg exist near Pasewalk (Uckermark) and near Allenstein (Ermland/Masuria) (Udolph no. 26; Dobbertin 1987).
- **Counts of Everstein**: Fulda's Vögte over Hameln until 1259/68; seats Polle, Holzminden, Ohsen; Konrad IV of Ohsen (fl. 1259–85); the family took part in Landesausbau as locators (Vogtland c. 1100) and Otto IV (1266–1313) founded the Eberstein-Naugard line in Pomerania "seit 1274" — i.e. Hameln's former lords had a Pomeranian branch exactly in the 1270s–80s, in the Naugard district where Udolph's Hindenburg family also settled (1317). 1284: forced sale of Burg Everstein to the Welfs.
- **Edelherren von Homburg** (seat Homburg near Stadtoldendorf, guarding Amelungsborn): Bodo the Younger (r. to 1228, murdered by a count of Everstein); Heinrich (r. to 1289; gave Stadtoldendorf town rights 1255); Bodo (r. to 1303; Bodenwerder named after him); the lordship later fell to the Welfs (de.wikipedia "Edelherren von Homburg"). No connection to 1284 claimed by anyone.

### B.3 Ostsiedlung in 1284 — where recruiters were active

- **Brandenburg (Askanier: Johann I's and Otto III's sons; Prignitz/Uckermark/Neumark):** Prignitz resettled since the Wendish crusade (1147) "especially from the Bremen area" (Landkreis Prignitz); the Uckermark wholly Brandenburg since the Treaty of Landin (1250) and, per L. Enders (*Die Uckermark*, 1992, pp. 45–51), "von Norden und Nordwesten her aus dem holsteinisch-mecklenburgischen und westfälisch-niedersächsischen Raum beeinflußt"; Neumark town foundations Landsberg 1257, Soldin after 1261, Arnswalde before 1269, Berlinchen 1278, Schivelbein c. 1292, Dramburg 1297; c. 1280 the margraves pushed into Hinterpommern (Land Schivelbein bought from the Bishop of Cammin c. 1280). Wichmann of Magdeburg had recruited Flemings and Westphalians from 1158. Udolph's whole name evidence sits in this zone (Hammelspring 1375, Hindenburg near Prenzlau 1269, Rutenberg 1300).
- **Pomerania (Bogislaw IV, r. 1278–1309, son of the great town-founder Barnim I):** Barnim I gave his church at Pyritz to Kloster Wülfinghausen (20 km from Hameln) in 1250, with a Magdeburg safe-conduct showing the convent's people travelling east (Udolph no. 14); Bogislaw was at war with Brandenburg in the early 1280s; the Spiegelberg count appears at his court 1283. **Land Schlawe/Rügenwalde**, where Kopahn lies, was in 1284 contested: pledged c. 1270 to Wizlaw II of Rügen (who founded Rügenwalde 1270), his pledge rights sold 1277 to Brandenburg, with Mestwin II of Pomerelia (r. 1273–94) the other claimant; the Swenzones held it by 1333; the Johanniter had a Hof "Kopan" 1270–94 (Dobbertin) and a Johanniter Gerhard von Kopan is named 1285/87 (de.wikipedia "Kopań"). Nobles from Lower Saxony migrated to Mecklenburg and Vorpommern throughout the 13th c. (F. Bertheau, ZHVNds 80, 1915).
- **Prussia (Teutonic Order):** the Great Prussian Uprising ended in 1283; thereafter the Order founded c. 100 towns and c. 1,000 villages, colonists "mostly Rhenish, Westphalian and Dutch" (de.wikipedia "Prußen"; C. Krollmann, "Die Herkunft der deutschen Ansiedler in Preußen", 1912; Th. Penners 1942 on town-dwellers' origins) — 1284 is precisely the first year of secure large-scale recruitment for Prussia, the background to the Dobbertin/Cammann Masuria variant (Spiegelberg near Allenstein; Rautenberg in Ermland).
- **Moravia/Bohemia:** Wenceslaus II nominally king from 1278 (under Otto V of Brandenburg's regency until 1283); Bishop Bruno of Olmütz died 18 Feb 1281; Klocke: no notable Westphalian inflow after his death; the bishopric's colonisation ran from c. 1240 — so 1284 is late for Moravia (Udolph's decisive argument against Wann).
- **Transylvania:** Saxon settlement 1141–62 (Andreanum 1224); post-Mongol (1241) repopulation drew on existing Saxon and Hungarian stock; Kroner: no origin traces lead to Lower Saxony.
- **Mecklenburg-Werle:** the Spiegelberg marriage of 1248 gives the family a Werle/Schwerin network — Dobbertin's genealogical starting point.
- **How locators recruited; group size.** The territorial lord contracted a *Lokator* (lesser noble or burgher) who advertised, assembled, equipped and led the settlers, laid out the village in equal Hufen (Flemish Hufe c. 16.8 ha; Franconian c. 24.2 ha) and became its hereditary Schulze (de.wikipedia "Hochmittelalterliche Ostsiedlung"; en.wikipedia "Lokator"). The classic primary description of recruitment is Helmold, *Chronica Slavorum* I.57 (c. 1170): Count Adolf II of Holstein "sent messengers into all the regions, namely Flanders and Holland, Utrecht, Westphalia and Frisia" to call settlers with promises of land. Villages of 40–64 Hufen were normal in the Uckermark (Landbuch 1375); a group of 130 young adults would therefore staff one or two new villages — the size is not implausible for a single recruitment.

### B.4 "Kinder" / "pueri" — philology

- Grimm, *Deutsches Wörterbuch* s.v. *kind* (DWDS): 3c — "mhd. aber umfaszte kint auch den jüngling, jungen mann, die jungfrau mit, ohne bezug auf die eltern" (e.g. Hätzlerin: two lovers in bed are "die selben chinder baid"; a 16th-c. poet excuses himself "ich bin noch ain junges kind"); 3d — craftsmen call their journeymen *kinder* ("so die tuchscherer"), and "de schepeskinder, schiffskinder" = crew; 7a — "ein kind des ortes, der gemeinde", *stadtkind*, "ein Leipziger kind", "ich ein Frankfurter kind" (Goethe), *landeskind*.
- Middle Low German (the language of the Hameln inscriptions): Lübben, *Mittelniederdeutsches Handwörterbuch* (1888), s.v. *kint*: "n. Kind; ... de schipher u. sine kindere, d. i. Matrosen" — a skipper's "children" are his crew; *adelkint* = a person of free birth; *kerkspel-kint* = parishioner. So "cxxx kinder binnen hameln geboren" can mean 130 natives/young people of the town, not 130 infants. (Schiller–Lübben's full dictionary was not consulted.)
- Latin: Lewis & Short s.v. *puer* gives besides "boy" the senses "a grown-up youth, young man" (Octavian at 19, Scipio at 20) and "a boy for attendance, a servant, slave" (Perseus). Medieval usage widens this further (Du Cange s.v. PUER — site unreachable; Niermeyer not consulted) — cf. Raedts on the *pueri* of 1212.
- Caveat: the Lüneburg MS's Latin says *pueri*/children who followed the piper "in the number of 130" and adds that the mother of Dean Johann von Lüde saw them leave; the vernacular 16th/17th-c. texts say *kinder*. Both words permit the "young townspeople" reading but do not require it; the Grimm-era assumption that they were "unter acht oder neun Jahren" (de.wikipedia) is the Grimms' embellishment.

### B.5 Calendar

- 26 June is the feast of SS John and Paul (4th-c. Roman martyrs, in the Roman Canon since the 5th c.); in the German countryside they were *Wetterheilige/Wetterherren*, invoked against hail and storms, with Hagelprozessionen and the blessing of black weather candles on their day, attested as weather patrons as early as 1150 in the imperial chronicle (de.wikipedia "Johannes und Paulus"). The day falls two days after St John's Day (24 June, Midsummer bonfires and dances) — the hook for Hüsam's "St John's night" ritual and for the dancing-mania comparanda (Aachen began 24 June 1374).
- Weekday: 26 June 1284 (Julian) = **Monday** (JDN 2 190 216; = 3 July 1284 proleptic Gregorian, also Monday; Easter 1284 = 9 April). Hence 24 June 1284 = Saturday, 25 June = Sunday. The later tradition that the adults were "in church" when the children left (a Sunday/feast-day scene) belongs to the 16th-c. narrative; on a Monday the feast of two weather saints would still have been a holy day for processions in a town so dependent on the Weser and the harvest.

### B.6 Topography

- **Ostertor / Bungelosenstraße:** the children left "through the East Gate" (Lüneburg MS: entered by the West Gate over the bridges, left by the East Gate) "quasi ad locum calvarie vel decollacionis"; the Bungelosenstraße (MND *bunge* "Pauke, Trommel" — Lübben; hence "drum-less street"), first named as such in 1475 (Weserbergland-Tourismus **[secondary]**), leads to the Ostertor; no music or dancing is allowed there by custom. Meckseper 1980 for the medieval street plan.
- **Koppen / Calvarie:** "to calvarie bi den koppen verloren" (Rattenfängerhaus, 1602/03) and "bei den Koppen" (Hochzeitshaus 1610–17); Wann: field name "Die sogenannten Koppen" 1356 **[unverified]**; F. C. Müller/Udolph: the *Koppen* = hills east of the medieval town, levelled for railway and industry; "Calvaria/locus calvarie" = the execution place (Kalvarienberg/Galgenberg being a common name for gallows hills) — thus in the oldest Latin text the children go out of the east gate "as if towards the place of execution" and vanish. The Coppenbrügge Koppenberg/Poppenberg (with two cross-stones, per local tradition) and the Ith "Teufelsküche" are the competing folk identifications (Woeller, Hüsam); Coppenbrügge lies c. 10 km ESE of Hameln on the road to Hildesheim/Braunschweig — i.e. on the natural land route toward the Altmark and Brandenburg, which is consistent with, but does not prove, Udolph's route. Spanuth (G.) derives *Koppen* from MND *kopp* "Becher, Hirnschale" (skull) — a pun on Calvaria = Schädelstätte that Udolph does not accept.

**Bottom line for testing against the sources:** every serious modern specialist (Dobbertin, Udolph, Humburg, Mieder, the Hameln museum) converges on "young townspeople recruited for eastern settlement" as the only reading compatible with (i) the precise, unanimous date, (ii) a round but specific number, (iii) the departure eastward behind a parti-coloured musician, and (iv) a memory of loss without bodies. The destination is unresolved: Udolph's onomastic case for Prignitz/Uckermark is the best-founded, Dobbertin's Spiegelberg/Kopahn scenario is the most detailed but rests on a rejected name equation and an unverified survival of Nicolaus into 1284. The one thing no theory explains is why the sources speak of a *loss* ("verloren", "perdiderunt") rather than a departure — unless the memory was shaped by an actual catastrophe on the road or at sea (Dobbertin/Cammann), or by the simple fact that emigrants never returned.
