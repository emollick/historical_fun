## 0. Headline findings

1. **Paracelsus names "Frau Troffea" exactly once** in the whole Huser edition (THEO search: one hit), in *De causis morborum invisibilium* (Huser vol. 1 p. 263 = Sudhoff I/9 p. 278). The chapter "Von der kranckheit / die mann nennet die Veitstantz" is a moral-psychological parable about a woman who feigned a dancing sickness to spite her husband, was imitated by other women, and whose pretence hardened, through belief, into a real disease that the crowd then attributed to St Vitus. It contains **no place-name, no year, no number of dancers, no death, no pilgrimage, no cure, no "vor vil jaren", and no statement that Paracelsus saw anything himself**. The words "Strasbourg" and "1518" occur nowhere near it (THEO: the 13 hits for Straßburg/Strasburg in Huser are all imprints, editorial matter or prognostications).
2. The medical classification (vitista / chorea aestimativa-imaginativa; chorea lasciva; chorea naturalis or coacta) stands in a different, earlier work, *De morbis amentium* (Sudhoff dates it 1525/26), Tract. I cap. 3 (origin; Huser IV,51–55 = Sudhoff I/2, 407–412) and Tract. II cap. 3 (cure; Huser IV,79–82 = Sudhoff I/2, 439–442). The cures — a wax or resin image burnt in the fire; dark confinement on bread and water "ohn erbarmung", a good stick, cold water; quintessences — belong there, not to the Troffea chapter, and are never connected with Strasbourg.
3. **No Strasbourg source names the first dancer.** The texts I read (Imlin chronicle, Reuss 1875 p. 26, image; Wencker's *Extractus* no. 3443 and Wencker's chronicle no. 3007 in Dacheux 1892; Specklin and Kleinlawel as printed by Martin 1914 pp. 116, 119; Rem 1518, image) all say "ein frauw"/"eine frau" or nothing. This agrees with the survey of every narrative source in `A2_chronicles.md` and with Clementz (2016, 2018): "Frau Trofféa n'a jamais existé … Son patronyme est une invention pédante de Paracelse … à partir du substantif grec *tropaia*". The coupling of the name with the date 14 July 1518 and with Strasbourg is John Waller's (2008/2009); Miller 2017's statement that "four of the six chronicle accounts" name "Lady Troffea" (citing Backman p. 237) is not borne out by any chronicle text I could read.
4. **How the ten standard claims arose** (details in Part 3): "fifteen a day" is the contemporary Augsburg chronicler Wilhelm Rem's *incidence* figure ("ain tag wol bei 15 menschen ankam" — fifteen people a day *were seized*), turned into a *death rate* first (as far as retrieved) in Waller's 2009 book and *Lancet* essay and copied into Wikipedia on 8 April 2016 from the *Lancet*; "400" is Martin's (1914) printing of the Imlin chronicle after Witkowski ("mehr denn 400"), whereas Reuss's 1875 edition from the manuscript reads "mehr dan 200" (checked on the page image); the stage, hired dancers, drums and pipes come from Specklin (c. 1580) and Wencker (1637); the red shoes, crosses, chrism and holy water from Specklin; the ban on dancing "bis Michaelis" with the wedding exception from Wencker's *Extractus* of the council record (the 2 August 1518 mandate); the physicians' "natural disease" verdict is contemporary (letter of 25 July 1518), but the same letter attributes the disease to an *astral conjunction and the heat*, which Waller says the physicians "excluded"; deaths first appear in Specklin ("Viel tanzten sich zu tode") and the 17th-century chronicles, never in a contemporary record; "heart attack, stroke or exhaustion" is Waller's 2008 phrasing (Endeavour and the Discovery News interview) which the first Wikipedia revision (8 Aug 2008) hardened into "Most of them died".
5. Hecker (1832) is not the source of any of these ten claims: he dates the Strasbourg episode 1418 (after Schilter 1698), gives no numbers except Schilter's verse "Viel hundert", no deaths, no Troffea, and knows Paracelsus only as a classifier of St Vitus's dance.
6. What was actually read of the principal modern works: Hecker 1832 — the relevant sections in full from OCR; Waller — *Endeavour* 2008, *Lancet* 2009 and *Psychologist* 2009 in full, the 2009 book **only through c. 120 search-inside snippets** (the book was not read); Backman 1952 — **snippets only, not read**; Midelfort 1999 — the section "A Dancing Mania" (pp. 32–49) read in full from the scan text saved in `midelfort-a2/` (unofficial upload; provenance flagged); Miller 2017, Bartholomew 2000, Rohmann 2020 (ZGO) in full; Rohmann 2009/2013 and Gotman 2018 not obtained.

(Geschrieben 1525 u. So: c. 1531–32, some thirteen years after the events and four or five after his Strasbourg stay.

Transmission (Sudhoff, Bd. 9 introduction and notes). Before Huser the work on invisible diseases "ist … nur einmal gedruckt worden" — in 1565, when the Cologne physician Theodor Birckmann appended "De morbis invisibilibus" to his print of the *Liber secundus De causa et origine morborum* (Bd. 9 intro, OCR lines 300–312 and 505–512). Huser 1589 printed it "leider nicht nach dem Original, sondern nach dem Manuskript eines (nicht genannten) andern", which differs "sehr erheblich vom Erstdruck"; the Vienna codex agrees with Huser's text, so both go back to the same copy, "offenbar das Neuburger Originalmanuskript" (OCR lines 505–525). Sudhoff also calls the whole work "eine erste Ausarbeitung mit allen Vorzügen und Mängeln einer solchen" and "ein Torso" (lines 540–556). Consequence for us: the Troffea passage is known from a 1565 print and a manuscript copy, not from an autograph; no variant affecting the name is recorded in Sudhoff's apparatus for p. 278 (apparatus read, OCR lines 26892–26960: the notes for pp. 251–265 record wording variants from "65" [the 1565 print] and "V" [Vienna codex] but nothing for the name).

### 1.2 The Troffea chapter — full text (Huser I,263–264) and translation

Huser 1589, vol. 1, p. 263 (THEO `theo_HE01_263_s0.html`; Sudhoff I/9, 278–279 agrees word for word apart from spelling):

> **Von der kranckheit / die mann nennet die Veitstantz.**
> Es ist ein seltzammer vrsprung in dieser kranckheit / etwas geteilt von denen / die gemeldt seind : Vnnd seindt also herkommen. Die Fraw Troffea ist die erste gsein in der kranckheit / dieselbige nam sich seltzamer lauhn vnnd weise an : Vnnd so sie in ein bleen kam / vnnd hals strittig gegen jhrem Mann / so er sie etwas hieß / das jhr nicht gefiele / so nam sie sich einer weise an / sie wer kranck / vnnd erdichtet ein kranckheit die jhr auff dißmal gelegen was. Nuhn nam sie sich der weise an zum tantzen / vnnd gab für / sie könnte nicht ohn getantzet sein : Dann den Mann verdruß nichts mehr dann das tantzen : Vnnd damit sie dem handel gnug thete / vnnd bestettete ein gleichnuß einer kranckheit / so hupfft sie vnnd sprang auff / sang vnnd lälet / vnd was dem Manne am vbelsten gefiel : Vnnd nach außgang des tantz / so fiel sie nieder / dem Manne zuleidt / zablet eine weil / vnnd schlieff darnach. Solchs zeigt sie für eine kranckheit an / vnnd verschwieg / das sie den Mann also närret. Hierauff begab sichs / das auch ander Weiber dergleichen solcher weise pflegten / vnnd vnterweiset je eine die ander : Vnd kam dahin / das die Gemeindt solche kranckheit für ein Buß hielten / vnd namen auß diesem anzeigen ein vrsachen / der jhn die kranckheit vertrieb. Auff die vrsach hafftet der Glaub / vnnd geriet in den Magor / ein Heydnischen Geist : Aber es blieb nicht lang / do ward Sanct Veit der Glaubengeist / vnnd must also hie zu eim Abgott werden / vnnd empfieng den namen darauff Sanct Veits Tantz.
> Kahm darnach dahin / das menniglichen in solchen glauben kahm / vnd ward also bestetet zu einer kranckheit : Vnd auff die / [p. 264] denen wol mit dantzen war / auff die fiel der Glaub / vnd behaftet dieselbig kranckheit / das sie also bleib neben dem glauben für vnnd für. Darauff merckent / ein jedlich angenomene weiß / die einer oder eine fürgibt / es sey ein warheit / so gibt dasselbig fürgeben ein solchen starcken glauben / das war wirdt / vnnd bestetet sich. Dann also kommen vil kranckheiten / nicht allein der dantz / sondern auch viel anderley geschlecht / die kein zahl haben. Dann also haben sich etliche fürgeben / sie seyndt besessen / vnnd ist jhnen am letzten / in solchem fürgeben wahr worden : Also auch berümbt in Sant Veltins kranckheit / vnd fielend am letzten darein.
> Also sindt viel kranckheiten entsprungen / die auch täglich nuhn zufallen / ein weg so nuhn kranckheiten kommen / die vor nie gesein sindt. Also auch mit den Frantzosen geschehen ist / do haben sie Sant Dionysium einhergereimpt / … Vnnd auch in der Pestilentz gar nahe auch die gröst vrsach ist / daß das volck in ein verzweifflung falt / es müß sein …

Sudhoff's modernised text of the opening (I/9, 278; read on the page image and in the OCR): "Von der krankheit, die man nennet die Veitstanz. Es ist ein seltsamer ursprung in diser krankheit, etwas geteilt von denen die gemelt seind, und seind also herkomen. die frau Troffea ist die erste gsein in der krankheit, dieselbige nam sich seltsamer laun und weise an; und so sie in ein bleen kam und halsstrittig gegen irem man, so er sie etwas hieß das ir nicht gefiele, so nam sie sich einer weise an, sie wer krank, und erdichtet ein krankheit, die ir auf dismal gelegen was. nun nam sie sich der weise an zum tanzen und gab für sie könte nicht on getanzet sein; dan den man verdruß nichts mer dan das tanzen …" The chapter heading is on p. 278; the text runs to p. 280, where the argument passes to faith as the cause of new diseases in general.

Translation (tr. B):

> **Of the disease which is called St Vitus's dance.** There is a strange origin in this disease, somewhat different from those already mentioned; and it came about thus. The woman Troffea was the first in this disease. She adopted strange moods and manners, and when she fell into a fit of temper [*in ein bleen kam*: the word is obscure] and grew stiff-necked against her husband — if he bade her do something that did not please her — she put on a manner as though she were ill, and invented a sickness that suited her at that moment. Now she took up the manner of dancing and gave out that she could not be without dancing, for nothing vexed the husband more than dancing. And so as to do enough for the business and confirm the semblance of a sickness, she hopped and leapt up, sang and babbled, and [did] whatever displeased the husband most; and when the dance was over she fell down, to the husband's grief, twitched a while and then slept. This she presented as a sickness and concealed that she was thus making a fool of her husband. Thereupon it happened that other women also practised the same manner, and the one taught the other; and it came to the point that the community held such sickness to be a penance [*Buß*] and took from these signs a cause that drove the sickness into them. On this cause faith fastened and fell into the Magor, a heathen spirit; but it did not remain long: then St Vitus became the spirit of the faith, and so had to become an idol here, and thereupon [the disease] received the name St Vitus's dance.
> It then came to the point that everyone fell into such a belief, and thus it was established as a disease; and on those who liked dancing the belief fell and infected them with the same disease, so that it remained together with the belief for ever after. Mark from this: every assumed manner that a man or a woman gives out to be true — that pretence produces so strong a belief that it becomes true and confirms itself. For thus many diseases come, not only the dance but also many other kinds without number. Thus some have given out that they were possessed, and in the end it became true to them in that pretence; and likewise [some] boasted of St Valentine's sickness [epilepsy] and in the end fell into it. Thus many diseases have arisen which still befall daily — a way in which new diseases come that never existed before. So it happened also with the French disease, where they rhymed up St Dionysius … And in the pestilence, too, well-nigh the greatest cause is that the people fall into despair, "it must be so" …

What the passage does and does not contain (each point checked against THEO and the Sudhoff page images):
- **Name form**: "Die Fraw Troffea" (Huser); "die frau Troffea" (Sudhoff). "Fraw"/"frau" = "the woman/wife", not a title in the modern sense; no first name; no husband's name (Waller's "Herr Troffea" is his own extrapolation — snippet: "the long-suffering Herr Troffea 'had asked something of …'").
- **What she did and why**: feigned a dancing sickness out of stubbornness against her husband; hopping, leaping, singing, babbling, collapsing, twitching, sleeping; other women imitated and taught one another; the community read it as a penance; belief made it real and attached it to St Vitus, who "had to become an idol".
- **Dating**: none whatsoever. No "vor vil jaren", no year, no "in my youth", no "when I was in …" (THEO search "jaren" over Huser: no hit in this work's relevant pages; the phrase does not occur in the chapter).
- **Place**: none. Strasbourg is not mentioned anywhere in *De causis morborum invisibilium* (THEO "Straßburg"/"Strasburg": 13 hits in the whole edition, none in this work and none about dancing).
- **Numbers, deaths, pilgrimage, Zabern/Saverne, council, physicians, music, cures**: none.
- **Statement that he was in Strasbourg or saw the dancers**: none. (His Strasbourg residence in 1526–27 is a biographical datum from secondary works — here attested only at second hand: Backman 1952, snippet: "In the year 1526 Paracelsus was in Strassburg [Sticker]"; press kit 2018 p. 10: "le séjour de Paracelse à Strasbourg n'eut lieu qu'à la fin 1526" — repeated by Rue89/Clementz "n'étant venu pour la première fois à Strasbourg qu'en 1526", Pennant-Rea 2018 "visited Strasbourg eight years after the plague" and Waller 2009 "Although he arrived in Strasbourg over seven […]" [snippet cut].)
- **The word "Troffea" elsewhere**: THEO finds no second occurrence in Huser. None of the Strasbourg chronicles or council texts read for this report contains it (Part 3, claim 3).

### 1.3 *De morbis amentium*, Tract. I cap. 3 (Huser IV,51–55 = Sudhoff I/2, 407–412) — origin and classification

Opening (HE4,51):
> Das Dritte Capitel. Von Sanct Veits Tantz / vnd die jhme gleichen. So wollen wir doch hie in diesem Capitel nicht zulegen / daß die Heiligen kranckheiten mögen geben / vnd denselbigen sollen also nachgenennet werden : oder daß ein Plag sey / bedüncket vns es treffe die heilige Geschrifft nit an : als denn viel sind die grosse Theologey darauß setzen / vnd sie mehr Gott zulegen denn der Natur / das ein vnnützes Gesprech ist. Vns mißfelt das geschwetz / hinder welchem kein warzeichen seind / sondern allein Glauben / Glauben / das ein vnmenschlichs ding ist / vnd die Götter auch nichts darauff halten. (N.) So wollen wir den Nammen nit verkehren / von wegen der bekanntnus den Heiligen nach / sondern es ist billich zu nennen Chorea lasciua, auß vrsachen wie hernach folgt … (N.) So ist die vrsach der kranckheit Choreae lasciuae allein ein aestimatz vnd ein angenommen Imaginatz / die da wirckt in dem / der sich also ein solcher aestimatz schetzet / vnd deßgleichen jm selbst ein solche sach imaginirt. Dise aestimatz vnd imaginatz ist der vrsprung dieser Kranckheit vnd des Tantzes. Vnd wiewol ein semlichs auch erfunden wirt in Leuthen in denen kein imaginatz oder aestimatz nit ist / als in den Kindern vnd gewachsenen personen : So ist die vrsach der Kinder auch ein imaginatz / nit auß dem verstandt / sondern auß dem hören / dz er hört vnd sicht …

(tr. B) "The third chapter. Of St Vitus's dance and those like it. We will not concede here in this chapter that the saints can give diseases and that these should be named after them, or that it is a plague; it seems to us this does not concern Holy Scripture, though there are many who build great theology on it and ascribe them more to God than to nature, which is useless talk. We dislike the chatter behind which there are no true signs but only faith, faith, which is an inhuman thing, and the gods too set no store by it. So we will not twist the name for the sake of the acknowledgement of the saints; rather it is properly to be called *Chorea lasciva*, for reasons which follow … So the cause of the disease *chorea lasciva* is only an estimation and an assumed imagination, which works in him who esteems himself such and likewise imagines such a thing to himself. This estimation and imagination is the origin of this disease and of the dance. And although the like is also found in people in whom there is no imagination or estimation, as in children and grown persons, the cause in children is also an imagination, not from the understanding but from hearing, [from] what he hears and sees …"

Two causes (HE4,52): "Nun also seind zwo vrsachen dieser kranckheit des tantzes : Ein natürliche / auß den lachenden Adern / vnnd ein zufellige / auß den gegenbildungen." — "So there are two causes of this disease of the dance: a natural one, from the laughing veins, and an accidental one, from impressions [received through sight and hearing]." The "laughing veins" theory follows (spiritus vitae in the veins sharpened like distilled wine, kindling the blood, tickling, laughter, leaping).

Whores and lute-players (HE4,53–54): "Darumb ist offenbar ein vrsach / das die Huren vnd die Buben / den wol mit Lauten vnd allen Seittenspielen ist / nimmer dauon kommen / vnd allen wollüsten vnnd deß leibs freuden genug thunt / mit jhrem guttem willen / Aestimatz vnd Imaginatz / in solcher gestalt in die kranckheit fallen … Diser Tantz den wir von Huren setzen / ist nicht von Natur / also das die Natur ein vrsach wer … sondern es ist ein zufall auß einem leichten muttwillen / vnd leichtfertigen schandtlichen leben / in dem kein Vernunfft noch Sinnligkeit ist …" — "Hence it is plainly a cause that whores and knaves, who delight in lutes and all string-music and who satisfy all lusts and pleasures of the body, never get away from it, and with their own good will, estimation and imagination fall into the disease in this form … This dance which we posit of whores is not from nature … but is an accident from light wantonness and a frivolous shameful life in which there is neither reason nor sense" (tr. B). Those who do not lead such a life and yet dance "auß vnbedachtem mutt" owe it to the laughing veins (HE4,54).

Closing (HE4,55): "Vnnd wollen also diese kranckheit deß Dantzs beschlossen haben / vnd den Göttern jhren gewalt nicht verachten / die dann wol gewalt haben ein vppigen Menschen zu straffen oder plagen mit solcher kranckheit. Aber das vns das die Natur vnd vrsprung dieser kranckheit nehme oder felsch / das ist nit … Vnd was weiter zu dieser kranckheit Dantz gehört / an seim endt weitter reden werden in capitulo de Cura Choreae lasciuae." — "And so we will have concluded this disease of the dance, not despising the power of the gods, who indeed have power to punish or plague a wanton person with such a disease. But that this should take away or falsify for us the nature and origin of this disease — that is not so … and what further belongs to this disease of the dance we shall discuss at its end in the chapter *de cura choreae lasciuae*" (tr. B).

Note on the threefold scheme. In this origin chapter Paracelsus proposes **one** name (chorea lasciva) and **two** causes (natural: laughing veins; accidental: imagination via sight/hearing, including the wilful case of "whores and knaves"). The labels *vitista* / *chorea aestimativa seu imaginativa*, *chorea lasciva* / *levitas animi* and *chorea coacta* / *naturalis* are introduced in the cure chapter (§1.4), and it is from there that Hecker (1832 p. 18) and everyone after him took the "three kinds".

### 1.4 *De morbis amentium*, Tract. II cap. 3 (Huser IV,79–82 = Sudhoff I/2, 439–442) — the three cures

Division (HE4,79): "… das durch die Schwür Sanct Veitstantz ein solche Imaginatz kommen mag / die introducirt die kranckheit / vnnd also das gemein volck semlichs für ein Plag achtet : das dann allein aegritudo aestimatiua seu imaginatiua ist … Wider solche Vitistam, Aestimatiuam seu Imaginatiuam geben wir ein sonderliche Cur. Darnach so sie auch ein vrsprung auß leichtfertigkeit des Gemüts vnd mit verhengung des Willens / das dann Chorea lasciua oder Leuitas animi geheissen wirt / haben : Setzen wir wider diß Leuitatem animi ein sonderliche andere Cur. Vnd zum dritten contra Choream coactam, der dann sein Vrsprung auß der Natur hat / setzen wir auch dergleichen sein natürlich Medicinam. … Vnd merck da auch ein vnterscheid zwischen Aestimatiua vnd Imaginatiua also / dz eine / auß Zorn / die ander auß Geyle kompt. Darumb wir da Vitistam das Erst heissen / das Ander Lasciuam". — (tr. B) "… that through the oath 'St Vitus's dance' [i.e. cursing someone with it] such an imagination may arise which introduces the disease, so that the common people take it for a plague; which is only an *aegritudo aestimativa seu imaginativa* … Against such a *vitista*, *aestimativa seu imaginativa*, we give a special cure. Next, since they may also have an origin in levity of mind and with the consent of the will — which is called *chorea lasciva* or *levitas animi* — we set against this *levitas animi* another special cure. And thirdly, against the *chorea coacta*, which has its origin in nature, we likewise set its natural medicine. … And mark a difference between *aestimativa* and *imaginativa*: the one comes from anger, the other from lust; hence we call the first *vitista*, the second *lasciva*."

Cure 1, *vitista ex ira* — the wax image (HE4,79–80): "Der Patient soll machen ein Bild jhm gleich von Wachs oder Hartz / vnd soll jhm in sein gedancken nemmen / das alle die Schwür so er gethan hat nach seinem willen in disem Bild sollen verbracht werden / … vnd all sein Gemüt vnd Gedancken diser Schwür ohn einfallung anderer person allein vollkommen in dz Bildt setzen / vnd demnach dasselbig in solchen meristen Gedancken in das Fewr werffen / vnd gar lassen verbrennen / das weder Aschen noch fuligo daruon …" — "The patient shall make an image like himself of wax or resin, and take it into his thoughts that all the oaths he has sworn shall by his will be fulfilled in this image … and set all his mind and thoughts of these oaths, without the intrusion of any other person, wholly into the image, and then in these thoughts throw it into the fire and let it burn entirely, so that neither ash nor soot remains of it" (tr. B). Hecker 1832 p. 20 paraphrases exactly this and quotes the clause "all sein Gemüth und Gedanken dieser Schwür ohn eynfallung anderer Person allein vollkommen in das Bild setzen".

Cure 2, *chorea lasciva* — confinement and fasting (HE4,80–81): "So ein Choreist / Fraw oder Mann / in solchen Choream, auß vil Geyle anhüb zu Tantzen / das mehrer theils in den Frawen geschicht / die do mehr aestimationes vnnd cogitationes haben dann die Mann / vnd auch viel stercker vnnd krefftiger imaginiren … So ist kein bessers / dann das dem wider ist mit den gedancken vnd wercken … Sperr sie ein an ein Finstere / laß fasten zu Wasser vnd Brodt ein gutte zeit / ohn erbarmung an einen vngeschickten orth / durch das wirdt sie wider beweget von dem Hunger in ein ander Natur vnd Gedancken / also das jhr die geyle da entzogen wirdt per abstinentiam … dz Blut setzt sich nider / der Spiritus vitae miltert vnd lasset nach / das Hertz nimpt an sich ein trauren … vnnd wirdt Tristitia ein Meisterin in jhr / in der kein Freüd ist / kein Lachen / kein Tantzen / kein Heülen / etc. … [81] Vnd ist gar ein vnbesints ding / dz man in solchem Tantz nachfolgt / vnd mit jhrem willen / leben vnd nachfolgen in Singen / Tantzen / etc. das alles ein anreitzen ist / vnnd ein fürderung der Kranckheit. Vnd vermeinen etlich / wo solchs nicht geschech / so müstend sie sterben / das nicht also ist / sondern das ist besser / ein guter Stecken genommen vnd wol geschlagen / vnd in die Gefengnuß als vorsteht / gelegt. So ist das herwider zumercken / so sie geschlagen werden / daß sie ein Zorn in jnen empfahen / der sie mag tödten … Wiewol es selten fehlet / es geraht zum bessern / [in] kalt wasser eingeworffen." — (tr. B) "If a choreist, woman or man, began to dance in such a chorea out of much lust — which happens for the most part in women, who have more estimations and cogitations than men and also imagine far more strongly … — there is nothing better than what is contrary to their thoughts and works … Shut her up in a dark place, let her fast on water and bread a good while, without mercy, in an uncomfortable place; through this she will be moved by hunger into another nature and other thoughts, so that lust is withdrawn from her by abstinence … the blood settles, the spirit of life softens and abates, the heart takes on sadness … and *Tristitia* becomes mistress in her, in which there is no joy, no laughing, no dancing, no howling etc. … [81] And it is a very senseless thing to follow along in such a dance and, with their will, to live and follow along in singing, dancing etc., which is all an incitement and a furthering of the disease. ### 2.5 Robert Bartholomew, "Rethinking the Dancing Mania", *Skeptical Inquirer* 24/4 (2000) 42–47 — read in full; Bartholomew & Wessely, *BJP* 2002 — abstract only

Thesis (p. 46): "Based on an examination of a representative sample of medieval chronicles, it is evident that these episodes are best explained as deviant religious sects who gained adherents as they made pilgrimages through Europe during years of turmoil in order to receive divine favor. Their symptoms (visions, fainting, tremor) are predictable for any large population engaging in prolonged dancing, emotional worship, and fasting." Strasbourg appears once, at second hand and with Hecker's wrong date: "A German chronicle reports that during a dance frenzy at Strasbourg in 1418, 'many of them went without food for days and nights' (Rust 1969, 20)." He relies on Backman's translations (1952) and on Hecker; on ergot: "Convulsive ergotism could cause bizarre behavior and hallucinations, but chronic ergotism was more … (Donaldson et al. 1997, 203)". Bartholomew & Wessely 2002 (abstract): a survey of "mass sociogenic illness" from possessed nuns to terrorism fears; nothing specific to 1518 in the abstract. Waller's rebuttal (Psychologist 2009; Discovery 2008): the dancers did not want to dance; contemporaries called it a sickness.

### 3.1 "Fifteen a day were dying"

**1518.** The only contemporary text with the number fifteen is the Augsburg merchant-chronicler Wilhelm Rem (*Cronica newer geschichten*, ed. F. Roth, Chroniken der deutschen Städte 25, 1896, p. 88; image checked): "Wie zů Straspurg vil leut sant Veitz tantz ankam. Anno dni. 1518 im sumer da kam es zů Straspurg fast vil leutt sant Veitz tantz an, daß ain tag wol bei 15 menschen ankam. es weret fast lang, also verbott man das tantzen und pfeiffen und paugkenschlagen." — "How at Strasbourg St Vitus's dance came upon many people. In the year 1518, in summer, St Vitus's dance came upon very many people at Strasbourg, so that on one day it seized a good fifteen people. It lasted very long, so they forbade dancing and piping and drum-beating" (tr. B). *Ankommen* here means "to befall/seize" (cf. the heading "vil leut … ankam"); Rem says nothing about deaths. No Strasbourg document of 1518 (commission of 23 July, letter of 25 July, Brant's *Annales*), and neither Imlin nor Gebwiler, mentions a death (A1, A2; Clementz 2016: "Aucun auteur contemporain n'évoque de décès liés à cette épidémie").

**Conduits.** Martin 1914 p. 114 is the first modern author to quote Rem (OCR read): "Bei einer gelegentlichen Durchsicht der Chronica neuer Geschichten des Nürnberger [sic] Chronisten Wilhelm Rem fiel mir auf, dass dort die Epidemie 1518 datiert ist. Es heisst da: 'Wie zu Straspurg vil leut sant Veitz tantz ankam. Anno dni. 1518 im Summer da kam es zu Straspurg fast vil leutt sant Veitz tantz an, daß ain tag wol bei 15 menschen ankam. es weret fast lang, also verbott man das tantzen und pfeiffen und paugkenschlagen'" — used by Martin to overturn Hecker's date 1418, not as a death toll. Hecker 1832 has no figure of fifteen and no deaths at Strasbourg. Whether Backman 1952 renders Rem, and how, I could not establish (no snippet). Midelfort 1999 pp. 32–49 (read in full): no "fifteen".

**Waller.** *Endeavour* 2008 p. 117: "Seldom pausing to eat, drink or rest, many of them danced for days or even weeks. And before long, the chronicles agree, dozens were dying from exhaustion." — no "fifteen". *Lancet* 373 (21 Feb 2009) 624: "On a far larger scale was the outbreak that struck the city of Strasbourg in 1518, consuming as many as 400 people. One chronicle states that it claimed, for a brief period at least, about 15 lives a day as men, women, and children danced in the punishing summer heat." — the earliest statement retrieved in which Rem's fifteen *cases* a day have become fifteen *deaths* a day; the chronicle is not named. 2009 book (snippet, batch 2): "…chronicle suggests that (at least for a time) fifteen were dying each day as they danced in the punishing summer heat, seldom pausing…" (page not visible). Rem is the only chronicle with "15 … a day", and his verb is *ankam*; the conversion is therefore Waller's (unless Backman's translation already did it — unverified).

**Onward.** Wikipedia: absent 2008–2015; added 8 Apr 2016 by user Czeer (rev 714168375, edit summary "Deaths caused per day"): "One report indicates that for a period the plague killed around around fifteen people per day.<ref name = lancet>" (citing Waller, *Lancet* 2009); since 2021/22 in the section "Veracity of deaths": "Some sources claim that for a period the plague killed around fifteen people per day, but the sources of the city of Strasbourg at the time of the events did not mention the number of deaths, or even if there were fatalities" (citing Clementz 2016). Miller 2017 p. 149: "Eventually, dancers began to die – unable to stop dancing, as many as fifteen people a day simply dropped dead and yet the dance continued on" (no citation attached; her chronicle citations are to Backman). Pennant-Rea, PDR 2018: "At its height, as many as fifteen people were dying each day. The final toll is unknown but, if such a daily death rate was true, could have been into the hundreds" (no note). Rue89 Strasbourg, 18 Oct 2018 (Clementz): no deaths in the sources (Part 2.11).

### 3.2 "400 dancers"

**Sources.** Imlin family chronicle (hand of 1516–43), Reuss 1875 p. 26 (image checked): "und in 4 wuchen wurden ir mehr dan 200 dantzet" — "and within four weeks more than 200 of them danced". Kleine Strassburger Chronik (c. 1600–33; A2): "in vier wochen uber 200". Brant's *Annales* (Extractus no. 3443, Dacheux 1892 p. 239): "uff fünfzig personen". Specklin (c. 1580; Martin p. 119): "über 100 zu Strassburg auff einmal tanzten". Schilter 1698's verse (Hecker 1832 p. 7 n. 1): "Viel hundert fingen zu Straßburg an / Zu tanzen und springen, Frau und Mann". The Basel notice (before 1537; A2): "etliche hundert". Nobody writes 400.

**Where 400 comes from.** Martin 1914 p. 119 prints Imlin from Witkowski (1879) with the reading "vnd in 4 Wochen wurden Ir mehr denn 400 Dantzer" (OCR read; A2 traced the variant to Witkowski). Reuss's 1875 edition "nach der Originalhandschrift" has 200. Midelfort 1999 p. 33 (read): "According to the seventeenth-century chronicle of Oseas Schad, despite efforts to curb the spread of the dancing mania, soon two hundred were afflicted. The Imlin family chronicle claims that within four weeks, more than four hundred were beset with exhausting, uncontrollable dancing" (n. 22: Martin pp. 116, 119; Backman pp. 235–42). Backman 1952 (not read) is quoted by Miller 2017 n. 12 (Backman pp. 236–237) as translating Imlin: "in spite of all this more than four hundred persons were afflicted within four weeks". Hecker 1832 has no number beyond the verse's "Viel hundert".

**Waller.** *Endeavour* 2008 p. 117: "What was it that could have impelled as many as 400 people to dance, in some cases to death?"; "And by the end of August, one chronicler asserts, 400 people had experienced the madness, dancing wildly, uncontrollably around the city [1,2,4,5]" (refs: Backman, Midelfort, Waller's book, Martin). *Lancet* 2009: "consuming as many as 400 people". *Psychologist* 2009: "Chronicles indicate that it then consumed about 400 men, women and children". 2009 book (snippets, batch 3): "…as four hundred people had become infected. Duntzenheim says only two hundred were involved. Even if…" — so Waller knew the 200 reading (he calls Reuss's *Kleine Chronik*/"Pro memoria" the "Duntzenheim chronicle") and preferred 400. Discovery News, 1 Aug 2008 (Viegas, interviewing Waller): "By the end of the week, 34 others had joined her and, within a month, the crowd of dancing, hopping and leaping individuals had swelled to 400."

**Wikipedia.** First revision, 8 Aug 2008 (Pillsberry, rev 230695239): "The crowd swelled to over 400 others by the end of the week." 9 Aug 2008 (Twas Now, rev 230759997): "within a month, there were around 400 dancers" (citing Discovery). Lead "Around 400 people took to dancing" from 2013; "Somewhere between 50 and 400 people" from 2021 (the 50 being the *Annales*' "uff fünfzig"). PDR 2018: "The Imlin family chronicle records that within a month the plague had seized four hundred citizens" (n. 6: Midelfort p. 33).

### 3.3 "Frau Troffea" as first dancer, and "14 July 1518"

**The name.** Paracelsus alone: "Die Fraw Troffea ist die erste gsein in der kranckheit" (Huser 1589, I,263 = Sudhoff I/9, 278; Part 1.2), in a chapter without place-name, year, numbers or deaths; first printed 1565 (Sudhoff's introduction to Bd. 9; the Bischoff/Clementz press kit 2018 p. 10 likewise: "sa première mention n'apparaît qu'en 1565"). No Strasbourg text names the first dancer: Imlin "hub ein frauw an zu dantzen"; Kleine Chronik "hub eine frau ahn zu tantzen"; Gebwiler "one woman" (Midelfort p. 34); the magistrate's letter of 25 July "Ça a commencé avec une femme" (A1 §3.5); *Annales*, Specklin, Wencker, Kleinlawel, Rem — no name. "Troffea" does not occur in Hecker 1832, Martin 1914 (OCR of pp. 113–134, 225–239 searched) or Midelfort 1999 (whole scan searched).

**The date.** Imlin: "Anno 1518 iar, acht tag vor S. Maria Magdalena tag hub ein frauw an zu dantzen" — eight days before 22 July = 14 July (inclusive count); the *Annales* say "um Margarethen" and the Kleine Chronik "umb S. Margredentag" (13 or 20 July; A2). Martin 1914 p. 119 prints Imlin's dating; Midelfort 1999 p. 33 computes it: "The dancing in Strasbourg apparently began with one woman, about July 14, 1518, but within four days, thirty-four men and women were infected." Miller 2017 p. 151: "Imlin's family chronicle account places the beginning of the dancing plague in Strasbourg on 14 July 1518" (her text from Backman pp. 236–37).

**The coupling.** Backman 1952 (snippets from the phrase searches, B_SOURCES §4; page not visible, c. p. 313 per PDR nn. 3 and 10): "In his Opus Paramirium Paracelsus described the origin and cause of St Vitus' dance, i.e., the Strassburg dance. 'Mistress Trophaca [OCR; presumably Troffea] was the first to suffer from this disease…'"; "In the year 1526 Paracelsus was in Strassburg [Sticker]. As we may remember, in 1518 … excited much surprise and terror. It is very probable that Paracelsus shared that experience." So the identification of Paracelsus's woman with the first Strasbourg dancer is at least as old as Backman, who presents it as "very probable", not as fact. Waller 2009 book (snippets, batches 1–3): chapter "FRAU TROFFEA'S DANCE"; "It was a week before the holy festival of Mary Magdalene, on July 14, 1518, that Frau Troffea began her dance"; "sometime in late July, just a week or more after Frau Troffea had started to dance, the epidemic had taken…"; and, from the husband of the parable, a "Herr Troffea" ("the long-suffering Herr Troffea 'had asked something of her which she did not…'"; "Frau Troffea had an unhappy relationship with Herr Troffea") — Paracelsus gives the husband no name. *Endeavour* 2008 does not use the name ("a lone woman", "the lone woman"); Discovery News, 1 Aug 2008, does: "In July of 1518, a woman referred to as Frau Troffea stepped into a narrow street in Strasbourg, France and began a fervent dancing vigil that lasted between four and six days."

**Onward.** Wikipedia 9 Aug 2008 (rev 230759997): "The outbreak began in July 1518, when a woman, Frau Troffea, began to dance fervently in a street in Strasbourg" (citing Discovery) — "Mrs. Troffea" in 2013–17 (with vandalism: "Madonna Louise Veronica Ciccone" in the 3 Mar 2013 snapshot, "Frau Lee" in the 5 Jan 2020 snapshot). Miller 2017 p. 151: "Four of the six chronicle accounts also make this assertion that a solitary woman by the name of Lady Troffea began the plague" (n. 13: Backman p. 237) — wrong on the evidence read here: no chronicle names her. Wikipedia (2022– ) copies Miller: "Of the six chronicle accounts, four support Lady Troffea as the first dancer." PDR 2018: "According to his Opus Paramirum, and various chronicles agree, it all started with one woman. Frau Troffea had started dancing on July 14th on the narrow cobbled street outside her half-timbered home … a ploy to embarrass Herr Troffea" (nn. 3, 10: Backman p. 313); the current Wikipedia lead quotes this sentence. The 2018 exhibition (press kit p. 10, Bischoff/Clementz): "Frau Trofféa n'a jamais existé … est restée anonyme dans le seul document authentique … Son patronyme est une invention pédante de Paracelse (1493-1541), à partir du substantif grec tropaia"; Clementz 2016: "un nom fort improbable". Two further OL findings: a modern German *Werke* edition (scan `werke0000para`) reads "Frau Troffea ist die erste in dieser Krankheit gewesen"; the name has since spread through popular books (OL full-text search: 81 scanned books contain "Troffea", among them *Get Well Soon*, *The Rhine*, History Channel and "unexplained" compendia — none earlier than Waller as far as the hit list shows).

### 3.4 "Within a week 34 / within a month 400"

**Source.** Imlin (image checked): the first woman danced "woll 6 tag"; while she was on the way to Saverne "fingen noch mehr an zu dantzen, bey dem Herrenstall also daß in 4 tagen bey 34 frauwen und man waren" — "so that within four days there were about 34 women and men"; "in 4 wuchen … mehr dan 200". The Kleine Chronik has the same 34 in four days and ">200" in four weeks (A2). Brant's *Annales*: fifty. Nothing says "within a week" or "within a month … 400".

**Chain.** Martin 1914 p. 119 (Imlin with 400) → Backman pp. 236–37 (per Miller n. 12: "thirty-four men and women were involved within four days … more than four hundred persons were afflicted within four weeks") → Midelfort p. 33 ("within four days, thirty-four … within four weeks, more than four hundred"). Waller *Endeavour* p. 117: "Some time in mid-July 1518 a lone woman stepped into one of its narrow streets and began a dancing vigil that was to last four or even 6 days in succession. Within a week another 34 had joined the dance. And by the end of August, one chronicler asserts, 400 people had experienced the madness" — "within a week" for Imlin's four days (after the first woman's six), "end of August" for Imlin's four weeks (which run from 14 July to about 11 August). 2009 book (snippet): "According to the Imlin'sche and Duntzenheim chronicles, by about July 21 as many as thirty-four…". Discovery News 2008: "By the end of the week, 34 others had joined her and, within a month … 400". Wikipedia 9 Aug 2008: "This lasted somewhere between four to six days. Within a week, 34 others had joined, and within a month, there were around 400 dancers" — unchanged until 2020; current text: "Soon, three dozen others joined in … By August, the 'dancing plague' afflicted between 50 and 400 people" (citing History.com 2015). PDR 2018: "within days more than thirty choreomaniacs were in motion"; "within a month … four hundred".

### 3.5 Wooden stage(s), hired musicians, "strong men", guildhalls

**Sources.** Guildhalls — Imlin (image): "Da verbotten meine herren die drummeln und pfiffer, und fürt ein theil uff die gerwerstub, das ander uff die zimmerleut stub" — "Then my lords forbade the drums and pipers, and led one part to the tanners' hall, the other to the carpenters' hall" (i.e. the council *suppressed* the music). Gebwiler (early 1520s, Stenzel 1926 pp. 74–75, in Midelfort's translation p. 34): he "had seen one woman dance for six days in a row, 'and strong men had to replace one another as guards in the job of protecting her from hurting herself'"; "the Council of Twenty-One ordered two guildhalls to be set aside for the dancers and for them to be provided with guards … 'they danced day and night with those poor people, but toward evening they tied them all onto wagons and took them to St. Vitus of Hohlenstein'" — the "strong men" are guards of the first woman, and the dancing "with those poor people" is Gebwiler's phrase for the guards. Stages, paid dancers and drums appear only in Specklin (c. 1580; Martin 1914 p. 119): "Da gab man in etliche zunftstuben ein, auch auff dem Ross- und Kornmarckt macht man gerüst und bestellte eigene leutt umb lohn, die mussten stets mit ihnen, tanzten mit trummen und pfeiffen; es half alles nichts" — "They gave them some guildhalls, and on the horse- and grain-market they made scaffolds and engaged special people for pay, who had to be with them constantly; they danced with drums and pipes; it all helped nothing" (tr. B); and in Wencker's chronicle (Dacheux 1892 no. 3007): "das man ihnen zu Strassburg der Zimmerleut- und Gerberstub gab, und sondere leute bestellet die genug mit ihnen tantzten … Auf dem Rossmarck schlug man ein gerüst auf, liess sie bey trummel und pfeiffen tantzen, half aber alles nicht". The 1518 records point the other way: the physicians allowed only string music (letter of 25 July; A1), the council ordered on "5a Mariae Magdalenae" that drums be stopped and "heimlich seitenspiel" used (Extractus, Dacheux p. 240; Midelfort p. 35 n. 28), and the *Annales* record "kein brucken/beucken schlagen". Platter's Basel woman of the 1540s (Observationes 1614; Midelfort pp. 37–38) danced "with specially chosen guards ('strong men'), dressed in red, who took turns for almost a full month".

**Conduits.** Hecker 1832 p. 7 gives Strasbourg dancers "begleitet von aufspielenden Sackpfeifern" (bagpipers — from his 1374 material, not from any Strasbourg source read here) and, in his general account of the later sixteenth century (Babington 1859 p. 96, after Schenck and Plater): "the magistrates hired musicians for the purpose of carrying the St. Vitus's dancers so much the quicker through the attacks, and directed, that athletic men should be sent among them in order to complete the exhaustion"; Babington's note adds Burton's "magistrates in Germany will hire musicians to play to them, and some lusty, sturdy companions to dance with them". Midelfort p. 35 translates Specklin ("set up platforms in the horse market and in the grain market and paid people to stay with them and to dance with them, [accompanied] by fife and drum") and p. 37: "the city's Council of Twenty-One ordered fife and drum, guards, and special dance stages. Within two weeks, however, it appeared that music was actually spreading the disorder".

**Waller.** *Endeavour* p. 117: "Instead they prescribed 'more dancing'. To this end they cleared two guildhalls and the outdoor grain market and they even had a wooden stage constructed opposite the horse fair … the authorities next paid for musicians and professional dancers to keep the afflicted moving. Every time the sick flagged, fainted, stumbled or slowed, the musicians raised the tempo of their playing and hired dancers held them firm and quickened their pace. 'They danced day and night with those poor people', one eye-witness recalled [1,2,4]" — Gebwiler's guards have become hired dancers, and "raised the tempo" has no source. *Lancet*: "they constructed a special stage in the heart of the city … They even hired professional dancers and musicians to keep them in constant motion." 2009 book (snippets): "…florins to pay, as the chronicles of Specklin and Wencker both relate, for robust men to…" (p. 125 visible). Wikipedia 15 Jan 2009 (Intothewoods29, rev 264197100): "authorities encouraged more dancing, in part by opening two guildhalls and a grain market, and even constructing a wooden stage" (from *Endeavour*). PDR 2018: "set up platforms in the horse market and in the grain market" (n. 5: Midelfort p. 35), then embellished: "dozens of musicians were paid to play drums, fiddles, pipes, and horns, with healthy dancers brought in for further encouragement". Current Wikipedia: "musicians and strong people to help keep those dealing with the dancing mania to stay upright" (citing PDR, which does not say "strong").

### 3.6 The physicians' "hot blood" and the advice to let them dance

**Sources.** The magistrate's letter to Bishop Wilhelm, 25 July 1518 (ADBR 12 J 1495; only the exhibition's French translation retrieved, dossier pp. 11–12): "Ça a commencé avec une femme, puis avec d'autres personnes qui se sont mises à danser. Nous avons gardé ces personnes quelques jours et interrogé les médecins, qui nous ont dit qu'il s'agissait d'une maladie naturelle due à une conjonction astrale et à la chaleur du moment. Elle touche néanmoins à sa fin" — a *natural* illness from an astral conjunction and the heat; the physicians' regimen (same dossier): no wine, barley water, and music only with "cithares, violons, luths ou orgues positifs". Wencker's Extractus fol. 171 as summarised by Jung (Dacheux, BSCMHA XIX, 1899, p. 42 no. 4398; image checked by A1): "die Aerzte erklärten es für eine natürliche krankheit, die von hitzigem geblüt herkomme" — Jung's résumé, not the protocol's words; the only verbatim words are the vicar's: "diwyl die Artz anzeigen, daß es eine natürlich krankheit sy, daß man auch natürlich mittel mit in versuche" (Martin 1914 p. 119; A1 §3.2). No source says the physicians advised dancing; the council's therapeutic dancing is Specklin's/Wencker's report (3.5).

**Conduits.** Martin 1914 p. 119 prints Jung's résumé and the vicar's reply. Midelfort p. 34: "physicians testified to the Council of the Twenty-One that the disease was 'a natural sickness that came from hot blood.' When the afflicted dancers petitioned for a Mass … the council consulted with the bishop's vicar, who replied 'that this seemed quite unnecessary to him, seeing that the physicians had declared that it was a natural sickness…'" (n. 25: Dacheux, *Fragments des anciennes chroniques d'Alsace* 4, 1901, p. 252; Martin pp. 118–19). Hecker: nothing of this.

**Waller.** *Endeavour* p. 117: "As the dance turned epidemic, troubled nobles and burghers consulted local physicians. Having excluded astrological and supernatural causes, the members of the medical fraternity declared it to be a 'natural disease' caused by 'hot blood' [2,4,5]. This was orthodox physic, consistent with Galen's view that bloody fluxes could overheat the brain … But the response of the authorities was neither to bleed nor to provide cooling diets. Instead they prescribed 'more dancing'." The 25 July letter contradicts "excluded astrological … causes". Wikipedia 15 Jan 2009: "concerned nobles sought the advice of local physicians, who ruled out astrological and supernatural causes, instead announcing that the plague was a 'natural disease' caused by 'hot blood'. However, instead of prescribing bleeding, authorities encouraged more dancing" — a paraphrase of *Endeavour*, kept until 2020; the current article has "Contemporaneous explanations included demonic possession, divine judgement and overheated blood" (citing Miller). PDR 2018: "the councillors listened instead to the guild of physicians, declaring the dance to be 'a natural disease, which comes from overheated blood'" (n. 4: "Annales de Sebastian Brant (1899), quoted in Waller 2009 p. 109" — i.e. Jung's résumé); "But the physicians instead recommended the treatment given to past victims … They must dance themselves free of it" — no source.

### 3.7 The ban on music and dancing until St Michael's day, with the wedding exception

**Sources.** Brant's *Annales* (Wencker's Extractus no. 3443; Dacheux 1892 p. 239, OCR read; A1 image): "Da setzten unsere herren uff und lissen ein gebot üssgon, dass niemand tantzen soll bis Michaelis in der ganzen Statt und burgban, bei 30 sch. pf. und kein brucken schlagen; wol möchte man bey brautläuften und ersten messen mit Saitenspiel tantzen nach eines jeden conscienz" — "Then our lords resolved and issued an order that nobody shall dance until Michaelmas in the whole city and its jurisdiction, on pain of 30 shillings, and no drum-beating; but at weddings and first masses one might dance to string music, according to each one's conscience" (tr. B; Martin p. 116 prints "beucken [Pauken] schlagen"). The original leaf, signed by Brant, is AVES 1 MR 3 f. 72r (shown in 2018 as "Interdiction des danses publiques … 2 août 1518"; Midelfort p. 35 n. 27 dates the same entry "On the second day after Vincula Petri [i.e., on August 3] anno 18" and translates it in full: "…forbade, on pain of a fine of 30 shillings, that anyone, no matter who, should hold a dance until St. Michael's Day [September 29] in this city or its suburbs or in its whole jurisdiction. For by so doing they take away the recovery of such persons. The only exception is that if honorable persons wish to dance at weddings or celebrations of first Mass in their houses, they may do so using stringed instruments, but they are on their conscience not to use tambourines and drums"). Imlin and Rem confirm the ban on drums and pipers.

**Chain.** Not in Hecker 1832 (whose Strasbourg page knows only the pilgrimage). Martin 1914 p. 116; Midelfort p. 35; Backman: unknown (no snippet). Waller: not in *Endeavour* or *Lancet*; the 2009 book — no snippet retrieved ("weddings" query failed). PDR 2018: "prohibiting almost all dance and music in the city until September … Sebastian Brant … detailed the exception to the ban: 'if honourable persons wish to dance at weddings…'" (n. 8: AMS R3 fol. 72r, quoted in Midelfort p. 35). Wikipedia: no revision read (2008–2026) mentions Michaelmas or the wedding exception.

### 3.8 Pilgrimage to St Vitus at Saverne; red shoes, small crosses, holy water

**Sources.** Pilgrimage: council commission of Friday 23 July 1518 with the *Instruction* for the escorts (AVES III 200/15; Martin 1914 pp. 117, 122–24 — "Nach jedem Amt sollten die armen Leute um den Altar geführt werden und jeder Kranke 1 Pfennig opfern"), Imlin ("da leiß sey meine herren nach S. Vit gehn Zabern füren … an dem andern tag da leiß man sey alle nach S. Vit füren"), the *Annales* ("zu dem lieben heil. St. Vit im Hohenstein bei Zabern geführt und fast erledigt"), Gebwiler ("tied them all onto wagons and took them to St. Vitus of Hohlenstein"); the wax image "ein centner schwer" in Jung's résumé no. 4398. Red shoes, crosses, chrism and holy water are Specklin's (c. 1580; Martin p. 119): "Do schickte man sie hinder Zabern zu St. Veit, zum holen stein, auf waegen; da gab man ihnen creuzle und rothe schuh, und macht mess über sie. An den schuhen war unten und oben creutz mit dem chrisam gemacht und mit weywasser besprengt in St. Veits nahmen, das halff ihn vast allen" — "Then they sent them on wagons beyond Saverne to St Vitus at the Hohlenstein; there they gave them little crosses and red shoes and said mass over them. On the shoes, below and above, a cross was made with chrism and sprinkled with holy water in St Vitus's name; that helped almost all of them" (tr. B); repeated by Wencker (no. 3007: "creuzlein und rothe schue daran oben und unden † mit chrisam gestrichen und mit weyhewasser in S. Viti nammen besprengt"). "Small crosses" = *creuzle/creuzlein*.

**Conduits.** Hecker 1832 pp. 7–8 (from Schilter): "So wurden sie denn zu Fuß und zu Wagen zu den Kapellen des heiligen Veit nach Zabern und Rotestein geleitet, wo ihrer Priester warteten … Nach vollbrachtem Gottesdienst führte man sie in feierlichem Umzuge um den Altar, ließ sie von ihren Almosen ein Geringes opfern" — no shoes; "Rotestein" is Schilter's misreading of "Holenstein" (Martin p. 117: "Schilter hat falsch gelesen, es steht im Original Holenstein … Hecker und Häser schreiben sogar zu den Kapellen des heiligen Veit zu Zabern und Rotenstein"). Backman 1952 pp. 235–42 translates Specklin and the *Annales* (snippets: "Then they were sent to St Vitus, to Hohlenstein, quite near Zabern. There they were given…"; "crosses were drawn in consecrated oil and in the name of St Vitus they were sprinkled"; "taken to the shrine of the holy St Vitus at Hohlenstein near Zabern, where they were almost cured") and describes the grotto chapel with its group of saints including "Pope Marcellus" (snippets). Midelfort p. 36 (Specklin in translation: "given a little cross and red shoes, on which the sign of the cross had been made in holy oil, on both the tops and the soles. In St. Vitus' name they were sprinkled with holy water") and p. 37 on the Vixberg grotto.

**Waller and after.** *Endeavour* p. 117: "the dancers were despatched to a mountaintop shrine in the Vosges mountains to pray for divine intercession. There they were led around an altar, wearing red shoes provided for the ceremony, upon which stood a bas-relief carving of St. Vitus, the Virgin and Pope Marcellus" — "around an altar" is the council's *Instruction* (via Hecker/Martin), the red shoes Specklin's, the carving Backman's description of the chapel. PDR 2018: "bundled into wagons and taken the three-day ride to the shrine of Saint Vitus, where Frau Troffea had been cured. Priests placed the choreomaniacs … underneath a wooden carving of Vitus. They put small crosses in their hands and red shoes on their feet. On the soles and tops of these shoes, they sprinkled holy water and painted crosses of consecrated oil" (n. 9: Specklin in Martin p. 121 via Midelfort p. 36). Wikipedia: the pilgrimage and red shoes enter only in the 2024 snapshot (from PDR).

### 3.9 "It ended in early September" / duration

**Sources.** The dated record: 22 July (drums to be stopped), 23 July (commission; first convoys to Saverne), 25 July (letter: "Elle touche néanmoins à sa fin" — "it is nevertheless coming to its end"), 2/3 August (dance ban until 29 September), 10 August (guilds to send their sick to St Vitus; Midelfort p. 36 n. 29, Martin p. 121) and further orders in mid/late August (A1, A2). Imlin and the Kleine Chronik count "in 4 wuchen"; Rem "es weret fast lang"; no text gives an end date.

**Modern.** Hecker: no dates. Midelfort p. 37: "Within two weeks, however, it appeared that music was actually spreading the disorder". *Endeavour*: "by the end of August, one chronicler asserts, 400 people…"; "In the following weeks the epidemic abated." 2009 book (snippet, batch 2): "Only in late August or early September 1518 did the epidemic finally subside, leaving many people…". PDR 2018: "from mid-July to late August or early September". Wikipedia: "over the period of about one month" (2008–2020); current: "from July 1518 to September 1518".

### 3.10 Deaths "from heart attack, stroke or exhaustion"

**Sources.** No 1518 text mentions a death (commission, letter, *Annales*, Imlin, Gebwiler, Rem). The earliest death notices are two or more generations later: Specklin (c. 1580; Martin p. 119): "Viel tanzten sich zu tode"; the Kleine Chronik (c. 1600–33; A2): "biss etliche starben, erlagen oder miedt wurden"; Wencker (1637; no. 3007): "Etliche stürben dran"; Kleinlawel 1625 (Martin p. 116; Midelfort p. 48 n. 77): "Biß das sie fielen in ohnmacht, / Viel sind Todt drüber blieben"; the 1636 astrological chronicle (Backman p. 238 via PDR n. 2): "until finally they fell down unconscious and succumbed to death". Hecker's verse (Schilter): "Bis ihn das Wüthen wieder gelag" — the raging subsided; no deaths.

**Modern.** Hecker 1832: none for Strasbourg. Midelfort p. 33: "many danced until they fell to the ground unconscious, and some never recovered"; p. 35: "Many persons danced themselves to death." *Endeavour* p. 117: "before long, the chronicles agree, dozens were dying from exhaustion"; "Only after those with weak hearts or prone to strokes began to die did the governors rethink their strategy" — the cardiological gloss is Waller's, without a source. Discovery News 2008: "by summer's end, dozens in the Alsatian city had died of heart attacks, strokes and sheer exhaustion due to nonstop dancing". Wikipedia 8 Aug 2008: "Most of them died due to strokes, heart attacks and exhaustion due to nonstop 'jigging'"; 9 Aug 2008 (rev 230759997): "over the period of about one month, most of the people died from heart attack, stroke, or exhaustion" (citing Discovery, which says "dozens"); "most" became "some" between the 5 Jan 2010 and 4 Jan 2011 snapshots (an anonymous edit of 29 Dec 2010, rev 404843287, has the summary "Need citation if going to claim most or many died" — the diff itself was not retrieved); 2021: "Controversy exists over whether people ultimately danced to their deaths"; from 2022 the section "Veracity of deaths" citing Clementz 2016. PDR 2018: "the most fatal and best documented" of the dancing plagues.

### 3.11 What this shows

1. None of the ten claims is in Hecker 1832, who dates the Strasbourg dance to 1418 (Schilter's misprint, exposed by Martin 1914 pp. 114–15), knows no number but "Viel hundert", no deaths, no Troffea and no stage; the modern narrative is not "Hecker's".
2. Four claims have a genuine sixteenth-century base but in a different form: fifteen *cases* a day (Rem), 200 dancers (Imlin), 34 in four days (Imlin), the pilgrimage with red shoes (Specklin, two generations later). Two are seventeenth-century (Michaelmas ban as recorded in the *Annales* is contemporary in substance; deaths are not).
3. The transformations are located: 200 → 400 (Witkowski/Martin, 1879/1914); Paracelsus's parable → the first Strasbourg dancer (Backman 1952, "very probable"; Waller 2008–09, as fact, with a date and a husband); "seized" → "dying" (Waller 2009); guards → hired dancers, "excluded astrological causes", "heart attack/stroke" (Waller 2008); "dozens died" → "most died" (Wikipedia 2008–10).
4. Wikipedia's article was built (8–9 Aug 2008) from a single press article about Waller's forthcoming book, then (Jan 2009) from *Endeavour*, then (2016) from the *Lancet*, then (2022–24) from Pennant-Rea 2018 and Miller 2017; the corrective material (Clementz 2016) arrived in 2021–22 but the lead still carries "Frau Troffea".

## 4. Open questions and what could not be done

1. **Backman 1952 was not read.** The archive.org copy (`religiousdancesi0000back`) is lending-only; search-inside on archive.org returned "Item not available"; the Open Library search-inside API gave c. 60 fragments over four batches and a dozen ad-hoc phrase searches (B_SOURCES §4). Established from fragments only: Backman translates Kleinlawel's verse, Specklin, the *Annales* and Paracelsus's Troffea chapter; he identifies Paracelsus's St Vitus dance with "the Strassburg dance", dates Paracelsus's Strasbourg stay to 1526 "[Sticker]" and calls it "very probable" that Paracelsus "shared that experience"; the name after "Mistress" is OCR'd "Trophaca". Not established: how Backman prints the name, whether he uses Rem's "15", what he says about deaths, and his page numbers (PDR: pp. 237, 238, 313; Miller: pp. 236–38; Midelfort: pp. 235–42, 238–42, 303–27). Any judgement on Backman's ergot thesis in this report rests on Midelfort's and Waller's summaries, not on Backman.
2. **Waller's book was not read.** *A Time to Dance, a Time to Die* (Icon 2008) / *The Dancing Plague* (Sourcebooks 2009): lending-only on archive.org; c. 130 snippet fragments from the 2009 edition (batches 1–4). His three articles (2008–09) were read in full. What his book cites for "fifteen dying a day" and for Troffea's "July 14" (the snippet shows a note mark "*?" after that sentence) is unknown.
3. **Midelfort 1999** was read for the whole section "A Dancing Mania" (pp. 32–49) — from the text of an unofficial archive.org upload saved in `midelfort-a2/`; the provenance is flagged. The rest of the book was not read.
4. **Not obtained:** Rohmann 2013 (*Tanzwut*, V&R — JS challenge page) and Rohmann 2009 (*Medieval History Journal* — SAGE challenge page); Gotman 2018 (*Choreomania*, OUP — Google Books 403; only the publisher's blurb); Clementz 2016 (*Revue d'Alsace* 142 — OpenEdition served a bot-check to curl; a page fetch yielded quotations only) and her 2018 catalogue chapter ("La danse de 1518 à l'épreuve des sources", *1518. (b) Whether the 1565 Birckmann print already has the name "Troffea" (Sudhoff says Huser's text differs "sehr erheblich" from the Erstdruck) is unverified — Sudhoff's apparatus for p. 278 was not checked line by line. (c) The Greek etymology proposed in the 2018 press kit (*tropaia*) is that author's conjecture; I found no evidence either way. 8. **Not done on purpose:** no attempt to bypass OpenEdition's bot-check, the SAGE/V&R challenge pages, Google Books' 403, or the archive.org lending wall; no use of the Libgen upload of Midelfort (see 3 for the copy I did use).
