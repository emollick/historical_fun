# C. The classical (Greek and Latin) testimonia for the "Hanging Garden(s) of Babylon": texts, translations, source criticism

Working copies of the raw fetched pages (`raw_C/`, file names given in square brackets after each URL) and of the plain-text extractions (`txt_C/`) are not included in this folder. ## 0. Summary of findings

1. **There are only five substantive ancient descriptions** of the garden: Berossus-in-Josephus (with parallels in Syncellus and the Armenian Eusebius), Abydenus-in-Eusebius (one sentence), Diodorus 2.10, Strabo 16.1.5, Curtius 5.1.31-35, and the late-antique Philo "of Byzantium". Everything else is a bare listing (Antipater, Gregory of Nazianzus, Pliny 19.49) or silence.
2. **Nobody except Berossus (as transmitted) and Abydenus/Megasthenes names Nebuchadnezzar.** Diodorus and Curtius attribute the garden to "a later **Syrian** king" (*τινος ὕστερον Σύρου βασιλέως*; *Syriae regem Babylone regnantem*), explicitly *not* Semiramis; Pliny 19.49 hedges "*sive illos Semiramis sive Assyriae rex Syrus fecit*"; Strabo, Philo and Antipater name no builder at all. "Syrian" in this idiom = Assyrian (Strabo 16.1.2 says so in terms; Rolfe's Loeb note "Syria for Assyria").
3. **The Berossus sentence is textually genuine in Josephus** (both AJ 10.226 and Ap. 1.141, and Syncellus p. 418 Dindorf reproduce it), **but its authenticity as Berossus is disputed**, because the rest of Josephus' Berossus paragraph tracks Nebuchadnezzar's own "East India House" inscription item by item and the garden is the one item with no cuneiform counterpart (van der Spek 2008; Dalley 1994/2013; contra Bichler 2004, Burstein 1978, Reade 2000, who accept it as Berossus "catering to Greek readers"). Eusebius, quoting Josephus in PE 9.40, *skips* the garden sentence, but quotes Abydenus' independent one-liner in PE 9.41.
4. **Diagnostics from the texts:** the two Alexander-derived accounts (Diodorus/Curtius, generally traced to Clitarchus) locate the garden *παρὰ τὴν ἀκρόπολιν* / *super arcem* in Babylon; Strabo puts it *ἐπὶ τῷ ποταμῷ* and has the water raised from the **Euphrates** by **screws** (*κοχλίαι*) worked by men; Philo has water forced up "**screw-wise**" (*κοχλιοειδῶς*) through "**the spiral of the machines**" (*τὸν ἕλικα τῶν μηχανημάτων*). Diodorus, by contrast, says only "*ὄργανα*" for the "*ἐπαντλήσεις*" of water and "*διατομαί*" (shafts/openings), which Dalley & Oleson themselves concede "might imply a vertical lift by means of ropes, rather than ... water screws" (2003: 12). No text mentions bronze; the waterproofing recipe (reed + bitumen + double baked brick in gypsum + lead) is only in Diodorus.
5. **The Greek tradition confused Assyria and Babylonia systematically:** Diodorus 2.3.2 (from Ctesias) founds Nineveh "*παρὰ τὸν Εὐφράτην ποταμόν*"; Herodotus 1.178 makes Babylon the royal seat of "Assyria" after Nineveh's fall; Xenophon calls Nimrud and Nineveh "Larisa" and "Mespila", "once inhabited by Medes"; Strabo 16.1.2 says "the Syrians" of the historians "are none other than those who set up the royal seat at Babylon and Ninus". This is the *only* real support the texts give to a Nineveh relocation: the texts themselves, without exception, say Babylon and the Euphrates.
6. **Wonder-list chronology:** the earliest surviving list with the garden is Antipater of Sidon (2nd/1st c. BC, AP 9.58: *κάπων τ᾽ αἰώρημα*); the Berlin papyrus *Laterculi Alexandrini* (late 2nd c. BC) preserves only Artemision, pyramids, Mausoleum; Diodorus 2.11.5 already knows a canon of "seven" (Semiramis' obelisk), Strabo 16.1.5 counts wall + garden among the seven, Philo (4th/5th c. AD) devotes ch. 1 to the garden, Gregory of Nazianzus (AP 8.177) includes *κῆποι*; Latin lists (Hyginus 223, Ampelius 8, Cassiodorus Var. 7.15.4, Gregory of Tours, ps.-Bede) all have the **wall** of Babylon and never the garden. The canon is a Hellenistic (Alexandrian) construct; Callimachus' *Collection of wonders* is lost.

Thackeray: "Within this palace he erected lofty stone terraces, in which he closely reproduced mountain scenery, completing the resemblance by planting them with all manner of trees and constructing the so-called hanging garden; because his wife, having been brought up in Media, had a passion for mountain surroundings." Thackeray's note: "Regarded as one of the seven wonders of the ancient world. A fuller account is quoted from Ctesias in Diodorus ii.10." His apparatus records "ὑπὲρ ὧν Syncellus" for the corrupt "ὧν … ἀνάστημα".

Observations on the two Josephan versions: AJ has *ἀναλήμματα λίθινα* ("stone terraces/retaining walls"), *Ap.* adds *ὑψηλά* ("lofty"); AJ has *τῆς οἰκείας διαθέσεως* ("her native surroundings"), *Ap.* *τῆς ὀρείας διαθέσεως* ("mountain surroundings"); the wife is unnamed in both. The key word *κρεμαστός* ("hung up, suspended") is what Latin renders *pensilis*.

**Parallel transmissions of the same Berossus sentence.**

*Syncellus, Ecloga chronographica* p. 418 (Dindorf, CSHB 1829, vol. 1) — fetched from the archive.org OCR of Dindorf's edition, https://archive.org/download/georgiussyncell01nikgoog/georgiussyncell01nikgoog_djvu.txt [raw_C/syncellus_dindorf1_djvu.txt, ll. 26410-26420; OCR, lightly normalised]: "…συνετελέσθη ἡμέραις ιε΄. ἐν δὲ τοῖς βασιλείοις τούτοις ἀναλήμματα λίθινα ὑψηλὰ ἀνοικοδομήσας καὶ τὴν ὄψιν ἀποδοὺς ὁμοιοτάτην τοῖς ὄρεσι, καταφυτεύσας δένδρεσι παντοδαποῖς, ἐξειργάσατο· καὶ κατεσκεύασε τὸν καλούμενον κρεμαστὸν παράδεισον, διὰ τὸ τὴν γυναῖκα αὐτοῦ ἐπιθυμεῖν τῆς ὀρείας διαθέσεως, τεθραμμένην ἐν τοῖς κατὰ Μηδίαν τόποις. ταῦτα ὁ Βήρωσσος ἱστορεῖ περὶ τοῦ Ναβουχοδονόσωρ καὶ ἕτερα πλεῖστα ἐν τρίτῃ τῶν Χαλδαϊκῶν βίβλῳ, μεμφόμενος τοῖς ψευδῶς γράψασιν Ἑλλήνων ἱστορικοῖς ὑπὸ τῆς Ἀσσυρίας Σεμιράμεως κτισθῆναι Βαβυλῶνα καὶ τὰ κατ᾽ αὐτὴν θαυμάσια." Syncellus therefore had the garden sentence in his copy of Josephus/Polyhistor (with the *Contra Apionem* wording).

*Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica* 9.40 quotes Josephus *Ap.* 1.136-138 and then jumps ("After other statements he says") to 1.146-153: the garden sentence is **omitted** (Gifford's translation, https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/eusebius_pe_09_book9.htm [raw_C/eusebius_pe9_gifford.html]; the Greek of Gifford's 1903 edition Tomus I at https://archive.org/download/evangelicaepraep01euse/evangelicaepraep01euse_djvu.txt [raw_C/evangelicaepraep01euse_djvu.txt] confirms this). This is abbreviation, not evidence that Eusebius' Josephus lacked the sentence.

*Eusebius, Chronicle* (Armenian version), from Alexander Polyhistor's epitome of Berossus, tr. R. Bedrosian, https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/eusebius_chronicon_02_text.htm [raw_C/eusebius_chron_armenian_bedrosian.html]: "…he constructed yet another palace near his father's palace [g68] whose size, beauty, and adornment one can hardly describe. Suffice it to say that it was a splendidly rare accomplishment, completely finished in fifteen days. The palace had a lofty turreted portion at the summit, constructed in such a manner as to resemble mountains and planted with a great variety of trees. This was named the Hanging Garden and it was created to satisfy the longing of his wife for the airy mountainous places where she was [g69] raised, in the high mountains of Media. This is what [Berosus] says about the king." — This is Eusebius' excerpt of *Josephus* (the chapter is headed "From Josephus' Antiquities about Nebuchadnezzar"), so it is not an independent witness to Polyhistor's Berossus.

**The Median wife's name.** Josephus never names her. The name comes from Polyhistor's Berossus in the *Chronicle* (Bedrosian, p. [g44]): "Sardanapallus ruled the Chaldeans for 21 years. He sent an auxiliary army to the patriarch and lord of the Medes, Azhdahak, to secure one of his daughters, **Amuhean**, as a wife for his son Nebuchadnezzar", and from Abydenus ([g54]): "[Busalossorus] plotted rebellion and sought the marriage of Amuhean, daughter of Azhdahak [Astyages], the patriarch of the Medes, to his son, Nebuchadnezzar." "Amuhean" is universally taken as the Armenian deformation of Ἄμυτις. Note that Ctesias' Amytis (FGrH 688 F 9) is Astyages' daughter married to **Cyrus** — Bichler 2004: 19 (achemenet PDF, see §2.4) suspects "Ctesias' Amytis found her way into Berossus' fragments". Oldfather's Loeb note on Diod. 2.10 prints the name as "Amyhia".

### 1a′. Abydenus (BNJ/FGrH 685 F 6) and Megasthenes (FGrH 715 F 1)

**Eusebius, PE 9.41.6** (p. 457c Viger). Greek from Gifford's 1903 edition (archive.org OCR, Tomus I, ll. 44275-44293) [raw_C/evangelicaepraep01euse_djvu.txt]; Ionic dialect (Abydenus imitates Herodotus):

> Ναβουχοδονόσορος δὲ διαδεξάμενος τὴν ἀρχὴν Βαβυλῶνα μὲν ἐτείχισε τριπλῷ περιβόλῳ ἐν πέντε καὶ δέκα ἡμέρῃσι, τόν τε Ἀρμακάλην ποταμὸν ἐξήγαγεν, ἐόντα κέρας Εὐφρήτεω, τόν τε Ἀκράκανον. Ὑπὲρ δὲ τῆς Σιππαρηνῶν πόλιος λάκκον ὀρυξάμενος, περίμετρον μὲν τεσσαράκοντα παρασαγγέων, βάθος δ᾽ ὀργυιέων εἴκοσι, πύλας ἐπέστησεν, τὰς ἀνοίγοντες ἄρδεσκον τὸ πεδίον· καλέουσι δ᾽ αὐτὰς ἐχετογνώμονας. Ἀπετείχισε δὲ καὶ τῆς Ἐρυθρῆς θαλάσσης τὴν ἐπίκλυσιν, καὶ Τερηδόνα πόλιν ἔκτισεν κατὰ τὰς Ἀράβων εἰσβολάς· **τά τε βασιλήϊα δένδροις ἤσκησε, κρεμαστοὺς παραδείσους ὀνομάσας.**

Gifford: "When Nebuchadnezzar had succeeded to the kingdom, he fortified Babylon with a triple circuit of walls in fifteen days, and he changed the course of the river Armacales … He also walled off the inundation of the Red Sea, and built the city Teredon at the place of the incursions of the Arabs. **His palace too he adorned with trees, and gave it the name of the Hanging Gardens.**" Eusebius introduces this as from Abydenus' *Concerning the Assyrians*, and the chapter opens "Now **Megasthenes** says that Nebuchadnezzar was braver than Hercules…" (= FGrH 715 F 1b; cf. Josephus AJ 10.227 = F 1a). The Armenian *Chronicle* adds, after the same Abydenus sentence: "[Abydenus] presents a detailed description of this so-called Hanging Garden. The Greeks, he says, regarded [the Hanging Garden] as [one] of the seven wonders of the world" (Bedrosian [g55]) — a detailed Abydenus description that Eusebius chose not to copy and that is lost.

Abydenus is thus an independent (Berossus-derived? Megasthenes-derived?) 2nd-c. AD strand attributing "hanging *paradeisoi*" (plural) to Nebuchadnezzar's *palace*; it gives no wife, no homesickness, no construction detail. No wife, no mountains: only "adorned the palace with trees". This matters for the interpolation debate (§2.4).

The park extended four plethra on each side, and since the approach to the garden sloped like a hillside and the several parts of the structure rose from one another tier on tier, the appearance of the whole resembled that of a theatre. When the ascending terraces had been built, there had been constructed beneath them galleries which carried the entire weight of the planted garden and rose little by little one above the other along the approach; and the uppermost gallery, which was fifty cubits high, bore the highest surface of the park, which was made level with the circuit wall of the battlements of the city. Furthermore, the walls, which had been constructed at great expense, were twenty-two feet thick, while the passage-way between each two walls was ten feet wide. The roofs of the galleries were covered over with beams of stone sixteen feet long, inclusive of the overlap, and four feet wide. The roof above these beams had first a layer of reeds laid in great quantities of bitumen, over this two courses of baked brick bonded by cement, and as a third layer a covering of lead, to the end that the moisture from the soil might not penetrate beneath. On all this again earth had been piled to a depth sufficient for the roots of the largest trees; and the ground, which was levelled off, was thickly planted with trees of every kind that, by their great size or any other charm, could give pleasure to beholder. And since the galleries, each projecting beyond another, all received the light, they contained many royal lodgings of every description; and there was one gallery which contained openings leading from the topmost surface and machines for supplying the garden with water, the machines raising the water in great abundance from the river, although no one outside could see it being done. Now this park, as I have said, was a later construction."

Note the vocabulary: the water-lifting devices are *ὄργανα* ("instruments/engines"), not *μηχαναί* and not *κοχλίαι*; the "openings" are *διατομαί* (cuts, shafts); the galleries are *σύριγγες* (literally "pipes", used of vaulted passages); "Persian by race" (*τὸ γένος Περσίδα*), a *παλλακή* (concubine), not a wife; the garden lies *παρὰ τὴν ἀκρόπολιν*, i.e. beside the fortified palace-citadel which 2.8.4-7 has just described, on the west bank (2.8.4 puts the more elaborate palace "on the west bank"? — Oldfather: the two palaces at either end of the bridge; **[unverified detail, not re-checked]**).

**Context, 2.7.2-4** (Oldfather; Greek in the same Wikisource file): "Taking the Euphrates river into the centre she threw about the city a wall with great towers set at frequent intervals, the wall being three hundred and sixty stades in circumference, as Ctesias of Cnidus says, but according to the account of **Cleitarchus and certain of those who at a later time crossed into Asia with Alexander**, three hundred and sixty-five stades; and these latter add that it was her desire to make the number of stades the same as the days in the year." (= Clitarchus FGrH 137 F 10.) Diodorus thus openly names two sources for the Babylon *ekphrasis*: Ctesias, and Clitarchus + Alexander-companions. Strabo 16.1.5 and Curtius 5.1.26 give 365 (Curtius: CCCLXVIII) stades, i.e. the Alexander-historians' figure, not Ctesias'.

**2.3.2-3 (Ninus founds Nineveh "on the Euphrates").** Greek: "…αὐτὸς δὲ τὰς πανταχόθεν δυνάμεις καὶ παρασκευὰς πάντων τῶν ἐπιτηδείων ἀθροίσας **παρὰ τὸν Εὐφράτην ποταμὸν ἔκτισε πόλιν** εὖ τετειχισμένην, ἑτερόμηκες αὐτῆς ὑποστησάμενος τὸ σχῆμα. εἶχε δὲ τῶν μὲν μακροτέρων πλευρῶν ἑκατέραν ἡ πόλις ἑκατὸν καὶ πεντήκοντα σταδίων, τῶν δὲ βραχυτέρων ἐνενήκοντα … τὸ μὲν γὰρ ὕψος εἶχε τὸ τεῖχος ποδῶν ἑκατόν, τὸ δὲ πλάτος τρισὶν ἅρμασιν ἱππάσιμον ἦν· οἱ δὲ σύμπαντες πύργοι τὸν μὲν ἀριθμὸν ἦσαν χίλιοι καὶ πεντακόσιοι, τὸ δ᾽ ὕψος εἶχον ποδῶν διακοσίων." **2.11.5** (obelisk): "καὶ τοῦτον τὸν λίθον ὀβελίσκον τινὲς ὀνομάζουσιν … καὶ τῶν ἑπτὰ θεαμάτων ἀριθμοῦσιν" **[Greek unverified — I quote Oldfather:]** "And this stone is called by some an obelisk from its shape, and they number it among the seven wonders of the world." So Diodorus (or his source) knows a canon of seven that includes something at Babylon *other than* the garden.

**2.13.1-3** (Semiramis' Median gardens). **Source of 2.10 — the scholarly positions** (secondary; none of the printed discussions was directly accessible):
- Jacoby printed Diod. 2.1.4-28.7 as Ctesias FGrH 688 F 1b, so 2.10 is formally "Ctesias F 1b §10"; Thackeray's Loeb note (1926) still says "quoted from Ctesias in Diodorus". Photius' epitome of the *Persica* (cod. 72) begins with book 7, so there is **no independent Ctesias fragment** for the Babylonian buildings; Nicolaus of Damascus (FGrH 90 F 1-5) is the only other channel **[from memory]**.
- The disclaimers in 2.10.1 ("not of Semiramis but of a *later* Syrian king", "*ὡς προεῖπον, ὕστερον κατεσκευάσθη*", "*φασιν*") show that the paragraph is an insertion into the Ctesian Semiramis narrative; **Bigwood 1980** (*Phoenix* 34: 195-207) argued generally that Diodorus reworked Ctesias with Hellenistic material; **Reade 2000** (*Iraq* 62: 195-217) and **Dalley 2013** derive 2.10 (and Curtius 5.1.31-35) from **Clitarchus**, i.e. from eyewitness Alexander-period reports; Livius.org (J. Lendering, summarising van der Spek 2008): "All our sources can, directly or indirectly, be connected to his [Cleitarchus'] biography of Alexander"; Wikipedia: "Diodorus … seem[s] to have consulted the 4th century BC texts of both Cleitarchus … and Ctesias." **Bichler–Rollinger 2005** (AOAT 325: 153-217) and **Stronk 2010** were not accessible; plinia.net records "some controversy about whether his description … is founded on Ctesias or Clitarchus". Rollinger (in *The World of Berossos*, 2013, per BMCR 2014.05.50) notes the gardens "were first mentioned (by Clitarchus?) after Alexander's conquest of Babylon".
- My assessment: the wall-circuit doublet in 2.7.3 (Ctesias 360 vs Clitarchus 365) proves that Diodorus had Clitarchus open beside Ctesias for the Babylon *ekphrasis*; 2.10's "Persian concubine" and "Syrian king" (both un-Ctesian: Ctesias' Amytis is Median, and Ctesias' Assyrians are "Assyrians") plus the explicit "later than Semiramis" fit a Clitarchan origin better. Nothing in 2.10 requires an eyewitness: the 22-ft walls, 10-ft passages, 16 × 4-ft stone beams and the roofing recipe are the kind of detail a Hellenistic historian could obtain from a Babylonian informant or invent.

Jones: "Babylon, too, lies in a plain; and the circuit of its wall is three hundred and eighty-five [MSS; edd. 365] stadia. Hoc enim ligni genus unum ex omnibus cariem non sentit … (4) At aquarum scatebrae ex eminentibus manantes fontibus partim recto cursu per prona labuntur, partim vi elisae per flexus et spiras in altum emicant, organorum quorundam impulsionibus per machinarum anfractus circumvolutae. Sic in crebra et ampla conceptacula sursum collectae totum irrigant hortum … (5) … Amoenissimae voluptatis et regalis munificentiae prorsus istud est opus, in quo vis est facta naturae dum agricolationis opera supra spectantium capita suspensa praebetur."

English (§4, D. Oates in Finkel, "The Hanging Gardens of Babylon", in Clayton & Price 1988: 46, as reprinted in Dalley & Oleson, *Technology and Culture* 44 (2003) 12 [raw_C/dalley_oleson_2003.txt]): "Streams of water issuing from springs higher up flow partly downward in a direct course, partly are forced upwards through bends and spirals to gush out higher up, being pushed through the twists of these devices by mechanical forces. So, brought together in frequent and commodious sources at a high level, these waters irrigate the whole garden…." The rest of the chapter is not available to me in a published translation; my paraphrase: a garden "hanging" above ground, cultivated in mid-air, on stone columns and a fully colonnaded undercroft; palm-trunk beams close-set (palm alone does not rot and bows upward under wet load); deep earth over them; broad-leaved garden trees and flowers; ploughable land over the heads of those walking in the colonnade; "a luxurious and royal work of art, and above all a violent one, to hang the labour of agriculture over the spectators' heads".

**Observations.** (i) Philo gives no city, no king, no river, no wife and no dimensions — nothing that ties the garden to Babylon *or* Nineveh; it is the only description built on structural engineering (columns, palm beams, soil depth) rather than on Alexander-historian measurements. (ii) The water words are *κοχλιοειδῶς* ("screw-wise"), *τὸν ἕλικα τῶν μηχανημάτων* ("the spiral/helix of the machines") and *ἀνάγκαις ὀργανικαῖς* ("by mechanical compulsion") — the only text apart from Strabo to imply screws, and it does so with the ordinary late-Greek vocabulary for a spiral, not with Strabo's technical *κοχλίας*. (iii) *ἐξ ὑπερδεξίων … τόπων* "springs from higher ground" is un-Babylonian and the one detail in the corpus that reads more naturally at Nineveh (Dalley's aqueduct argument); but Philo's account is a rhetorical ecphrasis of an ideal *pensilis hortus*, not a report. (iv) Date: the *De septem spectaculis* is a work of the 4th-6th c. AD by its language (Kroll, *RE* 20.1: 54-55 "Philon (49)"; Brodersen 1992: 15; Dalley & Oleson 2003: 10 "a paradoxographer and rhetorician of the fourth century A.D."; Gainsford "C. 4-5 CE"); the date "c. 250 BC" in Finkel (Clayton & Price 1988: 45) and much popular literature confuses him with Philo the Mechanicus (Dalley & Oleson: "unaccountably"). The text is therefore later than every other testimony and cannot be used as "early Hellenistic" evidence for screws.

## 2. The silences and the authenticity of the Berossus sentence

**Herodotus 1.178-187** (Greek Perseus 1999.01.0125 and el.wikisource [raw_C/herodotus_1_178_greek.html; herodotus_1_el_wikisource.html]) describes Babylon at length — "τῆς δὲ Ἀσσυρίης ἐστὶ μὲν κου καὶ ἄλλα πολίσματα μεγάλα πολλά, τὸ δὲ ὀνομαστότατον καὶ ἰσχυρότατον καὶ ἔνθα σφι Νίνου ἀναστάτου γενομένης τὰ βασιλήια κατεστήκεε, ἦν Βαβυλών" (1.178.1); walls of 50 royal cubits' breadth and 200 height (1.178.3); the two royal palaces and the Belus temple (1.181); the two queens Semiramis and Nitocris (1.184-187) — and a search of the whole book yields no κῆπος, παράδεισος or κρεμαστός. Herodotus visited (or claims to have visited) Babylon under the Persians a century after Nebuchadnezzar. **Xenophon, *Anab.* 3.4.7-12** [raw_C/xenophon_anab_3_4_greek_full.html]: "ἐνταῦθα πόλις ἦν ἐρήμη μεγάλη, ὄνομα δ᾽ αὐτῇ ἦν Λάρισα· ᾤκουν δ᾽ αὐτὴν τὸ παλαιὸν Μῆδοι … (10) πρὸς τεῖχος ἔρημον μέγα … ὄνομα δὲ ἦν τῇ πόλει Μέσπιλα· Μῆδοι δ᾽ αὐτήν ποτε ᾤκουν" — Nimrud and Nineveh in 401 BC, deserted, misattributed to the Medes, no garden; *Cyrop.* 7.5 (capture of Babylon, 86 sections) has no garden vocabulary either. **Ctesias** (Photius cod. 72, *Persica* epitome from book 7; Diodorus 2 for books 1-6): nothing on a hanging garden survives under his name, and the *Persica* fragments were not fetched [from memory]. **Arrian 3.16.3-5** (entry 331 BC) and **7.17-25** (the Belus temple, canals, Alexander's death) [raw_C/arrian_3_el_wikisource.html; arrian_7_*]: no hanging garden — only the *παράδεισος* of 7.25.3 above. **Diodorus 17.64.3-4** [raw_C/diodorus_17_64_greek.html]: Alexander "κατήντησε μετὰ πάσης τῆς δυνάμεως εἰς Βαβυλῶνα … πλείους δὲ τῶν τριάκοντα ἡμερῶν ἐνδιέτριψε τῇ πόλει" — thirty days in Babylon and not a word of the garden that the same author had described in book 2. **Pliny 6.121-123**: silent (above). Nothing survives from Callisthenes, Aristobulus, Nearchus or Ptolemy about it.

**Berossus versus Babylon.** Josephus' Berossus paragraph (1a) lists Nebuchadnezzar's works in the sequence of the king's own "East India House" inscription (walls, Esagila, palace built in fifteen days, etc.); the garden is the one item with no cuneiform counterpart, and no cuneiform text from Babylon — including the topographical series *Tintir* — mentions a hanging garden (van der Spek 2008, as summarised at Livius.org [raw_C/livius_hanging_gardens.html]: "The similarity between the Berossus fragment quoted by Josephus through Polyhistor and the East India House Inscription is so striking that it is impossible that Berossus does not quote (a copy of) it. This leaves us with only three possibilities: Josephus added information from Cleitarchus to information he found in Polyhistor (unlikely…); Polyhistor added information from Cleitarchus to Berossus (likely…); Berossus added information from Cleitarchus to the East India House Inscription … it seems very likely that the Hanging Gardens are in fact Cleitarchus' fantastic description of the royal palace in Babylon"). Dalley 1994 (*Iraq* 56) argued that "Josephus or his source wrongly attributed to Berossus a passage about the Hanging Gardens" and that Abydenus' parallel "may be attributed correctly to Berossos but be corrupt in having the name of Nebuchadnezzar substituted for the name of [Sennacherib]" (quoted in Bichler 2004 n. 49 [raw_C/rollinger_assyria_classical.txt]); in *The Mystery of the Hanging Garden* (2013, ch. 3 — not accessible) she develops this into the claim that the whole Greek tradition transferred Sennacherib's garden to Babylon. Against this, Bichler 2004: 19-20: "Berossus remains the only one to attribute their construction to King Nebuchadnezzar … The crucial point does not lie in the location of the Hanging Gardens nor in the romantic nature of the story … There is another problem: the combination of Astyages as a Median satrap under the rule of Nabopolassaros and a princess named Amytis" — i.e. the Median-marriage frame is itself Ctesianic; Burstein 1978: 27 n. 106 ("Berossus was catering to the interests of his Greek readers") and Reade 2000: 200 ("It was the failure to recognize the contribution of Nebuchadnezzar … that especially offended Berossus") accept the sentence as Berossan. Points the texts themselves settle: (i) the sentence stands in *both* Josephan quotations and in Syncellus, so it was in Alexander Polyhistor's Berossus by the 1st c. BC at latest; (ii) Eusebius *PE* 9.40 omits it but *PE* 9.41 (Abydenus) has an independent, wife-less version, and the Armenian *Chronicle* names the wife "Amuhean" — so the garden was in Polyhistor's Abydenus too; (iii) the name Amytis is nowhere in the Greek Josephus, only in the Armenian; (iv) Abydenus' wording (*τὸν κρεμαστὸν κῆπον … κατασκευάσαι*, water from the Euphrates/Armakales, a "lake" with sluices) is not simply copied from Josephus' Berossus and may descend via Megasthenes (FGrH 715 F 1: the Armenian Eusebius cites Megasthenes' *Indica* book 4 alongside Abydenus — Gainsford 2018 [raw_C/kiwihellenist_hanging_gardens.html]). The dispute is therefore between "Berossus wrote it to please Greeks who already believed in Clitarchus' garden" and "Polyhistor (or Megasthenes) interpolated it"; nothing in the manuscript tradition of Josephus supports the latter, and De Breucker's BNJ 680 F 8 commentary (not accessible) is reported to treat the sentence as Berossan but Greek-oriented [unverified].

Only the Berossus-Abydenus-Megasthenes line names Nebuchadnezzar (Bichler 2004: 19: "Berossus remains the only one to attribute their construction to King Nebuchadnezzar"). The Alexander-derived line says a **later Assyrian king ruling in Babylon** — a formula that, in Ctesias' scheme where "Assyria" runs unbroken from Ninus to Sardanapalus and Nebuchadnezzar has no place, fits any Sargonid who held Babylon (Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, Ashurbanipal) as easily as it fits Nebuchadnezzar; Rolfe's "Syria for Assyria" and Strabo 16.1.2 ("οἱ Σύροι … οἱ ἐν Βαβυλῶνι καὶ Νίνῳ τὸ βασίλειον καταστησάμενοι") show that the Greek word covers both. This ambiguity, not any explicit statement, is what Dalley's relocation exploits.

Fit with the Nineveh dossier (Sennacherib's "palace without rival" texts with their water-raising device that Dalley reads as a bronze screw; the Jerwan aqueduct and Khinis canal; Ashurbanipal's garden relief BM 124939 with an aqueduct on arches and a pillared pavilion): the Greek texts offer three genuine points of contact — Strabo's *κοχλίαι*, Philo's *ἕλιξ* and *ὑπερδέξιοι πηγαί*, and Strabo's/Diodorus' arched masonry which resembles the relief's arcaded aqueduct — and Berossus' "made to look like mountains" echoes Sennacherib's garden "like the Amanus" [Assyrian wording from memory — outside my brief]. Against them: (i) every text that names a place says Babylon and the Euphrates, and Strabo's screws are worked by men from the river, not gravity-fed; (ii) no Greek or Latin text mentions bronze, an aqueduct, or Nineveh; (iii) Diodorus' *ὄργανα*/*διατομαί* need not be screws at all (Dalley & Oleson 2003: 12: "might imply a vertical lift by means of ropes"), and the same authors admit "Only the passage in Strabo is early enough and clear enough to carry some authority, and yet there is no guarantee that the water screws he mentions appeared in his source or that the source itself predates Archimedes" (p. 14); (iv) the 4-plethra square and the 20/22-ft walls are the numbers of a Hellenistic eyewitness-style description (Clitarchus) of something at Babylon, and Curtius' *inviolata durat* presupposes a standing structure there; (v) Philo's columns-and-palm-beams recall the relief's pillared walkway (Dalley & Oleson n. 34), but Philo is 4th-6th c. AD and describes no place. In short, the classical dossier is consistent with a real terraced palace garden at Babylon described by Alexander's companions and afterwards decorated with Ctesianic romance (Median/Persian wife) and, in Strabo/Philo, with Archimedean screws; it is *also* consistent with Dalley's transfer hypothesis, but supplies no positive statement for it.

### 3c. Babylon/Nineveh confusion in the Greek tradition (all quoted above)

Diodorus 2.3.2 (Ctesias): Ninus founds Nineveh "παρὰ τὸν Εὐφράτην ποταμόν"; Diodorus 2.7-9: Semiramis founds Babylon; Herodotus 1.178.1: Babylon is where "Νίνου ἀναστάτου γενομένης τὰ βασιλήια κατεστήκεε" of Assyria, and 1.184 promises the kings of Babylon "ἐν τοῖσι Ἀσσυρίοισι λόγοισι"; Xenophon *Anab.* 3.4.7-12: Nimrud = "Λάρισα … ᾤκουν δ᾽ αὐτὴν τὸ παλαιὸν Μῆδοι", Nineveh = "Μέσπιλα· Μῆδοι δ᾽ αὐτήν ποτε ᾤκουν"; Strabo 16.1.2-3: the "Syrians" of the historians "are none other than those who set up the royal seat at Babylon and Ninus", and Nineveh lay in "Aturia"; Curtius 5.1.35 "Syriae regem Babylone regnantem"; Pliny 19.49 "Assyriae rex Syrus", 6.121 "reliqua pars Mesopotamiae Assyriaeque Babylonia appellata est"; Martial "Assyrius … labor"; Judith 1:1 "Nebuchadnezzar, who reigned over the Assyrians in Nineveh" [Septuagint, from memory — not fetched]. Nineveh had been ἐρήμη since 612 and was mis-labelled by 401; a Greek of 331 could attach any Assyrian marvel he had heard of to Babylon, the only Mesopotamian capital he saw standing. This is the strongest *textual* support for relocation — and it is inferential.

### 3d. Where the Alexander historians put it

Diodorus: "παρὰ τὴν ἀκρόπολιν", water "ἐκ τοῦ ποταμοῦ"; Curtius: "super arcem" (the citadel with a 20-stade circuit, 5.1.31); Strabo: "ἐπὶ δὲ τῷ ποταμῷ ὁ κῆπος", screws "ἀπὸ τοῦ Εὐφράτου"; Berossus: inside "these palaces"; Abydenus: fed from the Euphrates via the Armakales canal. All four independent phrasings place it in the palace-citadel on the Euphrates bank — exactly where Koldewey's Southern Palace and the river-side Western Outwork lie. Arrian (from the *Ephemerides*) knows only a *παράδεισος* "πέραν τοῦ ποταμοῦ" (7.25.3), reached by boat, and never a κρεμαστὸς κῆπος; Diodorus 17.64 and Arrian 3.16, narrating Alexander's month in Babylon, are silent. The silence of Arrian's sources (Ptolemy, Aristobulus) against the garrulity of the Clitarchan "vulgate" (Curtius' *vulgatum Graecorum fabulis miraculum*) is the single most important source-critical datum: the garden belongs to the sensational tradition, not to the sober one.

## 4. Chronology of the "seven wonders" canon and the garden's place in it

Herodotus (5th c.) admires individual monuments but has no list; Callimachus' *Θαυμάτων … συναγωγή* (Suda) is lost and nothing shows it contained the garden [from memory]. The earliest list, the Berlin papyrus *Laterculi Alexandrini* (P. Berol. 13044, late 2nd c. BC), is "partly lost" — "Preserved entries: temple of Artemis, pyramids, Mausoleum" (Gainsford 2017, endnote [raw_C/kiwihellenist_seven_wonders_2017.html]); Antipater of Sidon (c. 100 BC) gives the earliest intact seven, with *κάπων αἰώρημα*; Diodorus 2.11.5 (c. 30 BC) already speaks of "the seven wonders" when describing Semiramis' obelisk; Strabo (c. AD 20) counts the wall *and* the garden among "τῶν ἑπτὰ θεαμάτων"; Pliny (AD 77) knows *pensiles* (19.49) but names Thebes' at 36.94; Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 380) has *κῆποι* in verse and the *walls* in prose; Philo (4th-6th c.) gives the garden first place; the Latin lists of Hyginus, Ampelius, Cassiodorus (507-511) and Gregory of Tours (c. 575) have only the wall, and ps.-Bede (8th c. or later) drops Babylon altogether; the Pharos "doesn't appear in any ancient list" (Gainsford). Two conclusions: the garden is canonical only in the Greek lists between Antipater and Philo, always at (or, when unlabelled, next to) Babylon; and, as Gainsford puts it, "it doesn't look like any Greek list-maker ever included a specific garden at Nineveh in the Greek canon of 'seven wonders'". The garden's *literary* history begins after 331 — Rollinger (in *The World of Berossos*, 2013, as quoted in BMCR 2014.05.50 [raw page not saved]) suggests it was "first mentioned (by Clitarchus?) after Alexander's conquest of Babylon" — and Berossus (c. 290-280) is the only pre-Antipater witness, if the sentence is his.
