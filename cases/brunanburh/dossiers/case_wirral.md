# Brunanburh (937) and Bromborough, Wirral: the case for and the case against

*Research dossier. Part of the Brunanburh location investigation.*

## 0. Scope, method and conventions

This dossier assembles (A) the place-name philology of Bromborough, (B) the *Dingesmere* argument, (C) the historical and strategic context claimed for a Wirral battle, (D) the Wirral Archaeology finds of 2019 onward and their independent assessment, and (E) the published case against a Wirral location, followed by a Balance section.

Evidence tags used throughout:

- **[READ]** – the source was read in full or in the relevant part during this research (file names in section 7 give the local extract).
- **[VIA x]** – the claim, page number or quotation is taken from a secondary source x that quotes or cites it; the original was not consulted.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** – could not be checked (paywalled, image-only scan, or no text available). Nothing marked UNVERIFIED should be quoted onward without checking.

Not every follow-up search could be run; the main gaps are listed in section 6.4.

Primary text at issue (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle poem s.a. 937, lines 53–56, ASPR VI text):

> Gewitan him þa Norþmen nægledcnearrum, / dreorig daraða laf, on Dingesmere / ofer deop wæter Difelin secan, / eft Iraland, æwiscmode.
> "The Northmen departed in their nailed ships, sad survivors of the spears, on/onto Dingesmere, over deep water to seek Dublin, back to Ireland, shamed."
> (E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., *The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems*, ASPR VI (New York, 1942), pp. 16–20; standard line numbering. Manuscript variants of *Dingesmere* are given in B.1.)

## A. Place-name philology of Bromborough

### A.1 The early spellings of Bromborough

The modern name Bromborough (SJ 34 82, Wirral, Cheshire) is not recorded in Domesday Book (1086); its earliest attestations are twelfth-century. The forms as recorded by John McN. Dodgson, *The Place-Names of Cheshire*, part 4, EPNS 47 (Cambridge, 1972), pp. 237–38 [VIA Cavill 2011 and PHC 2020; PN Ch 4 not consulted directly], laid out by Cavill as a sequence of regular sound-changes from OE *Brūnan burh*:

| Process (Cavill's description) | Form | Date of original document |
|---|---|---|
| loss of nasal after unstressed syllable | *Bruneburgh* | 1153 |
| syncope of unstressed vowel | *Brunburg* | 1100–35 |
| assimilation of -n- to following bilabial -b- | *Brumburg'* | 1153 |
| lowering of -u- to -o- | *Brombur'* | 1153–59 |
| parasite vowel in second element | *Bromboreh* | c. 1200 |
| modern form | *Bromborough* | 1277 |

Source: P. Cavill, "The Place-Name Debate", in M. Livingston (ed.), *The Battle of Brunanburh: A Casebook* (Exeter: University of Exeter Press, 2011), pp. 327–49, at p. 344 ("illustrated from the spellings recorded by John Dodgson … PN Ch 4.237–38 … The dates given are those of the original document"); pre-print at https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/-sczsteve/09essay-cavill.pdf [READ]. Cavill also notes the second element is variously spelled *-burch-, -burg(h), -burth*. The same processes are summarised by J. D. Niles, "Skaldic Technique in Brunanburh", *Scandinavian Studies* 59 (1987), 356–66, at 364 n. 2 [VIA Cavill 2011].

The council-commissioned assessment (Downham's chapter) gives the same sequence in a slightly different orthography: "Brunberg (1100–1135) Bruneberg (1153) Brumburg (1153) Brombur (1153–59) Bromboreh (c. 1200)", citing Cavill 2011 p. 344 (PHC Services report, March 2022 redacted version, p. 107; see D for full reference) [READ]. The *-burg* vs *-berg* discrepancy between the two secondary reports is unresolved here and should be settled from PN Ch 4.237–38 directly.

**Which documents.** The 1100–35 and 1153 forms are dated by the originals in the way EPNS volumes date charters; the underlying documents are almost certainly the charters and cartulary of St Werburgh's Abbey, Chester (which held Bromborough manor) — but I could not verify the specific documents behind each spelling **[UNVERIFIED]**. Dodgson (PN Ch 4.239) states "there is evidence which suggests that before 1086 Bromborough was the capital of an extensive tract of country", and (4.241) records a local tradition of a fortification at Bromborough Court House, a nine-acre earthwork that later became a stronghold of the abbots of St Werburgh's [VIA Cavill 2011, §6; Harding 2011].

### A.2 Derivation: OE *Brūnan burh* 'Bruna's stronghold'

- **Dodgson 1957.** J. McN. Dodgson, "The Background of Brunanburh", *Saga-Book of the Viking Society* 14 (1957), 303–16, first argued in detail that Bromborough < OE *Brūnanburh* and set the battle in Wirral; reprinted in PN Ch 5:2 (1997), pp. 249–61, and in P. Cavill, S. E. Harding and J. Jesch, *Wirral and its Viking Heritage* (EPNS Popular Series 2, Nottingham, 2000), pp. 60–69 [VIA Cavill 2011, Cavill 2014]. **The Saga-Book scan obtained (https://www.vsnrweb-publications.org.uk/Saga-Book%20XIV.pdf) is image-only with no text layer, so Dodgson's own wording was NOT read [UNVERIFIED]**; all statements about it here are via Cavill.
- **Rejection of 'broom' etymology.** Ekwall, *Concise Oxford Dictionary of English Place-Names* (4th edn, 1960) still explained Bromborough as 'burh where broom grows' (OE *brōm*), despite A. H. Smith; the twelfth-century forms with *Brun-/Brune-* and no *-m-* until the assimilated *Brumburg'* exclude *brōm* and require a first element *Brūnan*, the weak genitive of a personal name *Brūna* [VIA Cavill 2011 §6; Cavill 2014].
- **The personal name Bruna.** *Brūna* is a hypocoristic (short) form of dithematic names in *Brūn-*. Keith Briggs, *An Index to the Personal Names in English Place-Names* (EPNS, 2021) lists only four place-names containing *Bruna*: Bromborough, Brimstage and Brimston (the last two on Wirral, plausibly one and the same landholder) and Bromham, Bedfordshire; PASE (Prosopography of Anglo-Saxon England) lists no *Bruna*, and Searle only a moneyer [VIA Cavill, "Spellings of Brunanburh Revisited", *Northern History* 59:2 (2022), 162–76, DOI 10.1080/0078172X.2022.2088437 (open access) — READ in extract]. Dodgson had already connected the Bruna of Bromborough with Brimstage (*Brunstath* 1260, *Brimstache* 1275, *Brunstach* 1326) and Brimston (PN Ch 4.234–36) [VIA PHC 2020 p. 108; Cavill 2011].
- **The -werc/-dun/-feld variants of the battle-name.** Dodgson (PN Ch 4.238) held that Symeon's *Brunnanwerc/Brunnanbyrig* forms "are no more than a paraphrase of Brunanburh" [VIA Cavill 2011]. The full set of name-forms in the sources is tabulated in the PHC report, pp. 105–06 [READ]: ASC A *Brun(n)anburh*, B *Brunannburh*, C *Brunnanburh*, D *Brunanburh*, E *Brunanbyrig*; Æthelweard *Brunandune*; *Annales Cambriae* *Brune*; Chronicle of the Kings of Alba *Duinbrunde*; Symeon/HR *Brunnanwerc*, *Brunnanbyrig*, *Wendune/Weondune*; William of Malmesbury *Brunefeld*; John of Worcester *Brunanburh*; Henry of Huntingdon *Brunebirih*. Cavill's 2022 *Spellings* article argues that the *-nn-* spellings are orthographic variants (or hypocoristic gemination) and do not point to a different name such as *brunna* 'spring' [READ in extract].

### A.3 Why Bromborough is the *only* surviving name regularly derived from *Brunanburh*

- Cavill's minimal claim: "since Bromborough on the Wirral is the only place-name known to derive from Old English Brunanburh, it should at least be considered" (P. Cavill, "The Battle of Brunanburh in 937: Battlefield Despatches", in S. E. Harding, D. Griffiths and E. Royles (eds), *In Search of Vikings* (Boca Raton: CRC Press, 2015), pp. 95–108, at p. 95; PDF at https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/-sczsteve/Cavill_2014.pdf) [READ].
- Even critics concede the derivation: Michael Wood accepts Bromborough is "convincing as a place-name" (Wood 2013, p. 154 [VIA Cavill 2022 Yorkshire]); Neil McGuigan's review of the Casebook (*Scottish Historical Review* 93 (2014), 286–88) allows that Bromborough "might be derived" from *Brunanburh* [VIA Cavill 2014/2022; review not read — UNVERIFIED wording]; Thomas Williams, reviewing Livingston in *British Archaeology* (Sept.–Oct. 2021), p. 58, accepts that Bromborough is the only *surviving* name from OE *Brunanburh* [VIA Wikipedia "Battle of Brunanburh" — UNVERIFIED].
- The 2025 book states the position flatly: "none of the many alternative sites proposed over the years can be shown to have ever been called Brunanburh in medieval sources" (P. Cavill and S. E. Harding, *Finding the Battle of Brunanburh* (University of Chester Press, Dec. 2025), ISBN 978-1-910481-15-8; quoted in Medievalists.net, 16 Dec. 2025, and University of Chester press release "New book paves the way for ending the debate") [READ press coverage; book not read — UNVERIFIED page].
- Supporting onomastic detail around Bromborough: Richard Coates, "A further snippet of evidence for Brunanburh = Bromborough", *Notes and Queries* 45:3 (1998), 288–90, identifies *Welondrys* (1357), Rice Wood near Bromborough Court House, as OE *wēoh* + *land* + *hrīs* 'brushwood of the heathen-shrine land', a possible correlate of Symeon's *Weondun* 'holy hill'; Harding adds "Harrow Fields" (OE *hearg*) with a postscript by Margaret Gelling (Casebook, Harding chapter) [VIA Cavill 2011 and Harding 2011; Coates not read — UNVERIFIED].

### A.4 The objection that a same-named place need not be the battle site — and the replies

**The objection.**

- Alistair Campbell, *The Battle of Brunanburh* (London: Heinemann, 1938), p. 59 n. 4: Bromborough's name, containing "Brunan-, a common, and -burh, a very common, place-name element, proves nothing"; p. 80: "Unless new evidence can be produced, an honest nescio is greatly to be preferred" [VIA Cavill 2014 pp. 95, 100; Campbell not read].
- Dodgson himself concurred with Campbell that "the identity of place-name does not prove that this Brunanburh in Wirral is the same as that near which the battle took place" (Cavill 2011, n. 63, quoting Dodgson), and Cavill 2014 p. 96 records Dodgson's reluctance to press the Wirral identification (citing Dodgson 1997 p. 263 n. 11 and Downham, *Viking Kings of Britain and Ireland* (2007), p. 104) [READ Cavill; Dodgson VIA].
- Kevin Halloran, "The Identity of Etbrunnanwerc", *SHR* 89 (2010), 248–53, at 249 n. 6, and Michael Wood, "Brunanburh Revisited", *Saga-Book* 20 (1980), 200–17, at 213 n. 4, repeat Campbell's point [VIA Cavill 2014].
- Wood 2013 (p. 154): the Bromborough case "will rest entirely on uncertain and overstated onomastic evidence" (also pp. 143 n. 15, 144 n. 16, 146 n. 43); Wood, "The spelling of Brunanburh", *N&Q* 64:3 (2017), 365–69, argues for two places, with Burghwallis (S. Yorks) as a possible *brunna* 'spring' + *burh* [VIA Cavill 2022 Yorkshire and Spellings — UNVERIFIED wording].
- Michael Deakin, "Bromborough, Brunanburh, and Dingesmere", *Notes and Queries* 69 (2022) [pages UNVERIFIED], denies the derivation itself: first element OE *brūn* 'brown' ('the brown [stone-built] manor/fort'), a tenth-century Dee wetland improbable, *dingesmere* a poetic term for the sea (cf. *Andreas* 'ding'); earlier "Brūnanburh, Bromborough and Brūnburh" (academia.edu, 16 Oct. 2019); latest, "Brunnanburh and Brunanburh: Two Names, One Place and a Battle in a Cambridgeshire Valley", *Battalia* 3 (Battlefields Trust, 2025), 49–70 (Bourn, Cambs.) [all VIA search snippets / sibling survey; not read — UNVERIFIED].
- Williams 2021: other *Brunanburh*s may have existed and simply not survived [VIA Wikipedia — UNVERIFIED].

**The replies.**

- Cavill 2014, p. 100: "Campbell was mistaken on this matter" — *Brūna* is not a common element (see A.2 for its rarity), and "even if" both elements were common, "on a merely statistical view, their co-occurrence in Bromborough would make that infinitely more probable" than any place lacking them; "only Bromborough is reliably known to contain both elements" [READ].
- Cavill 2022 *Spellings*: with only four *Bruna* names in England and three of them on Wirral, the probability argument sharpens; and no form of Burghwallis resembles *Brun(n)anburh* (Cavill, "The Battle of Brunanburh: The Yorkshire Hypothesis", *English Studies* 103 (2022), DOI 10.1080/0013838X.2022.2154045, open access) [READ].
- Cavill, "The Battle of Brunanburh: The Lanchester Hypothesis", *Neophilologus* 107 (2023), 431–49, DOI 10.1007/s11061-022-09758-1 (open access) [READ]: a strong-masculine *Brūn* (as in river names, or 'brown') gives genitive *Brunes-* (Brownsall, Dorset, *Bruneselle* c. 1086; *Bruneshurst*, Wybunbury, Cheshire, 1275) and "cannot therefore have given the weak oblique form Brunan-"; this answers both Breeze's Browney and, by the same logic, Deakin's 'brown'.
- Downham (PHC 2020, p. 107): "It is possible that there may have originally been more than one place called Brunanburh in England, but it is surprising, if it was an important and strategic site, that record of the name would be lost" [READ].
- On Deakin's proposal in particular no published specialist reply was located other than Cavill & Harding 2025's general statement **[gap]**.

## B. *Dingesmere*

### B.1 The manuscript readings

ASC A and C: *dinges mere*; B: *dyngesmere*; D: *dynigesmere*; the burnt Otho B.xi text (known from Nowell's transcript): *dinnesmere*. Campbell (1938, p. 115) regarded the Otho reading as "an alteration by the scribe" [VIA Cavill, Harding and Jesch 2004, below]. Nowell's collation in BL Add. MS 43,703, f. 229r reads "Dyngesmere alias Dinnesmere. dyniges mere al." [VIA Cavill 2023, READ].

### B.2 Earlier interpretations

- 'Sea of noise/din': Bosworth–Toller (1898) entered *dynge* 'noise', whence "the sea of noise", followed by Cockburn (1931); but Toller's 1921 *Supplement* excised *dynge* and remarked that *dinnes* "seems to point to a proper name". Cavill, Harding and Jesch reject 'noise' on grammar (the genitive *-es* on a supposed feminine/abstract noun) and sense; Cavill 2014 pp. 104–06 adds that *dinne/*dynge are "ghost words" (Dictionary of Old English), that D's *dyniges-* would be 'wild parsnip', that the persistent *-g-* excludes *dyne*, that *dyncge* 'dung' does not take *-es*, and that Norse *dynja/dengja/dyngja* fail [READ]. Wood (2013, p. 143) claimed that the Bosworth–Toller Supplement suggested 'loud-sounding sea'; Cavill (2022 Escaping, n. 63) finds "no such suggestion" [READ].
- Dodgson's Dee derivation (PN Ch 4.240, a *-ing-* formation on the river-name Dee, 'the Dee-ings' mere'), withdrawn by Dodgson himself as a tautology and later half-revived (PN Ch 5:2, p. xxi, 1997, via Margaretting) [VIA 2004 paper].
- Breeze: emend to *on dingles mere* 'into the sea of the dingle/abyss' (Breeze 2018a, p. 74) — rejected by Cavill 2023 because *dingle* is a West-Midland Middle English word (first in *Sawles Warde* c. 1240) and no scribe made the emendation [READ Cavill].
- Halloran 2010 (p. 253 n. 24) prefers *dinnes* 'noise' [VIA Cavill 2014].
- Foulness (E. Yorks; 'dung-coloured' wetland) has been suggested in the Yorkshire literature [VIA Wikipedia "Dingesmere" — UNVERIFIED].

### B.3 The Thingwall argument (Cavill, Harding and Jesch 2004)

Paul Cavill, Stephen E. Harding and Judith Jesch, "Revisiting Dingesmere", *Journal of the English Place-Name Society* 36 (2004), 25–38; PDF at https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/-sczsteve/JEPNS_paper.PDF [READ in full]. Steps of the argument, with page references to the article as far as the OCR permits:

1. **It is a place-name.** Charter parallels — *dinmere* (Thanet), *dinnes hangran* (= Deanshanger, from the personal name *Dynne*), *dinnes hlinch* — show the first element in genuine toponyms; Toller's *Supplement* already pointed this way.
2. **The element is ON *þing*.** The paper proposes that "the element refers to the þing of Thingwall", one of "a series of eleven names in Britain deriving from Old Norse þing-vǫllr 'place where the assembly meets' (Fellows-Jensen 1993)", recorded 1086 DB and named in the Raby boundary; Thingwall "is on relatively high, gently sloping ground just over two miles from the Dee" at Heswall.
3. **Initial D- for Norse þ.** Compare Dingwall, Ross-shire (first recorded 1227 as *Dingwell*): Gaelic renders /þ/ as /t/ and then voices after the article ('i n-'); Wirral has a demonstrably Gaelic-Norse settlement stratum (Arrowe, Noctorum, Irby within 1.5 miles of Thingwall; Landican shows voicing), and Dingieshowe, Orkney (*þing-haugr*) shows the same D- in a Norse/Norn environment.
4. **Genitive -es.** ON *þings-* compounds are rare but exist (Tingsvallen, Dalarna; Glen Hinnisdal, Skye < *þings-dalr*; Dingieshowe); alternatively the "secondary genitive" seen in Cheshire Domesday names on Scandinavian bases (Basford *Berchesford*, Knutsford *Cunetesford*, Rostherne *Rodestorne*; cf. Thurstaston), or a purely English *þinges mere*.
5. **Mere means wetland/pool, not 'sea'.** In verse the simplex *mere* can mean 'sea', but the authors find no example of *mere* with a genitive-singular qualifier meaning 'sea'; in place-names *mere* is 'pond, lake, pool, wetland' (Gelling and Cole 2000, p. 21; Cole 1993, p. 40 'wetland'; Jacobsson 1997, p. 218 'land liable to flood'); Mersea is compared.
6. **Which water.** "A substantial area of wetland, between Gayton Sands and offshore of Heswall, just over two miles south-west of Thingwall" — i.e. the Dee estuary shore below Heswall/Thurstaston; *on … ofer* in the poem can refer to two different waters (*Solomon and Saturn* 204–05), giving the gloss "on the wetland by the thing, [went] over deep water to seek Dublin".
7. **Conclusion (quoted):** "the ding of dinges mere in the Old English poem of Brunanburh refers to the þing of Thingwall … the name probably derives ultimately from the hybrid Norse-OE þings-mere 'wetland by the thing', or possibly from a pure Norse compound, þings-marr 'marshland by the thing'. In our view this name was spoken with a Gaelic inflection and heard by a speaker of English. The process producing the form dinges mere from the posited þings-mere or þings-marr is undoubtedly complex, but there is sufficient contextual and comparative evidence to make it plausible."
8. **Poetic purpose.** The poet emphasises the desperation of the fugitives, fleeing from "a place close to the centre of Scandinavian power" on Wirral.

Origin: the identification was first suggested by Harding (talk at Thurstaston, 3 April 2004; https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/ncmh/vikings/brunanburh.aspx) and publicised in *The Independent*, 8 Dec. 2004 (Louise Jury; reproduced at the end of the Nottingham PDF) [READ]. Cavill developed it in "Coming back to Dingesmere", in P. Cavill and G. Broderick (eds), *Language Contact in the Place-Names of Britain and Ireland* (EPNS, 2007), pp. 27–41 [not read — UNVERIFIED contents].

Jesch's contribution (as co-author) supplies the Norse comparanda: the *þing-vǫllr* series, the treatment of *þ* in Gaelic-Norse contact names, and the possibility of ON *marr*; the paper also situates Thingwall within the Dublin–Chester sailing corridor. No separate Jesch article on Dingesmere was located.

### B.4 Later defence and refinement by the Wirral school

- Cavill 2014, pp. 104–06: reviews all rival etymologies, notes that Old English glosses spell *þing-* as *ding-* (*aengi dinga*, *dingere*), and concludes the Thingwall derivation "may not yet be unassailable, but it has not been effectively challenged by appeals to outdated guesswork"; Fig. 6.2 shows Heswall Point [READ].
- Cavill 2023 (Lanchester article): *þing*-names with D-/T-/F- initials (Pantos 2004, p. 194): Dingwall; Dingbell (Northumberland); *Dingil gate*, Somerby, Leics (1247); *Dings*, Hoby (1322); Dingley, Northants (*Dinglei/Tinglea* 1086, plausibly *þing* + *lēah*) [READ].
- Harding, "Wirral: Folklore and Locations", Casebook pp. 351–64 (pre-print PDF: https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/ncmh/documents/dna/brunanburh-locationschapter-may09.pdf) [READ]: Heswall Point (now "Sheldrakes") is the nearest accessible Dee shore from Cross Hill, the posited Thing site near the lost settlement of Warmby; Gayton ferry, Parkgate/Neston and Ness (Tudsbery 1907, p. 30) are alternatives; "the whole stretch of the Dee coast from Ness to Heswall Point could have been known … as Dingesmere"; Higden/Trevisa on the Dee's shifting channel; Bryant's 1831 map; a second escape route to Meols is traced through the 51 *carr* and 24 *holm* names (ON *kjarr* 'marsh', *holmr*) which lie almost all between Bebington and Meols (e.g. Lingham SJ 252910). Harding walked a posited retreat, Poulton Hall → Storeton woods → Cross Hill → Heswall Slack → Heswall Point, c. 12 km, in about six hours (January 2005).

### B.5 Disagreement *within* the Wirral camp about the water

- Livingston, *Never Greater Slaughter: Brunanburh and the Birth of England* (Oxford: Osprey, 2021), places the fleet in Wallasey Pool (the Mersey side), calling the Dee estuary "a profoundly ill-advised place to harbour an invasion fleet" (pp. 144–45) and having the retreat go to Wallasey Pool (p. 175) [VIA Cavill 2022 Escaping, which quotes these pages; book not read]. Wallasey Pool is also favoured by Wirral Archaeology's Dave Capener and Peter Jenkins (PHC 2020, p. 49) [READ].
- Cavill, "Escaping from Brunanburh and John of Worcester", *English Studies* 103 (2022), 899–919, DOI 10.1080/0013838X.2022.2067708 (open access) [READ]: the point of departure need not be the point of arrival; the poem distinguishes cohorts (Norsemen by ship, Constantine by land, *fleam* being flight over land); a Dee-side Dingesmere is compatible with a Mersey-side landing.
- Downham (PHC 2020, pp. 108–09) [READ] is more cautious than her co-authors: the OE personal name *Dynne* (four PASE examples) is a possible alternative first element; Thingwall "does not preside over a body of water"; Wallasey Pool remains possible.

### B.6 Standing objections to the Thingwall reading

Breeze: "despite the difficulties of phonology and sense" (2018a, p. 70), "far-fetched" (p. 74) [VIA Cavill 2023]. Halloran: 'noise' (2010, p. 253 n. 24). Deakin 2022: poetic sea-term [UNVERIFIED]. Wood 2013 pp. 143–44: dismisses as overstated [VIA Cavill]. The general point made by all critics is that the derivation requires several stacked assumptions (Gaelic voicing + secondary genitive + hybrid compound + *mere* = wetland) each of which is individually defensible but none independently attested for this name. The Wirral school's answer (Cavill 2014, 2023) is that no competitor has produced a *better* explanation of the *-g-* and the *-es-*, and that *þing*-names with initial D- are now well exemplified.

## C. Historical and strategic context claimed for Wirral

### C.1 Norse Wirral: Ingimund, Æthelflæd's Chester, Thingwall

- *Fragmentary Annals of Ireland* §429 (ed. J. Radner, 1978): Ingimund, expelled from Dublin (902) and beaten in Anglesey (cf. *Annales Cambriae* s.a. 902), was granted lands "near Chester" by Æthelflæd, later attacked Chester and was repulsed. PHC 2020, Section E, p. 134 [READ]: the assumption that the lands were "in the Wirral" is "not unreasonable, but it is unproven"; the Anglesey defeat is corroborated by *Annales Cambriae*; the Rollo/Normandy grant is the obvious comparison. Chester was "restored" by Æthelflæd in 907 (ASC, Mercian Register) after Viking attacks in 893 (ASC) and c. 906 (FA) [READ PHC].
- Archaeological and onomastic footprint of Norse Wirral (PHC 2020 Section E, pp. 134–39; Harding 2011) [READ]: Meols beach-market and Scandinavian-period finds (Griffiths, Philpott and Egan, *Meols: The Archaeology of the North Wirral Coast*, 2007); Irby ('farm of the Irish'); Moreton buildings; fifteen pre-Norman sculpture fragments from St Barnabas, Bromborough (found 1863); Neston crosses, West Kirby hogback, Hilbre, Overchurch runic stone, Woodchurch; a Viking silver ingot (24.55 g) from Ness (1995); a Neston strap-end (2007); a Stamford-ware sherd from Bromborough. Thingwall, one of only two Thingwall names in England, attests a Norse assembly (Harding 2011, p. 359 area) [READ].
- Dodgson 1957 argued the Norse population of Wirral would have been sympathetic to Anlaf's coalition [VIA PHC p. 114].

### C.2 The Dublin–Chester axis and the Chester mint

- Downham (PHC 2020, pp. 113–18, "Why the Wirral?") [READ]: "Dublin and Chester were the wealthiest ports in the Irish Sea region in this period with a regular trade between them, taking advantage of a well-established sailing route along the north Welsh coast and minimising the dangers of sailing across a large stretch of the Irish Sea. The route from the Wirral to Dublin is still in use today". Sitric of Dublin was at Davenport (Cheshire) in 920 (Mercian Register); Chester rebelled against Edward the Elder in 924, and Edward died at Farndon [READ PHC].
- David Griffiths, *Vikings of the Irish Sea* (Stroud: History Press, 2010) and *Meols* (2007): Meols as a haven for Scandinavian traders; no Meols coins of 915–50; a Chester lead customs tag found at Coppergate, York (Eadwig) [VIA PHC and Harding; Griffiths not read — UNVERIFIED pages].
- Chester mint: under Æthelstan Chester had "at least 25 moneyers … probably as many as 20 striking at any one time, compared with 10 in London and 7 in Winchester" (VCH Chester vol. 5.1, "Early medieval Chester 400–1230", British History Online, https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/ches/vol5/pt1/pp16-33; cf. J. D. A. Geddes, "The Chester Mint", *THSLC* 107) [READ BHO; Geddes VIA]. Blunt, "The Coinage of Athelstan", *BNJ* 42 (1974), pp. 89, 92: no break in York's output around 937 [VIA PHC/Wood — UNVERIFIED].

### C.3 Æthelstan's aims and the coalition's objective

- Cavill 2014, pp. 97–99 [READ]: there is "a profound improbability" in a Dublin fleet sailing round Scotland to the Humber to meet allies who could have met it in the west; John of Worcester's "entered the mouth of the Humber" is his formula for any invasion (parallel: Tostig and Harald in 1066; Woolf 2007, p. 171), and JW gives >20 examples of the formula (Cavill 2022 Escaping, pp. 913–14); *Annals of Ulster* 937/938 record only Anlaf's return to Dublin ("Amhlaiph m. Gothfrith i nAth Cliath iterum"). Livingston (pers. comm. to Cavill, Aug. 2011) noted Anlaf moved from the Lough Ree campaign in central Ireland to Brunanburh "in less than two months". Cavill: "The goal of the coalition was Winchester, not York"; William of Malmesbury says the invader *multum in Angliam processerat*; cf. Foot, "Where English becomes British", in Barrow and Wareham (eds), *Myth, Rulership, Church and Charters* (2008), pp. 127–44, at 133 [VIA Cavill].
- Cavill 2022 Yorkshire [READ]: Carlisle–Chester by Roman roads (Margary 7/70) is c. 200 miles, about the same as Carlisle–Burghwallis; a western route runs through Cumbrian (allied) territory, easing provisioning; a Wirral rendezvous opens Margary 6/41/43 from Chester towards Winchester and is "more threatening to Athelstan" than a Yorkshire one; Ireland to north-west England is at most five days' sail; Ribble/Mersey to York is c. 80 miles; Plummer (*Two of the Saxon Chronicles Parallel*, II, 139–40): "such a spot must be sought in the west of England"; no early source mentions York in connection with the battle; the St Cuthbert community, whose lands straddled the north-east, is silent; Castleford's Chronicle refers to the 934 campaign, not 937.

### C.4 Routes proposed by Wirral advocates

- **Higham 1997.** N. J. Higham, "The Context of Brunanburh", in A. R. Rumble and A. D. Mills (eds), *Names, Places and People* (Stamford: Paul Watkins, 1997), pp. 144–56: the fleet may have moored as far north as the Ribble (p. 153); *othlynn* = *oð Lynne* 'up to the Lyme' (p. 152 n. 66); escape via Meols (p. 155); critique of the Humber (p. 144 n. 6) [VIA PHC 2020 p. 50 and Cavill — Higham not read; page numbers UNVERIFIED].

### C.5 "Over deep water to Dublin"

The poem's *ofer deop wæter Difelin secan* implies a sea-crossing to Dublin by ship directly from *Dingesmere*. From the Dee estuary or Mersey the crossing follows the north-Welsh-coast route to Dublin still in use (Downham above); *Annals of Ulster* 938 confirms Anlaf reached Dublin. Cavill 2022 Escaping (pp. 899–919) argues that "the Norse escaped by ship" while the Scots fled overland, and that on Wirral "one is never more than four miles from navigable waters" [READ]. A Humber departure would require Anlaf to sail round Scotland to reach Dublin (Cavill 2014 p. 98) — or, on Wood's reading, to winter in Scotland (Wood 2013 pp. 144, 146, 149 [VIA Cavill 2022 Yorkshire]).

### C.6 Egil's saga and Vínheiðr, as used and disowned

- Early Wirral advocates (Tudsbery, *Brunanburh A.D. 937* (1907); Mathews) used Egil's saga; Harding 2011 (Casebook, Harding chapter, p. 353) explicitly calls it one of the "unreliable sources" and notes the tradition [READ]. Kelly (Casebook, p. 210) suggested the saga's fortified town south of the battlefield echoes Chester [VIA Cavill 2014 n.]. Cavill 2011 §3.9 discusses Townend's view that assimilation of *Vína* to *Wen-* is "problematic, but … not inconceivable" [READ]; Cavill's later "Scandinavian Vína" argument (Casebook pp. 357–61 area) denies a York link [VIA summary — page UNVERIFIED].
- Downham (PHC 2020, pp. 109–10) [READ]: lists five discrepancies between the saga and the tenth-century sources (after Grant 2019) and concludes the saga has "such a tenuous relationship with real events" that it cannot locate the battle; Townend shows *Vína–Wen* implausible. In short, the current Wirral case does **not** rest on the saga; it is the Yorkshire school (Wood) that uses it (see E.2).

### D.2 The independent assessment (Sherman, Downham, Philpott 2020/2022)

*The Wirral Archaeology "Search for the Battle of Brunanburh" Project: An Initial Assessment of the Project to Date, on behalf of Wirral Borough Council*, by PHC Services (Paul Sherman, with Clare Downham and Robert Philpott), July 2020; redacted version issued March 2022; 155 pp. URLs: https://www.wirral.gov.uk/files/ecc31-search-battle-brunanburh-june-2021.pdf ; https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3150392/ [READ: Sections A, B, C, E, F, G, I and appendices]. This is the only independent expert assessment located; **no separate Historic England assessment, HER entry, excavation report or Register listing exists** (Historic England was a consultee of the council). Findings:

- **Method and records.** Detecting "concentrated almost entirely on one field"; no maps or written methodology; c. 4,000 finds, c. 1,600 removed as modern, 2,400 remaining, of which 335 (Group 1) and a random 124 (Group 2) were examined, 1,941 unexamined; over 60% iron (Section B, pp. 55–65).
- **Diagnostic early-medieval finds.** Philpott: "A small number of finds are diagnostically early medieval": strap-end WA21 (Thomas Type B, mid-8th–11th c.; "No GPS available"); strap-end plate WA9980 (label SJ 32884 82223, 06 02 20); gaming pieces and lead weights with Torksey parallels (WA9988, WA36 etc.); spindle whorls WA56/57; certain arrowheads WA20b, WA2415 (Jessop T1/T3 hybrid), WA42; an Abbasid dirham of Madinat al-Salam 159 AH (775/6) (identified by Jani Oravisjärvi) (pp. 56–65, 75–79).
- **Downgrades.** "Despite claims by Wirral archaeology that the sheet is rune inscribed, analysis … by runic specialists Dr Andrea Freund and Dr Maja Bäckvall confirm that there are no discernible runes" (lead plaque WA93/94; p. 60 area); WA54's possible rune is at best one character; WA9956 is not a sword blade after X-ray; WA1853 is a cast-iron pipe, "definitely not a sword pommel"; WA165, a lead 'sword pommel', may be a pattern and needs a specialist; "nothing … unequivocally identified as a spearhead"; tanged 'blanks' are more likely reamers; "X-ray analysis has reduced considerably the number initially suspected as weapons"; the single bone submitted is a modern sheep metacarpal; other coins are an Edward I penny, a Byzantine aspron trachy and a Charles I half-groat (pp. 56–65) [READ].
- **Findspots.** "The diagnostic early medieval finds are in almost all cases unlocated. This is highly regrettable … It undermines the claims of Wirral Archaeology to have found the battle site" (p. 65); Section G, p. 144: "almost total lack of findspot information for the most important diagnostic early medieval finds".
- **Philpott's conclusions (p. 98, verbatim):** (1) "the small quantity of confirmed weaponry, which has been reduced as a result of X-ray analysis, does not provide secure evidence of the battle site by itself"; (2) "The amount of securely dated 10th-century material is limited but it is significant in a north-west context. The ironworking tools, material and waste could be associated with the battle but there is nothing intrinsically military about this part of the assemblage"; (3) "The finds do not represent conclusive proof that the battle of Brunanburh took place within the area of mid Wirral, but they are consistent with that interpretation"; (4) "Conclusive proof … requires further corroborative evidence, which might include dated and diagnostic early medieval finds, a larger assemblage of battle-related items, and/or securely dated archaeological deposits of the relevant period."
- **Section G (pp. 140–44):** the assemblage "clearly meets the requirements of an unusual concentration of early medieval finds, certainly in a regional context"; but "the lack of spears, swords and scarcity of arrowheads means this is not conclusive"; some thick billets look "more like steam-pressed than hand-wrought iron"; claimed Roman-road alignments "remain unverified hearsay". Section F: evidence of both smelting and smithing implies activity at different times, limiting the Fulford 'recycling camp' comparison.
- **Section I:** potential for Register listing exists, but "there is not unanimous agreement within academia"; "The main drawback … is not the lack of material but rather the lack of proper location and context records and the inadequate standards of preservation and storage".
- **Sherman (p. 51):** the finds "could well fit … as being the site of a potential Saxon camp close to the edge of the battle field as proposed by Dave Capener"; WA "do not claim that their main find site … is actually the battle site".
- The report was "compiled during the COVID-19 lockdown … must therefore be considered as an interim" (p. 100).

### D.5 Status, 2024–2026

The battle is not on Historic England's Register of Historic Battlefields (earliest entries Maldon 991, Hastings 1066).

## E. The case against Bromborough

### E.1 Andrew Breeze (Lanchester, Co. Durham)

**Works.** "The battle of Brunanburh and Welsh tradition", *Neophilologus* 83 (1999), 479–82; Society of Antiquaries lecture, 4 Dec. 2014, "Brunanburh in 937: Bromborough or Lanchester?" (discussed on Tim Clarkson's *Senchus* blog, 9 Dec. 2014) [READ blog]; "The battle of Brunanburh and Cambridge, CCC, MS 183", *Northern History* 53 (2016), 138–45 (the gospel-book given to St Cuthbert's community after the victory implies Æthelstan was near Chester-le-Street); "Brunanburh located: the battle and the poem", in M. Ogura and H. Sauer (eds), *Aspects of Medieval English Language and Literature* (Frankfurt: Peter Lang, 2018), pp. 61–80 (= 2018a); "The names of Yorkshire's rivers", *TYDS* 23 (2018), 79–92; *British Battles 493–937: Mount Badon to Brunanburh* (London: Anthem Press, 2020; pbk 2021), Brunanburh chapter pp. 121–27. All VIA Cavill 2023 (READ) and Pickles 2023 (READ); Breeze's texts not read.

**Arguments** (as quoted by Cavill 2023 with Breeze's page numbers):
1. *Annales Cambriae* *Bellum Brune* "shows that the form is a toponym, not a personal name" (2018a: 61); the Welsh article in *y Brune* proves *Brune* "was no longer perceived as a river-name" (70); the River Browney "was once called Brune" (63).
2. The Roman fort at Lanchester (Longovicium) was a *burh*: "If it is objected that the form is nowhere elsewhere recorded, we may remember that study of County Durham's early place-names is problematic" (73).
3. *Weondun* = *wenn* 'tumour' + *dūn*, with back-mutation, describing the hill of Lanchester (73).
4. The Thingwall etymology of *Dingesmere* is held "despite the difficulties of phonology and sense" (70) and is "far-fetched" (74); emend to *on dingles mere* (74).
5. John of Worcester's Humber entry is accepted.
6. Strategic: "Partisans of Bromborough will find it hard to explain … the supposed return home by sea from Cheshire of a Scottish army in England: an occurrence with no parallel"; "By entering a peninsula off the road north, the Scots and Strathclyders would put their necks in a noose. Without marine transport, they would be trapped" (2018a: 67, 71; quoted Cavill 2022 Escaping).
7. No source connects the battle with Wirral or Chester.

**Rebuttals.** Cavill 2023 [READ]: the Browney is *Brun* (strong; *Brune* c. 1190, *Brun* c. 1195 Finchale; Ekwall 1960 p. 70; Watts 2002 p. 19), genitive *Brunes-*, so it "cannot therefore have given the weak oblique form Brunan-"; no evidence Lanchester was ever called *Brunanburh* ("depends on a complete lack of supporting evidence, as he acknowledges"); back-mutation "almost certainly did not occur" in *wenn-dūn*; *dingle* is Middle English and West-Midland; Welsh interest in the battle suggests proximity to Wales, and the St Cuthbert community's own records (*Historia de sancto Cuthberto*; *Cronica monasterii Dunelmensis*; *Annales Lindisfarnenses et Dunelmenses*) never mention a battle in their territory; Lanchester is c. 17 miles from the sea, yet Anlaf fled by ship. Cavill 2022 Escaping [READ]: few "partisans of Bromborough" say the Scots fled by sea; Wirral is "never more than four miles from navigable waters"; the coalition had a strategy (Pickin's pincer), not a death-wish; Æthelstan in any case did not or could not prevent escape. Pickles, *Northern History* 60:1 (2023), 117–18 (https://chesterrep.openrepository.com/handle/10034/627517) [READ]: contrasts "Paul Cavill's careful elucidation" with Breeze's; the book "is about the etymology of names" and does not treat history or archaeology. Breeze never cites the Casebook (Cavill 2022 n. 12; S. Morillo, TMR review 2021 [VIA]). Clarkson (Senchus 2014) and a commenter (Chris Pickles) found Lanchester's "steep sided and narrow valley" unlike a battle-plain [READ blog].

### E.2 Michael Wood (the Humber / South Yorkshire case)

**Works.** "Brunanburh Revisited", *Saga-Book* 20 (1980), 200–17 (Brinsworth/White Hill); *In Search of England* (1999), pp. 203–21; "Searching for Brunanburh: The Yorkshire Context of the 'Great War' of 937", *Yorkshire Archaeological Journal* 85 (2013), 138–59 (abstract READ via Taylor & Francis; body VIA Cavill 2022 Yorkshire nn. 5–17); "The spelling of Brunanburh", *N&Q* 64:3 (2017), 365–69 [VIA Cavill 2022 Spellings]; *The Times*, 21 Nov. 2017; Society of Antiquaries lecture, Nov. 2020; Citadel Today interview, 14 Oct. 2021 [READ].

**Arguments** (2013, page numbers as cited by Cavill):
1. "a consensus has grown over the last few years that it should be located in the Wirral … the battle probably took place south of York … A new location is proposed near the River Went, whose name … is contained in an alternative Northumbrian name for the battle, Wendun" (abstract, verbatim) [READ].
2. York was the coalition's goal (pp. 140, 142); Anlaf's 938–39 return to York fits a Humber exit and wintering in Scotland (pp. 144, 146, 149).
3. John of Worcester's Humber entry "cannot be John's own invention" and derives from "a set of tenth-century northern annals from York" (pp. 147–49); "If the invaders landed in the Humber they cannot have left from the Dee" (pp. 149–50, 154).
4. The *Historia Regum* annal is "a tenth-century annal from Chester-le-Street" (pp. 141, 146–49, 155) and *Weondun/Wendune* is a real Northumbrian name = Went Hill (pp. 155–57; *Wenet* 1154×89, *Wente* 1190; Barnsdale Bar).
5. Castleford's Chronicle and Egil's saga (pp. 152 n. 73, 153–54, 158 n. 96); Northumbrian submission (pp. 146, 149, 152–54); coins (p. 154).
6. "how could the site of so momentous a battle have been so completely forgotten?" (p. 143); Bromborough "will rest entirely on uncertain and overstated onomastic evidence" (p. 154); Bromborough's name "first appears … first half of the twelfth century" (p. 149); 2017: two places, Burghwallis = 'burh at the spring'.

**Rebuttals.** Cavill 2022 Yorkshire [READ]: no early source mentions York; Castleford's Chronicle refers to the 934 Scottish campaign; the St Cuthbert community is silent; no form of Burghwallis resembles *Brun(n)anburh*; Went Hill is *Weneteshil* (1180–1200), a *hyll* not a *dūn*, and Wood ignores the *Weon-* spelling; a "land invasion" is not in evidence. Cavill 2022 Spellings [READ]: *-nn-* spellings are southern scribal variants. Cavill 2022 Escaping [READ]: JW's phrase is formulaic (>20 instances); JW never says the invaders returned to their ships from the Humber; Langtoft is the first to write "Bruneburge sur Humbre"; a Danish tradition (Langtoft/Mannyng) confuses matters. Downham (PHC pp. 103–04; THSLC 2021 pp. 21, 23 [VIA]): all Humber references trace to JW, who "added details … based on his own deductions" with "geographical naivety"; Fulford confusion. Cavill 2014 pp. 97–99: circumnavigation improbability.

### E.3 Kevin Halloran (Burnswark, Dumfriesshire)

"The Brunanburh Campaign: A Reappraisal", *SHR* 84 (2005), 133–48 (abstract READ: questions "the reliability of the variant form Brunanburh as a guide to the battle's location" and finds "the most likely site was Burnswark in Annandale"; p. 146 n. 84 assumes Anlaf and the Northmen embarked together [VIA Cavill]); "The Identity of Etbrunnanwerc", *SHR* 89 (2010), 248–53 (the *burh* form adopted "for poetic purposes", p. 248 n. 3; "Wendune rather than Weondune is the correct form and refers to the hill of Burnswark", p. 250; a Brittonic *brïnn* etymology via A. ### E.9 The objections in summary

1. **No early source ties the battle to Wirral, Chester or Mercia.** The ASC poem gives only *Brunanburh* and *Dingesmere*; the Mercian Register is silent; the twelfth-century northern tradition (HR/Symeon) supplies *Weondun/Wendune* and *Brunnanwerc*, which the Wirral case explains only by Coates's *Welondrys* (slight) or by treating them as paraphrase; JW places the landing in the Humber (though formulaically).
2. **Strategic geography.** Constantine's and Owain's marches to a peninsula in the far south-west of the Northumbrian–Mercian frontier seem odd if York was the prize (Wood; McGuigan; Breeze). The Wirral reply is that Winchester/Mercia, not York, was the objective and that the west route ran through allied Cumbria (Cavill 2014, 2022; Downham 2021).
3. **Forgotten site.** Wood's question — why would so momentous a battle leave no local tradition in Cheshire before Dodgson? — is answered only with the Court House earthwork and late folklore.
4. **Onomastic caution.** Same-name ≠ same-place (Campbell, Dodgson, Halloran, Wood, Williams); *Dingesmere* requires several stacked hypotheses (B.6).
5. **Archaeology.** No located, published, diagnostic battle assemblage exists; the group has withdrawn its claim (D.5).
6. **Anlaf's later career.** His acceptance at York in 939 and Constantine's return north are used to argue an eastern theatre; the Wirral school counters that AU records only his return to Dublin and that York's coinage shows no break (Blunt).

## 6. Balance

### 6.1 Strongest points FOR Bromborough

1. **The name.** Bromborough is the only English place-name whose recorded medieval forms (*Brunburg/Bruneburgh* 1100–59) derive regularly from OE *Brūnanburh*; the personal name *Brūna* is rare (four names in England, three on Wirral); rival candidates have no attested *Brunanburh* form at all (A.1–A.3). This is agreed even by opponents (Wood 2013 p. 154; Williams 2021).
2. **Dingesmere has a philologically respectable explanation** (þing of Thingwall + Gaelic voicing + secondary genitive + *mere* 'wetland') that accounts for the persistent *-g-* and *-es-* which every rival etymology fails to explain (B.3–B.4), and Thingwall lies two miles from a real estuarine wetland.
3. **Context.** Wirral was densely Norse-Irish, close to the Norse assembly place, on the Dublin–Chester sea-lane, within reach of Chester's mint and Roman road hub, and reachable from Dublin in days; a coalition aiming at Mercia/Wessex rather than York makes strategic sense there (C.1–C.4; Downham 2021).
4. **Escape by sea to Dublin** is straightforward from the Dee/Mersey; from the Humber it is not (C.5).
5. **Weak but suggestive local correlates**: Court House earthwork, *Welondrys*/Rice Wood and Harrow Fields (*wēoh*, *hearg*) for *Weondun*, sculpture at Bromborough church (A.1, A.3, C.1).
6. **Archaeology is at least consistent**: an "unusual concentration of early medieval finds, certainly in a regional context" including Anglo-Scandinavian gaming pieces, weights, a strap-end and some arrowheads (D.2).

### 6.2 Strongest points AGAINST

1. **Silence of the sources.** Nothing before Dodgson (1957) connects the battle with Cheshire; the only medieval geographical pointer (JW's Humber) points east, and the northern battle-names *Weondun/Wendune*, *Brunnanwerc* and Æthelweard's *Brunandune* have no secure Wirral referent.
2. **The same-name objection is logically valid**: a *Brunanburh* that existed in 937 could have vanished (Williams; Downham herself concedes the possibility while calling it "surprising").
3. **Dingesmere is a hypothesis-stack**; and the Wirral camp itself cannot agree which water it names (Dee at Heswall; Meols; Wallasey Pool; or a personal name *Dynne*) (B.5).
4. **Strategy.** A Scottish–Cumbrian army marching 200 miles to a peninsula off the road north, if the aim was Northumbria, is a genuine puzzle (McGuigan, Breeze, Wood); the Wirral answer requires accepting that Wessex/Mercia was the target, which no source states.
5. **No decisive archaeology.** After fifteen years of detecting, the assemblage is unlocated, partly downgraded on X-ray, unpublished, and its finders now disclaim identification of the battlefield; the 2023 isotope pilot proves nothing about the battle; the 2025 protocol is a promise (D).
6. **Reception.** Bromborough is the majority view in specialist onomastics and among several historians (Foot cautiously; Downham; Livingston), but the Battlefields Trust and Historic England treat the battle as unlocated, and a minority of active scholars (Wood, Breeze, Halloran, Deakin) continue to dissent in print.

### 6.3 Honest assessment

The Wirral case is the best-documented of all candidates and the only one anchored to a genuine, regularly derived medieval name-form; the strategic and contextual arguments are coherent and were strengthened by Downham (2020–21) and Cavill (2022–23), whose rebuttals of Wood and Breeze are, on the evidence read here, decisive on the philological points. But "best candidate" is not "proven": every strand of the positive case is inferential, the sources that would corroborate it (a Mercian or Cheshire notice; a securely located tenth-century battle assemblage) do not exist, and the Wirral school's own principal scholars (Cavill 2014: "may not yet be unassailable"; Philpott 2020: "consistent with … not conclusive proof"; Downham 2020: "very plausible") stop short of certainty. A fair probability judgement would put Bromborough well ahead of any single rival while leaving a substantial residual that the battle was fought at an unrecorded *Brunanburh* elsewhere. The decisive test remains archaeological: a gridded, GPS-recorded survey and dated deposits in the Bebington–Brimstage–Storeton area, or a positive isotope signature on securely tenth-century weaponry.
