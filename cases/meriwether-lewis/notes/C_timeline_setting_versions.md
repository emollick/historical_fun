**Horseback plausibility check (summary).** Fort Pickering → Grinder's is c. 150 straight-line miles and roughly 230–245 road miles by the Trace (Fort Pickering–Agency c. 100 road miles per Ambrose/Merritt, or 120 per Colbert via Nutt in D14; Agency–Grinder's 144.5 parkway miles). Eleven travelling days (29 Sept–10 Oct) less the two rest days = nine days, i.e. c. 26–27 miles a day, which is ordinary for a mounted party with pack horses. Every dated anchor (29 Sept departure; c. 2 Oct arrival in the nation; two days' rest; river crossing c. 8–9 Oct; camp one day beyond; Grinder's at sunset 10 Oct) is mutually consistent. The gaps where the record is silent are: the exact route between the fort and the river (Agency or not), the dates 3–8 Oct, the hour of Neelly's arrival on 11 Oct, and 12–17 Oct.

## 2. The physical setting of Grinder's Stand

### 2.1 The Grinders and the date of the stand

* Name: "Grinder" in every 1809–1813 document; the family signed "Griner" (Coues 1893 footnote on Joel P. Morrison's letter of 9 July 1899: "From this letter it appears that the name was not Grinder, but Griner, and that his place was near a creek of the same name"; Moore 1904; TNGenWeb: "ROBERT EVANS GRINDER signed his name 'GRINER'"). Guice (WPO 2002 n. 1): "The name was actually Griner, but because it is spelled Grinder in most historical accounts, Grinder is used here."
* Persons: Robert Evans Griner/Grinder and Priscilla Knight. Family genealogy (Holmes 2013, `sources/setting/web_2013_family_history_daily_holmes_griner_ancestors.txt`): "Robert Evans Griner (1767_1827) eloped with Priscilla Knight (1774-1848)"; TNGenWeb: Robert Evans Grinder "son of JOSHUA GRINDER of Stokes County, North Carolina. His wife was PRISCILLA KNIGHT, also of Stokes Co., N.C." (unverified family/local sources). The Open Library highlight of Starrs & Gale 2009 (scan p. 422) also has "Griner/Grinder died in 1827". Spence 1900 names the sons "William, John, Albert, Robert, Jr., and the twin brothers, Hulett and Noble" and says "Albert and Noble, in fits of insanity, committed suicide by hanging".
* When built: Spence, History of Hickman County (1900), pp. 49-50: "About 1807 the upper end of the bend was settled by Robert Griner, Sr., near the west end of the railroad trestle. He obtained water from the 'blowing spring' near the ford of the river. The next year he moved out on the Natchez Trace, and had an inn near where the Lewis monument now stands. This was then in Hickman County, but is now in Lewis County". Holmes 2013 (family tradition) says "about 1800" and that the army made them move an earlier cabin as "in violation of the treaty". NPS pages give no founding date. **[INFERENCE]** 1808 (Spence) is the best-supported date; the stand was therefore about a year old when Lewis arrived.
* Indian country or Tennessee? The 1809 texts disagree: Clarion: "the house of Mr Grinder near the Indian line in this state"; Wilson: "seventy-two miles from Nashville ... the last white man's as you enter the Indian country"; Russell 1811: "the house of a Mr. Grinder within the jurisdiction of Tennissee"; Jacob 1859 (Gass biography, in Coues): "just within the Chickasaw nation, near the Tennessee line"; the 1848 committee: "on the borders of the Indian nation"; Pease 1894: "Being in the Indian nation, there was no grand jury or examining magistrate". NPS CLI: Grinder's Inn "was said to be the 'last white settlement before entering Indian Territory in the southwest.'" **[INFERENCE]** the stand stood on or just inside the Tennessee side of the 1806 Chickasaw boundary; the sources do not allow more precision, and the "no magistrate" claim is Pease's, not a record.

### 2.2 Buildings and distances as first described

* **Neelly, 18 Oct 1809**: "no person there but a woman who discovering the governor to be deranged gave him up the house & slept herself in one near it, his Servant and mine Slept in the Stable loft some distance from the other houses".
* **Brahan, 18 Oct 1809**: "gave him up the house, and Slept herself in another house near it—the two Servants Slept in a Stable loft Some distance off".
* **Clarion, 20 Oct 1809**: "Mrs Grinder retired to the kitchen with her children and the servants ... went to a stable about three hundred yards distant to sleep—no one in the house with the governor".
* **Nashville letter, 18 Oct 1809 (NI)**: "the woman of the house, who slept in a house near the other".
* **Russell, 26 Nov 1811**: "he went to bed in a cabin by himself and ordered the servants to go to the stables ... after every body had retired in a seperate Building"; later "went to the house where Mrs. Grinder and her children were lying".
* **Wilson, 1811 (Port Folio 1812)** – the fullest: "it being now dusk the woman went off to the kitchen, and the two men to the barn, which stands about two hundred yards off. The kitchen is only a few paces from the room where Lewis was"; "The logs being open, and unplastered, she saw him stagger back and fall against a stump that stands between the kitchen and room. He crawled for some distance, raised himself by the side of a tree, where he sat about a minute. He once more got to the room; afterwards he came to the kitchen door, but did not speak; she then heard him scraping the bucket with a gourd for water"; the grave "close by the common path, with a few loose rails thrown over his grave"; Wilson "gave Grinder money to put a post fence round it, to shelter it from the hogs, and from the wolves". So: guest room/house, a separate kitchen "a few paces" away with a stump between them, a tree nearby, a barn/stable 200 (Wilson) or 300 (Clarion) yards off, the Trace ("the common path") beside the grave. No other structure is mentioned in 1809–1811.
* **Wright 1876/1894** (from 1876 informants): "The house was a double log-cabin, with a hall between the two rooms; Governor Lewis occupied one of the rooms, Mrs. Grinder the other."
* **Park 1891** (in Coues): the site "marked by the ruins of a stone chimney, a mound of earth, and the remains of a garden"; the grave "North of the site of the old house, and about 150 yards from it, on the east side of the road".
* **Pease 1894**: "Near the road and some few yards distant from the monument is an old pit, as though it had been a cellar, now nearly leveled, with two piles of crumbling stone; marking the location of a settler's house of an early day with the inevitable rock chimneys at either end." (His "four or five rooms", porch and rag carpet are romance.)
* **Bakeless 1947**: "the kitchen door, some twelve feet away" (composite; no source given).
* **Daniels 1962** (Open Library highlight): "log houses, unplastered and unchinked, and a barn 100 yards away".
* "Chinks": the word does not occur in any 1809–1812 text. Wilson's phrase is "The logs being open, and unplastered". Later writers paraphrase it as "unchinked" (Daniels 1962; Jenkinson 2011: "his unchinked quarters"), "peer between the chinks in her door" (K. Moore, HNN 2004), "peeked through the lodge [logs] of the unshanked [unchinked] house" (Danisi talk transcript, `sources/scholarship/danisi_missouri_archives_talk_transcript.txt`, l. 174), and Guice 2002: "through the cracks of her log cabin with the door bolted shut".

### 2.3 What the site is now (NPS)

Sources: NPS Cultural Landscapes Inventory (`sources/setting/nps_cli_...txt`), Environmental Assessment April 2002 (`sources/setting/nps_2002_environmental_assessment_...txt`), NPS web pages (`sources/setting/nps_web_pages_...txt`), National Parks Traveler 27 Nov 2009, Daily Progress 27 Jun 2010.

* Location: "Meriwether Lewis Death and Burial Site, milepost 385.9 on the Natchez Trace Parkway, near Hohenwald, TN" (NPS place page). EA 2002: "The burial site is located north/northeast of the site of Grinders Stand and east of the Old Natchez Trace."
* Remains of the stand: "Unfortunately there are no known drawings or descriptions of what Grinder's Stand look like. The house itself was destroyed before the state of Tennessee erected the monument to Meriwether Lewis in 1848. All that is left of the stand is hearth stones just past the Grinder's Stand sign" (NPS place page). EA 2002 §3.13.4: "A sign installed during the War Department era identifies the turf-covered chimney stones north of the cabin as the site of the former Grinders Stand." CLI: "Excavations of the Grinder's Inn took place in 1933, though only a few nails and the chimney foundation were uncovered"; "A General Management Plan written in 1987 called for archeology and the location of the Grinder's Inn was found in 1992"; chronology entry "CE 1992 - 1998 ... Archeologists conduct a series of surveys at the site to locate the site of Grinder's Inn. The archeology includes, shovel test pits, ground-penetrating radar, and remote sensing." (The SEAC report itself was not obtained; see the end list.)
* The log "cabin" visitors see is not a replica: EA 2002 §3.13.3: "The monument headquarters cabin is a small log cabin located near the site of Grinders Stand. The cabin was constructed during the CCC era. The cabin which is often mistaken as replica of Grinders Stand serves as Park Ranger office". CLI: "A log office-museum built in 1935 resides near the site of the Grinder's Inn". (The 2002 WPO caption calls it a "speculative reconstruction of the main cabin at Grinder's Stand ... completed in 1935. The original was probably less finely built.")
* Monument and grave: 1848–49, Lemuel W. Kirby of Columbia, "broken column"; "approximately 20 feet high" (EA); committee report: "twenty and a half feet". CLI: "In 1849, the land surrounding the monument was sold for use as a cemetery. The oldest extant grave marker dates to 1856"; EA: "In August 1849 one-acre of land surrounding the Meriwether Lewis monument was conveyed by deed for use as cemetery. The gravesites of approximately 108 local residents are located south of the monument in an area referred to as pioneer cemetery". NPS Pioneer Cemetery page: "roughly 100 burials"; NPS to the Daily Progress 2010: "109 other graves, including some that have yet to be located".
* Later alterations: 1925 Meriwether Lewis National Monument (Coolidge proclamation; 50 acres from Clint Moore and the county's one-acre tract); War Department 1926–33 (flush marble markers replace gravestones; "The monument was set on a concrete base with 20 cannonballs lining the perimeter to replace the iron fence"; 250 acres "including the site of Grinders Stand" added 1927); NPS from 1933; "the addition of four feet of fill to the ellipse in 1935 during regrading of the site ... The fill covered the original Natchez Trace nearest the monument"; 1961 transferred to the Natchez Trace Parkway (P.L. 87-131); 1975 National Register; "In 2000, the monument was rehabilitated following the recommendations of a 1998 Historic Structure Report. The column, plinth, and base were dismantled and a new foundation raised the location of the monument vertically. In 2001, new marble curbs were added"; ARRA project $3.2 million announced 12 Nov 2009 ("rehabilitation of the interpretive cabin, new interpretive and wayside exhibits ... improvements to roads and site parking"), work "by spring of 2010"; DAR marker placed 10 Nov 2006. The 900-acre "developed site" figure is NPS's (ARRA release).
* **[INFERENCE]** Because of the 1935 fill and the 2000 re-founding on concrete, the grave now lies under c. 4 ft of twentieth-century fill and a concrete/stone base — the practical objection the NPS raised against exhumation in 2009–10.

## 3. Every version of the Grinder story, in order of first record

For each: date of the telling / of first print, provenance chain, and the verbatim core. Full texts are in the files named.

**V1. James Neelly to Jefferson, Nashville, 18 Oct 1809** (`sources/1809-10-18_neelly_to_jefferson.md`; in Brahan's hand; carried by Pernier; received 21 Nov). Provenance: Neelly was not present; he reports "the woman" and the servants. Core: "the woman reports that about three OClock She heard two pistols fire off in the Governors Room: the Servants being awakined by her, came in but too late to save him. he had shot himself in the head with one pistol & a little below the Breast with the other—when his Servant came in he Says; I have done the business my good Servant give me Some water. he gave him water, he survived but a short time". Effects: "his Rifle, Silver watch, & Brace of Pistols ... a Dirk & Tomahawk" (with N; trunks with Neelly; two trunks with Russell). Same-day parallel: Brahan (V1b): "about three oClock the woman heard two pistols fire off. being alarmed She went & waked the servants when they came in they found him weltering in his blood. he had shot himself first it was thought in the head. the ball did not take effect. the other Shot was a little below his breast, which proved Mortal: he lived until Sun rise & expired"; and the anonymous Nashville letter of 18 Oct in the National Intelligencer (V1c): "About three o'clock, the woman of the house, who slept in a house near the other, heard two pistols fired. She awoke the servants, who pushed into the room, and found the unfortunate Governor weltering in his blood. He had shot himself in the head, and just below his heart; he died in about three hours."

**V2. Nashville Democratic Clarion, 20 Oct 1809** (earliest print found: Kentucky Gazette 31 Oct 1809; continued in the Enquirer 17 Nov and Alexandria Gazette 20 Nov; `sources/setting/loc_*`). Provenance: unstated ("we have received"); differs from Neelly on the time and on razor wounds, so **[INFERENCE]** it drew on Neelly's or Pernier's talk in Nashville rather than on Neelly's letter. Core (OCR, Kentucky Gazette): "On the night of the 10th instant Meriwether Lewis Esq governor-general of Upper Louisiana on his way to Washington City came to the house of Mr Grinder near the Indian line in this state—called for his supper and some spirits of which he partook and gave some to his servants. Mr Grinder not being at home Mrs Grinder retired to the kitchen with her children and the servants (after the governor went to bed which he did in good order) went to a stable about three hundred yards distant to sleep—no one in the house with the governor—and some time before midnight Mrs Grinder was alarmed by the firing of two pistols in the house—she called to the servants without effect—and at the appearance of daylight the servants came to the house, when the governor said he had now time for himself ... and would die, and requested them to bring him water, he then lying on the floor, where he expired about 7 o'clock in the morning of the 11th. he had shot a ball that grazed the top of his head and another through his intestines, and cut his neck, arm and ham with a razor ... spoke about a trunk of papers ... under the influence of a deranging malady for about six weeks". The Russellville letter of 20 Oct (Alexandria Gazette 20 Nov): "shot himself twice, and then cut his throat" and "cut the arteries in his thighs and arms" – hearsay from a traveller.

**V3. Gilbert C. Russell**, letters of 4 and 31 Jan 1810 and statement of 26 Nov 1811 (`sources/1810-01-31_russell_to_jefferson.md`; statement as reprinted WPO 28:1 p. 14 from Jackson 2:573-75). Provenance: Russell was at Fort Pickering; his knowledge of the night is second-hand ("from the Statement of Grinders wife"). 31 Jan 1810: "from the Statement of Grinders wife where he Killed himself I can not help beleiving that Purney was rather Aiding & abeting in the murder than otherwise"; money: "the Govr had more than One hund $ in Notes & Specia besides a Check I let him have of 99 58/100 none of which it is said could be found". 1811 statement: "he went to bed in a cabin by himself and ordered the servants to go to the stables and take care of the Horses, least they might loose some that night; Some time in the night he got his pistols which he loaded, after every body had retired in a seperate Building and discharged one against his forehead without much effect—the ball not penetrating the skull but only making a furrow over it. He then discharged the other against his breast where the ball entered and passing downward thro' his body came out low down near his back bone. After some time he got up and went to the house where Mrs. Grinder and her children were lying and asked for water, but her husband being absent and having heard the report of the pistols she was greatly allarmed and made him no answer. He then in returning got his razors from a port folio which happened to contain them and siting up in his bed was found about day light, by one of the servants, busily engaged in cuting himself from head to foot." The statement ends (as quoted by Chuinard 1991 and Jenkinson 2011): "he lay down and died with the declaration to the Boy that he had killed himself to deprive his enemies of the pleasure and honor of doing it."

**V4. Alexander Wilson, letter to Alexander Lawson**, dated "Natchez, May 28th, 1811" in the Port Folio (Jan 1812, pp. 34-47) and "Natchez, Mississippi Territory, May 18th, 1810" in Ord (1828) and Grosart (1876) (`sources/1811-05-28_wilson_letter_portfolio_1812.md`, `sources/1810-05-18_wilson_letter_ord_1828.md`, `..._grosart_1876.md`). Provenance: Wilson stopped at Grinder's and "took down from Mrs. Grinder the particulars", i.e. the only first-hand interview of the innkeeper's wife in the record (date of the visit: spring 1810 or 1811 – the printings disagree). Core: arrival "about sun-set, alone"; "loose gown, white, striped with blue"; "two servants were behind"; supper hardly eaten; "he would be seen walking backwards and forwards before the door, talking to himself"; "Madam this is a very pleasant evening"; the "sweet evening"; "he would sleep on the floor" on "some bear skins and a buffaloe robe"; "it being now dusk the woman went off to the kitchen, and the two men to the barn"; "she heard him walking backwards and forwards ... talking aloud, as she said, 'like a lawyer'"; "She then heard the report of a pistol, and something fall heavily on the floor, and the words 'O Lord!' Immediately afterwards she heard another pistol, and in a few minutes she heard him at her door calling out 'O madam! give me some water, and heal my wounds.' The logs being open, and unplastered, she saw him stagger back and fall against a stump that stands between the kitchen and room. He crawled for some distance, raised himself by the side of a tree, where he sat about a minute. He once more got to the room; afterwards he came to the kitchen door, but did not speak; she then heard him scraping the bucket with a gourd for water; but it appears that this cooling element was denied the dying man! As soon as day broke and not before, the terror of the woman having permitted him to remain for two hours in this most deplorable situation, she sent two of her children to the barn, her husband not being at home, to bring the servants; and on going in they found him lying on the bed; he uncovered his side and showed them where the bullet had entered; a piece of the forehead was blown off, and had exposed the brains, without having bled much. He begged they would take his rifle and blow out his brains, and he would give them all the money he had in his trunk. He often said, 'I am no coward; but I am so strong, so hard to die.' He begg'd the servant not to be afraid of him, for that he would not hurt him. He expired in about two hours, or just as the sun rose above the trees."

**V5. Jefferson's memoir, 18 Aug 1813** (`sources/medical/jefferson_to_allen_1813-08-18_biography.txt`): based on N: "He stopped at the house of a Mr. Grinder, who not being at home, his wife, alarmed at the symptoms of derangement she discovered, gave him up the house, and retired to rest herself in an outhouse, the governor's and Neely's servants being in another. About 3 o'clock in the night he did the deed which plunged his friends into affliction". No new detail.

**V6. The "1839" account ("schoolteacher from the Cherokee Nation")**. Provenance as reported by the secondary literature: an anonymous writer, said to be a schoolteacher from the Cherokee Nation, who heard Mrs. Grinder tell the story in 1838/39 "when she was in her 60s" (Nashville Scene 2003: "an anonymous schoolteacher from the Cherokee Nation published an account Grinder gave when she was in her 60s"); first printed in the New York Dispatch (reported date 1 Feb 1845) from the North Arkansas (Batesville) newspaper — per Jenkinson 2011 citing Starrs & Gale 2009 p. 259; reprinted in Fisher 1962 (Open Library scan p. 298; 1993 ed. p. 294; Jenkinson cites Fisher, p. 155), Chandler 1994 p. 394, Morris 2004, Starrs & Gale 2009 (scan p. 422) and Gale 2015 (scan p. 586). **I did not obtain the full text**; the following are the verbatim fragments recovered through Open Library and Jenkinson: "About dark two or three other men rode up and called for lodging. Mr. Lewis immediately drew a brace of pistols, stepped towards them and challenged them to fight a duel. They not liking this salutation, rode on to the next house, five miles. This alarmed Mrs. Grinder. Supper, however ..."; Mrs. Grinder asked Pernier to take the pistols away and was told "[he] has no ammunition, and if he does any mischief it will be to himself, and not to you or anybody else." The secondary summaries add (Nashville Scene 2003, verbatim): "Grinder also adds hearing a third pistol shot and finding Pernier in the stable the next morning, wearing the clothes Lewis had on the night before and carrying the explorer's gold watch. Pernier claimed that Lewis gave the items to him. When the servants found Lewis, who had scrambled across the road, he was wearing old and tattered clothing." Jenkinson 2011 (n. 91, "Quoted in Fisher, Suicide or Murder?, p. 155") adds Fisher's own verdict: "That Lewis rushed out and challenged two or three men to a duel merely because they asked for lodgings is so fantastically improbable that we must assume either that Mrs. Grinder invented it to support her story of derangement, or got Lewis mixed up with another lodger". These paraphrases are **not verified against the 1845 text** and should be so treated. (Note: the sentence that Lewis killed himself "to deprive his enemies of the pleasure and honor of doing it" belongs to Russell's 1811 statement, not to this account.)

**V7. Tennessee informants of 1876 (Marcus J. Wright)** – Southern Magazine March 1894 quoting his June 1876 article in Ware's Valley Magazine, from "Maj. Bolling Gordon, Hon. A. M. Hughes, Robert M. Cooper, and Robert O. Smith": "The house was a double log-cabin, with a hall between the two rooms; Governor Lewis occupied one of the rooms, Mrs. Grinder the other. About three o'clock in the night Mrs. Grinder heard the sound of the discharge of a pistol, and very soon afterwards Governor Lewis called to her to bring him some water. Although the request was made in a pleasant and polite manner, she was afraid to leave her room, and did not go. Very soon the sound of another pistol shot was heard and on entering the room Governor Lewis was found dead in his bed with a bullet-hole under the chin, and leading up to and through the skull." Wright's John V. Wright letter (same place) gives Col. Robert M. Cooper's testimony (1854-55): "it was suspected at first that Capt. Lewis' valet, a Frenchman or Spaniard, murdered him, but ... it was finally settled in the neighborhood, and universally believed that Lewis committed suicide. Grinder was not suspected, and neither was he a half-breed Indian; but a full-blooded white man, and a reputable man."

**V8. The Polly Spencer / Christina B. Anthony tradition (James D. Park, "John Quill", Nashville American 6 Sept 1891; in Coues 1893 pp. lii-liii; repeated by Pease 1894, Moore 1904)**. Provenance: Mrs. Christina B. Anthony, 77, of Newburgh, "when a young married woman ... boarded with the family of Polly Spencer, the servant girl at 'Old Grinders'" (Pease, April 1894); Park: "She was washing dishes in the kitchen after supper with some of the females in the family, when they heard a shot in the room where Captain Lewis was sleeping. All rushed into the room and found him dead in his bed. Captain Lewis, being fatigued from his journey, had retired immediately after supper. His only companion, she said, was a negro boy, who was attending the horses in the barn at the time. Old Grinder, who was of Indian blood, was at once suspected of the murder, ran away, was captured on Cane creek, brought back, and tried; but the proof not being positive, he was released. Only 25 cents was found on the person of Captain Lewis after he was shot. Old Grinder soon afterward removed to the western part of the State, and it was reported in his old neighborhood had bought a number of slaves and a farm". Coues's comment: "Jefferson's account makes the hour 'about three o'clock in the night,' when Polly Spencer is not likely to have been washing dishes". Other witnesses to Polly Spencer's story named by Pease: Jason Boshears and Sallie Barham Sims.

**V9. Verne S. Pease, "The Death of Captain Merriwether Lewis", Southern Magazine, Feb 1894** (`sources/setting/southern_magazine_1894-02_...txt`): a literary "local traditions" version collected at Summertown: "old Joshua Grinder, a half-breed Indian"; a rifle shot "a few minutes" after Lewis retired; "in his bed they found Captain Lewis dying, with a cruel bullet hole in his left temple"; the Grinders "hastily buried the body in the yard back of the house ... setting fire to the miserable house, left that night"; "A coroner elected from their number ... After hearing the testimony of Polly Spencer, the servant girl who was in the house when the tragedy was committed, and other witnesses, a verdict was rendered of 'murder at the hands of Joshua Grinder'". Pease himself conceded (April 1894): "In no passage of the article did the writer claim to represent more than the local stories and traditions". Rebutted in the same magazine by Marcus J. Wright, John V. Wright and A. S. Horsley (Major Bolling Gordon: Grinder "removed to a small farm on Duck river, below Shady Grove, and made a quiet, inoffensive citizen ... a jury of inquest was held and the testimony was positive that the deed was suicide during mental derangement"). Horsley also reports "the published death at or near Charlottesville, Va., seven or eight years ago, of an old negro man who was a body servant of Governor Lewis in October, 1809 ... solemnly stating on his deathbed ... that his master, Governor Lewis, was murdered at the Grinder stand".

**V10. Pete and Malinda ("Lindy") – the Griner slaves (Dr Samuel B. Moore's interview, c. 1879; J. H. Moore, American Historical Magazine, July 1904; Spence 1900)** (`sources/scholarship/moore_1904_death_of_meriwether_lewis_AHM.txt`; `sources/setting/spence_1900_...txt`). Provenance: J. H. Moore's father (Dr Samuel B. Moore, a member of the 1848 committee) knew Pete (c. 13 in 1809, later owned by Judge Elijah Walker of Savannah); J. H. Moore interviewed Lindy (c. 12 in 1809; Spence: "born in 1797") in the presence of "Mrs. Griner, the widow of Capt. Robert Griner, who was a son of the owner", and published it in the Maury County Sentinel and Hickman Pioneer; Spence 1900: "substantiated by a statement made in 1879 by 'Linda". Core (Moore 1904 pp. 223-24): "late in the afternoon the Governor rode up to the house alone ... Soon thereafter two servants, one white and one black, came up on horses, with one or two pack horses ... the white man's name was 'Perney' and that he was a Spaniard or some sort of a 'furiner.' The servants, after removing the packs, took charge of the horses and repaired to the stables. The Governor at once began to walk up and down the yard, talking to himself and muttering ... Mrs. Griner became alarmed, so much so that she went, carrying all the children—that is, her child and Pete and Lindy—to the kitchen, which was several yards from the 'big house' ... he preferred to sleep on his buffalo robe on the floor ... The servants said they were afraid to stay in the house with him, as he had been acting strangely for the last two or three days, and went off to the barn to sleep. The Governor did not lie down; he continued to walk—sometimes in the house and then in the yard—continually talking to himself and repeating: 'They have told lies on me and want to ruin me.' The children soon went to sleep, but Mrs. Griner could not and sat up all night. Just before day all were aroused by the report of firearms. Two shots were fired in rapid succession. Immediately Perney came running to the house, and the Governor crawled to the door and called for water. They all went together to the house, and found the Governor writhing in pain on the floor. Mrs. Griner asked: 'Why in the world did you do this?' He replied that if he had not done it, some one else would. 'They are telling lies and trying to ruin me.' He was bleeding profusely from a wound in the body near the heart. He drank great quantities of water, and would immediately throw it up. He lingered in great agony until twelve o'clock, when he died. He was buried just outside of the inclosure where the monument stands." Spence's summary differs in one point: "During the night three shots were heard, and when Lewis' room was entered he was found lying near the door, begging for water. He lived until noon". The Malinda tradition as retold through the Griner in-laws (Bakeless 1947, from William J. Webster's inquiries): "About daylight, according to Aunt Lindy, the 'furriner,' Pernia, came to the kitchen and told Mrs. Grinder that Lewis was cutting the veins in his throat. If Mrs. Grinder would give him water, he would give up his razor"; "The niece of Grinder's daughter-in-law ... averred that Grinder's son kept buried in a chimney corner 'the old man's pants,' the legs of which were filled with gold ... She insisted that Aunt Malinda 'saw the old man have a lap full of gold,' but Malinda herself never said so"; "Aunt Malinda asserted that after his arrival in Virginia, Pernia was accused by Mrs. Marks ... of killing her son. Exclaiming 'I'll see Gov L before dark,' he 'cut his throat from ear to ear in Mrs. L's yard.'"

**V11. Lewis-family tradition (Wheeler 1904, pp. 66-68)** (`sources/scholarship/wheeler_1904_...txt`): Mrs. Caroline D. M. ### 3.3 The moon: who claims she SAW anything, and who first used the lunar phase in print

**Who claims she saw anything outdoors, and by what light.** Only one version puts anything before her eyes: Wilson's (V4): "The logs being open, and unplastered, she saw him stagger back and fall against a stump that stands between the kitchen and room. He crawled for some distance, raised himself by the side of a tree, where he sat about a minute. He once more got to the room; afterwards he came to the kitchen door, but did not speak; she then heard him scraping the bucket with a gourd for water". Wilson's letter names **no light** – neither moon, nor fire, nor candle, nor lantern – anywhere; the only light-related words are "sun-set", "dusk" and "As soon as day broke". Ord's 1828 printing of the same letter does not alter this passage. Every other telling is auditory or after-the-fact: Neelly/Brahan/NI – she "heard two pistols"; Clarion – "alarmed by the firing"; Russell – she "heard the report of the pistols" and he came to *her* door; Jefferson – nothing; Wright 1876 – heard the shots, "afraid to leave her room"; Park 1891/Pease 1894 – the household rushed into his room; Lindy 1904 – heard the shots, then "all went together". The 1839/1845 account cannot be checked (text not obtained); the secondary summaries have Lewis "scrambled across the road" but do not say she *saw* it. Modern paraphrases that convert Wilson's "saw" into "peeked/peered through the chinks" (K. Moore 2004; Danisi's talk) are paraphrase, not source.

**Who first used the moon against her story in print.** Earliest found: **John D. W. Guice, "Moonlight and Meriwether Lewis: How could Mrs. Grinder have seen what she claimed on that pitch-black night?", We Proceeded On 28:1 (February 2002), pp. 21-23** (`sources/scholarship/wpo_wpo_2002_feb_special_section_death_WPO28-1.txt`, PDF pp. 23-25). Guice writes that "until my geologist friend asked me that question, I had never thought about moonlight on the Natchez Trace"; "A check of the Website of the Astronomical Applications Department of the U.S. Naval Observatory revealed the following data for Nashville. There had been a new moon on October 9, 1809, at 1:30 A.M. Central Standard Time. When Lewis arrived at Grinder's Stand the evening of Tuesday, October 10, 1809, the phase of the moon was a 'waxing crescent with 3% of the Moon's visible disk illuminated.' Moonset that night was at 6:21 P.M."; "nowhere does she mention lamps or candles in either the guest cabin or the kitchen cabin. Nor does she refer to an outside fire or to Lewis's carrying a lantern or a candle"; "How, one may fairly ask, without even a hint of moonlight could she have seen these things even had they occurred?"; "Historians will never know how much starlight there was, but we know there was no moonlight." The editor's introduction (p. 11) summarises: Guice "argues that Mrs. Grinder ... could not possibly have seen what she claimed because the night was moonless." The author's note says he "incorporated the ideas in this article in a paper entitled 'Why Not Homicide?: Historians and Their Case for Suicide,' delivered at the Foundation's 2001 annual meeting, in Pierre, South Dakota" – so the argument was first aired orally in August 2001 and first printed in February 2002. Later uses: Guice's chapter in Guice ed., *By His Own Hand? The Mysterious Death of Meriwether Lewis* (2006), p. 94 (per Jenkinson 2011 n. 69; Open Library scan p. 206: "a barely visible moon would have rendered the night too dark for Mrs. [Grinder] ..." and "to prove that Mrs. Grinder could not have witnessed much on the moonless night of 10-11 October"); Starrs & Gale, *The Death of Meriwether Lewis* (2009) (scan p. 422: "established that the night of October 10-11, 1809 was the night after a new moon. Therefore, the accounts of seeing ..."); Tucker, Smithsonian, 8 Oct 2009 (unattributed: "Scholars have reconstructed lunar cycles to prove that the innkeeper's wife couldn't have seen what she said she saw that moonless night"); Jenkinson 2011 (Guice's point 31: "It was too dark that night for Mrs. Grinder to have seen Lewis struggling about the yard, and there is no mention of a candle or lantern"; Jenkinson: "The argument from lunar data, frequently played as a kind of trump card to discredit Mrs. Grinder's testimony, raises as many questions as it settles"). **Not found in**: Fisher 1962/1993 (Open Library full-text search for moon/moonless/dark-night phrases returns nothing for Fisher in the Grinder context, though the search is not exhaustive), Dillon 1965, Cutright 1986, Chuinard's three WPO articles 1991-92, Chandler 1994, Guice's own "Fatal Rendezvous" (WPO May 1998; no moon or light argument), and the WPO 2002 letters responding to the issue. Danisi 2012 was not checked directly.

## 4. The 1848 committee and its report

Source: the report as printed in the Appendix to the Journal of the House of Representatives of Tennessee, 1849-50, pp. 238-240, transcribed by Coues 1893 (pp. lvii-lxii) from Park's article and "carefully verified, June 10th, 1893" against the State Library copy; full OCR in `sources/1849_lewis_monument_committee_report.md` and `sources/setting/coues_1893_v1_supplement_...txt`; also in Wheeler 1904 pp. 69-71, Moore 1904, and quoted by the court in 1998 (citing White, Messages of the Governors 1845-1857, p. 386).

* Enabling act: Acts of Tennessee 1847-48, ch. cxxxv, §9 (passed 4/9 Feb 1848): $500 appropriated; agents "Robert A. Smith of Lewis County, the Hon. Edmund Dillahunty and Barclay Martin of Maury, and Dr. Samuel B. Moore of Hickman". (Lewis County itself was created by the act of 21 Dec 1843.)
* Members who signed: **Edmund Dillahunty, Barclay Martin, Robert A. Smith, Samuel B. Moore.**
* What they did (verbatim): "The monument was erected by Lemuel W. Kirby, of Columbia ... The base is of rough, unhewn stone, eight feet high, nine feet square at the bottom ... a plinth ... four feet square and eighteen inches thick ... the shaft, or broken column, ... eleven feet ... The whole height twenty and a half feet." "Great care was taken to identify the grave. George Nixon, Esq., an old Surveyor, had become very early acquainted with its locality; he pointed out the place; but to make assurance doubly sure the grave was reopened and the upper part of the skeleton examined, and such evidences found as to leave no doubt of the place of interment. Witnesses were called and their certificate, with that of the Surveyor, prove the fact." Inscriptions by Prof. Nathaniel Cross (west: name, born 18 Aug 1774, "Died October 11, 1809, aged 35 years"; south: career, "His melancholy death occurred where this monument now stands, and under which rest his mortal remains"; east: Jefferson's words; north: the Latin "Immaturus obi ..." and "Erected by the Legislature of Tennessee, A.D. 1848").
* The assassin sentence and its grounds (verbatim): "The impression has long prevailed that under the influence of disease of body and mind—of hopes based upon long and valuable services—not merely deferred but wholly disappointed—Governor Lewis perished by his own hands. It seems to be more probable that he died by the hands of an assassin. The place at which he was killed is even yet a lonely spot. It was then wild and solitary, and on the borders of the Indian nation." The report then quotes (as the only other ground) "a letter from Maj. C. L. [M. L.] Clark, of St. Louis, to the Rev. Mr. Cressey" saying Lewis was "murdered by his servant, a Frenchman, who stole his money and horses, returned to Natchez", and suggests an acre be reserved around the grave. **No forensic observation is stated** as the basis of the assassin sentence; what the skeleton showed is not described beyond "such evidences ... as to leave no doubt of the place of interment". Coues's footnote: the printed report's date of death "Oct. 17" is a misprint; Killebrew (1874) reported the fence "dilapidated"; Park in 1889 found the fence gone ("the iron ... taken away during the war to make horseshoes").
* Local traditions of what happened at the grave-opening (all later, none in the report): Pease 1894: "Captain John Nixon, the old surveyor of Lewis county who located the tomb and assisted to open it ... said that when the skeleton was unearthed the forehead was crushed, showing indubitable proof of a shot in that portion of the body"; John V. Wright 1894 (from Col. Robert M. Cooper): "When the remains where found, the wound in the head, the coffin and the nails corresponded with the statement made by the old negro" who made the coffin; TNGenWeb: "Unable to locate the grave three bodies were exhumed before positive identification was made ... ROBERT M. COOPER said, 'This is the grave of MR. LEWIS for these are my nails.' ROBERT O. SMITH seeing the skull with the hole in it reminded those present of his father's story of the great hole in LEWIS' head"; "they found a few coins, a brass button or two, and a medal or insignia, and a piece of his uniform. They went to the Spring and found a flat rock, and scraped a hole in it and put everything in it and put another rock on top, and put these rocks in the grave." **[INFERENCE]** these traditions conflict with the report's naming of George Nixon as the identifier and cannot be reconciled from the record.
* Dr Samuel B. Moore's own view (per his son, J. H. Moore 1904): the son concludes for suicide and reports Pete's and Lindy's testimony (V10); Moore says the murder theory was "comparatively new" in the county (quoting Claggett).

## 5. The 1996 coroner's jury and the exhumation requests, 1996–2026

* **Background**: Starrs "met with the Natchez Trace Parkway Superintendent in 1992 to discuss a proposal to exhume" (court opinion 1998). Superintendent's refusal 9 July 1996 and Regional Director's ARPA denial 24 Sept 1997 (opinion; WPO Aug/Nov 1996).
* **3–4 June 1996, Hohenwald (National Guard Armory)**: coroner's inquest called by Lewis County coroner Richard Tate "at the urging of Starrs and district attorney Joseph D. Baugh" (UPI 3 June 1996); "Tate and seven fellow jurors, along with two alternates ... 14 scheduled witnesses" (UPI); court opinion: "seven residents of Lewis County, and one alternate. The jury heard testimony from 13 witnesses during the 2-day hearing. The testimony of the witnesses was coordinated by the District Attorneys and Professor James Starrs". Witnesses reported: Gerald Richards (FBI document examiner: the recorded will "Neither the writing nor the signature is that of Lewis" – a clerk's copy), Thomas Streed (criminal psychologist: "Today I would be investigating it as a homicide"), L. Ruth C. Frick (finances), Arlen J. Large (for suicide), Ravenholt (late-stage syphilis), William M. Bass (WPO Aug 1996; Deseret 5 June 1996). Verdict (Deseret News, AP, 5 June 1996): "After deliberating less than 90 minutes Tuesday ... the coroner's jury of laymen said that, 'because of the importance of the person in question to the history of Lewis County, we feel exhumation is necessary for closure in this matter'"; Coroner Tate: "very little tangible evidence for this jury to base a credible ruling as to the matter of murder or suicide"; the WPO summary of the four recommendations: "1) There was little evidence to support the suicide theory; 2) exhumation; 3) work is to be done on-site and 4) remains are to be returned to the same grave site." NPS response the same day (Superintendent Dan Brown, Tupelo): "an exhumation of Meriwether Lewis' remains would not be permitted." Starrs (Deseret): "I think it's clearly undecided, but a strong case could be made for murder."
* **16 Dec 1996**: the State of Tennessee (D.A. Baugh) petitions the Circuit Court of Lewis County under T.C.A. §38-1-104 to exhume and autopsy. **June 1997**: Starrs and William M. Bass file an ARPA application; **24 Sept 1997** Regional Director denies; appeal to the Director; **16 Dec 1997** hearing in Atlanta (GWU release 15 Dec 1997; NPR 15 Dec 1997). **9 Dec 1997** the United States removes the state petition to federal court.
* **24 March 1998**: *In re Exhumation of Meriwether Lewis*, 999 F. Supp. 1066 (M.D. Tenn.) (Higgins, J.): remand denied; "the United States' motion to dismiss the State's petition to exhume will be granted"; "ARPA is simply the only remedy"; "This action is dismissed without prejudice." (The opinion also recites the 1848 report: the committee "concluded Lewis probably 'died by the hands of an assassin.'") WPO May 1999 quotes the judgment's close: "The bones of Captain Lewis will remain undisturbed, at least for the present."
* **20 Feb 2002**: Senator Frank Murkowski (ranking member, Senate Energy and Natural Resources) asks the NPS to allow exhumation: "All that stands in the way is world-class bureaucracy" (Fox/AP). Hearings were later cancelled (per the 2003 Nashville Scene article).
* **January 2008**: Assistant Secretary of the Interior Lyle Laverty "wrote lawyers seeking the exhumation permit that, after careful consideration, he found it 'appropriate and in the public interest'" (Scripps/HeraldNet 8 July 2009); Daily Progress 2010: "The bid to exhume was approved by a Department of the Interior official in January 2008 ... noting that the family is united behind the exhumation and that the examination could shed light on historic issues."
* **Late January 2009**: permit application received by the NPS; **4 June 2009** the family launches SolveTheMystery.org (HNN press release: "the Lewis family's more than decade-long quest to gain federal permission for the exhumation as well as a Christian reburial"; Howell Lewis Bowen); **June 2009** NPS letter promising an environmental assessment (Daily Progress); **8 July 2009** National Press Club appearance by Col. Thomas McSwain and Howell Lewis Bowen ("Our family wants to put an end to the mystery hanging over how Uncle Meriwether died"; lawyer Kirsten Nathanson: the family is "weary and puzzled" by "mixed signals"); WPLN 10 July 2009.
* **April 2010**: Interior re-reviews "after a reported Congressional query into the plan" (Daily Progress). Decision text (Thomas L. Strickland, Assistant Secretary for Fish and Wildlife and Parks, quoted 27 June 2010): "I have reviewed the prior approval and in consultation with the National Park Service and the office of the solicitor, have determined that no compelling arguments exist that are sufficient to overturn [park service] management policies ... The information proposed to be gained (determination of cause of death) does not add to the commemoration of Governor Lewis' accomplishments. While a dispute over the circumstances of his death might be a matter of some public interest, it is not necessarily a sufficient reason to ignore [park service] policy." NPS grounds: "policies prohibiting disturbing graves that are not 'threatened by destruction'"; "109 other graves, including some that have yet to be located"; William Reynolds (NPS): "It's not clear that the exhumation would result in a clear understanding of his death and the could result in harm to other graves during the process." **25 Aug 2010**: Strickland's letter to the family, "Please consider this a final decision on this matter" (per WSJ 25 Sept 2010, `sources/scholarship/wsj_2010-09-25_esterl_exhumation.txt`; the letter PDF is captured but its text did not extract, `sources/scholarship/interior_strickland_2010-08-25_final_decision_letter.txt`); the family's appeal letter of Sept 2010 likewise (`lewis_family_2010_appeal_letter_wsj.txt`, text not extracted).
* **NPS position since**: the current NPS place page (2021-24) states "Historical accounts support the probability of suicide"; the CLI: "Historians studying the cause of Lewis' death support a suicide theory, though local legend maintains that he was murdered."
* **2011–2026**: no new formal exhumation request, permit application, court action or legislative measure was found. SolveTheMystery.org was static from 2013 through its 24 Jan 2020 capture and the domain had passed to unrelated content by 2024 (`sources/setting/web_solvethemystery_org_capture_*`). Publications only: Danisi 2012 (*Uncovering the Truth about Meriwether Lewis*), Kira Gale 2015 (*Meriwether Lewis: The Assassination of an American Hero*), WPO pieces (Turnbow 2012, Danisi 2014, Gale 2016, Peck 2022, Holmberg 2024, Bartsch 2025 – see `sources/scholarship/`), Turnbow's *Jefferson's Spy* (c. 2023). Physical work at the site since 2010 has been maintenance (ARRA rehabilitation completed c. 2010-11; DAR marker 2006; web-page updates 2021, 2024). Checked: NPS news/pages, Wayback captures, press searches through 2026 – nothing further.
