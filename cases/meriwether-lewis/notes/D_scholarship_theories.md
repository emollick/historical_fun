## 0. Conventions

* **Extent tags.** Every work below carries one of: **[WHOLE]** (the whole text read), **[CHAPTER]** (the relevant chapter(s) read whole), **[SNIPPETS]** (only search-hit fragments from the archive.org full-text API or Google Books, typically 100-1,300 fragments of one to two lines each, read in full but with no continuous pages), **[ABSTRACT]**, **[REVIEWS/QUOTATION ONLY]** (known only through other authors' quotations or reviews), **[NOT OBTAINED]**. The rule I was given was to read the principal opposing work in full before claiming to answer it; where I could not, the tag says so and section 8 says what I tried.
* **Sides.** Each work is marked SUICIDE, MURDER, ACCIDENT/SELF-INFLICTED-NOT-SUICIDE, or UNDECIDED, as the author states it, with the author's own hedges quoted.
* **Quotation.** Verbatim wording is in double quotes with the page where I have it. Page numbers from snippet sets are those printed in the fragment (running heads and index entries), so they are approximate to a page or two. Where I draw a conclusion the sources do not state, it is marked **[INFERENCE]**.
* **Abbreviations.** WPO = *We Proceeded On*, the quarterly of the Lewis and Clark Trail Heritage Foundation (PDFs at lewisandclark.org/wpo/pdf/). WMQ = *William and Mary Quarterly*. Jackson, *Letters* = Donald Jackson, ed., *Letters of the Lewis and Clark Expedition, with Related Documents, 1783-1854*, 2nd ed., 2 vols (Urbana, 1978). D&J = Danisi & Jackson 2009. "Neelly's letter" = James Neelly to Jefferson, Nashville, 18 Oct 1809 (`sources/1809-10-18_neelly_to_jefferson.md`). "Wilson's letter" = Alexander Wilson to Alexander Lawson, dated 18 May 1810 (Ord 1828; Grosart 1876) or "May 28th, 1811" (*Port Folio*, Jan 1812) (`sources/1810-05-18_wilson_letter_ord_1828.md`; `sources/1811-05-28_wilson_letter_portfolio_1812.md`). "Russell's statement" = the statement dated 26 Nov 1811 in Jonathan Williams's papers (Jackson, *Letters* 2:573-75; excerpts in `sources/1811-11-26_russell_statement_excerpts_wpo.md`).
* **The data file** `data/wpo_death_index.tsv` lists every WPO item 1974-2026 whose text, title or author matched death-related search terms (573 rows), with OPEN/PRINT status.

## 1. The debate, work by work, in date order

Format for each entry: **date, author, work [extent tag] — side.** Then: thesis; evidence relied on; new material introduced; what it added to the *evidence* (as distinct from interpretation).

### 1a. Before there was a debate: 1809-1904

**1809-1813. The contemporaries** [WHOLE, in the sibling transcriptions under `sources/`] — SUICIDE, without a recorded dissent as to the manner of death.
The record that every later writer argues over is small: Neelly's letter of 18 Oct 1809 ("I am Sorry to Say by Suicide"; the woman "discovering the governor to be deranged gave him up the house"; "about three OClock She heard two pistols fire off in the Governors Room ... he had shot himself in the head with one pistol & a little below the Breast with the other—when his Servant came in he Says; I have done the business my good Servant give me Some water"); Brahan's three letters of the same day (to Jefferson, Eustis and Stoddard; the Stoddard copy printed by Thwaites; Brahan writes that the shot was "first ... thought in the head" — Rogers, WPO 31:4); the *Nashville Democratic Clarion* of 20 Oct 1809 ("shot a ball that grazed the top of his head, and another through his intestines, and cut his neck arm and ham with a razor ... under the influence of a deranging malady for about six weeks"; `sources/1809-10-31_kentucky_gazette_reprint_of_clarion_20oct1809.md`); the *Missouri Gazette* of 2 Nov 1809 ("discharged the contents of a brace of pistols in his head and breast" — Phelps 1956); the *National Intelligencer*, 13 Nov 1809 ("So many concurring accounts reach us of the death of Meriwether Lewis, Esq. Governor of Louisiana, by suicide, that we can no longer refuse giving credit to it") and 27 Nov 1809 (the earlier "circumstantial statement ... in several respects incorrect", after which it printed Neelly's letter — the correction concerns details, not the manner of death); Madison to Jefferson, 30 Oct 1809 ("had recourse to his Dirk with wch he mangled himself considerably" — Jenkinson 2011 ch. VI); Clark to Jonathan Clark, 28 Oct 1809 ("I fear this report has too much truth, tho' it may have no foundation ... I fear O' I fear the weight of his mind has overcome him") and 26 Nov 1809 (Russell's lost letters: "detaind by him 15 days in a State of Derangement most of the time and that he had attempted to kill himself before he got there"); Bates to Richard Bates, 9 Nov 1809 ("die in such a manner"); Mahlon Dickerson's diary ("Poor Meriwether Lewis killed himself this night" — Cutright 1986); Russell to Jefferson, 4 and 31 Jan 1810; Wilson's letter (May 1810 or 1811); Russell's statement of 26 Nov 1811; Jefferson to Paul Allen, 18 Aug 1813 ("Governor Lewis had, from early life, been subject to hypocondriac affections. it was a constitutional disposition in all the nearer branches of the family of his name ... he did the deed which plunged his friends into affliction"). The only contemporary use of the word "murder" is Russell's of 31 Jan 1810, and it sits inside a suicide narrative: "from the Statement of Grinders wife where he Killed himself I can not help beleiving that Purney was rather Aiding & abeting in the murder than otherwise" (`sources/1810-01-31_russell_to_jefferson.md`). Nobody ordered an inquiry; the Tennessee record of any coroner's inquest, if one was held, does not survive (C section 6). Later nineteenth-century notices — the *Analectic Magazine* 1816, Howe 1856, Jacob 1859, Killebrew 1874 — all say suicide (Cutright 1986, WPO 12:1) [QUOTATION ONLY].

**1848-1850. The Tennessee monument committee (Dillahunty, Martin, R. A. Smith, S. B. Moore), report printed in the Appendix to the Journal of the Tennessee House, 1849-50, pp. 238-40** [WHOLE, in Coues's verified transcription; `sources/1849_lewis_monument_committee_report.md`; C section 4] — MURDER (first statement in print).
Verbatim: "The impression has long prevailed that under the influence of disease of body and mind—of hopes based upon long and valuable services—not merely deferred but wholly disappointed—Governor Lewis perished by his own hands. It seems to be more probable that he died by the hands of an assassin. The place at which he was killed is even yet a lonely spot. It was then wild and solitary, and on the borders of the Indian nation." The only other ground given is a letter from Meriwether Lewis Clark of St Louis to the Rev. Mr Cressey: Lewis was "murdered by his servant, a Frenchman, who stole his money and horses, returned to Natchez", which "removes—from my mind, at least, the only stigma upon the fair name I have the honor to bear" (Moore 1904 prints the letter). What it added to the evidence: the grave "was reopened and the upper part of the skeleton examined, and such evidences found as to leave no doubt of the place of interment" — nothing about wounds is stated. Every later writer who says the committee "found" evidence of murder is reading in (Gale 2016: they "must have seen gunshot entrance wounds in the back of his skull, or in his back" — her inference, not the report; Pease 1894's Nixon tradition that "the forehead was crushed" fits a forehead shot as well as anything). Moore 1904: "The legislative committee clearly indicates that the theory of suicide was general up to 1848"; Holmberg 2006: M. L. Clark's letter "planted the murder seed".

**1891. James D. Park, "John Quill" article, *Nashville American*, 6 Sept 1891 (reprinted 4 Jan 1903)** [WHOLE as printed by Coues 1893 and Moore 1904] — MURDER (by Grinder).
Local tradition gathered from Christina Anthony (77, of Newburg, whose daughter had eloped with a Griner): "Old Grinder, who was of Indian blood, was at once suspected of the murder, ran away, was captured on Cane creek, brought back, and tried; but the proof not being positive, he was released"; the Grinders "removed to the western part of the State, and ... bought a number of slaves and a farm"; money and watch gone. Evidence: oral tradition eighty years after the event. Added: the first named suspect in print, the arrest/trial story (no court record has ever been found — C section 6), and the prosperity story (deed records show 100 acres for $250 in 1814 — Bakeless; Phelps).

**1893. Elliott Coues, ed., *History of the Expedition under the Command of Lewis and Clark*, vol. 1, Memoir of Lewis, pp. xlviii-lxii** [the memoir's death section WHOLE; `coues_1893_history_of_the_expedition_v1_memoir_death_notes.txt`] — MURDER (the "first documented challenge", Cutright; Jenkinson counts him a "half-hearted murderist").
Verbatim: Mrs Grinder's story as told to Wilson "is wildly improbable upon its face; it does not hang together; there is every sign that it is a concoction on the part of an accomplice in crime, either before or after the event"; she is "fit for treason, stratagem, and spoils," and not to be believed under oath"; Lewis "should be given the full benefit of the doubt, that no stigma may rest on his illustrious name"; Park's article is "perhaps the strongest that will ever be drawn up ... to clear so great a name from so grave an imputation". Evidence relied on: a close reading of Wilson's text (no new documents) plus Park. Added: printed the 1848 report and Park's article, the report "carefully verified, June 10th, 1893" against the State Library copy; fixed the reading of Wilson's letter date as 18 May 1811 (Coues's dating, followed by Fisher).

**1894. Vernon S. Pease (Feb 1894) and John V. Wright's rebuttal from Col. Robert M. Cooper; Horsley 1894 ("old negro" deathbed story)** [NOT read by me; read and tabulated in C section 6] — Pease MURDER ("Joshua Grinder"; "Jim Murrill"; house burned); Wright: Grinder "was not suspected"; Cooper made the coffin nails and identified the grave in 1848.

**1904. J. H. Moore, "The Death of Meriwether Lewis," *American Historical Magazine* 9 (1904)** [WHOLE; `moore_1904_death_of_meriwether_lewis_AHM.txt`] — SUICIDE.
Son of committee-member Dr Samuel B. Moore. Prints "Lindy's" account (the Grinders' servant girl, interviewed in old age): Lewis rode up alone; "Perney ... a Spaniard or some sort of a 'furiner'"; Lewis said "They have told lies on me and want to ruin me"; "Just before day ... Two shots were fired in rapid succession"; "if he had not done it, some one else would"; found "bleeding profusely from a wound in the body near the heart"; died at "twelve o'clock"; two sisters visited the next fall and planted roses. Prints M. L. Clark's letter verbatim and Park's 1891 article. Moore's judgment, from the local lawyer Claggett: "the murder theory is comparatively new ... I have no doubt it originated in Major Clark's letter published by the legislative committee in 1849." Added: Lindy's account (a new, late, witness) and the first argument from silence about the murder tradition's late origin.

**1904. Olin D. Wheeler, *The Trail of Lewis and Clark*, vol. 1, ch. on Lewis's death** [CHAPTER; `wheeler_1904_trail_of_lewis_and_clark_v1_death_chapter.txt`] — MURDER-leaning (family tradition), with reservations.
Prints letters from Lewis-family connections: Mrs Goodlett — Lucy Marks, c. 1820, "never entertained the idea for a moment that he had committed suicide. The theory that the family have ever advanced is that he was murdered by his Spanish servant"; "no insanity in his branch of the family"; Quarles corroborates ("family jewels of far greater value"); Shelton — the servant sent a trunk of papers some thirty years later to Mary Garland Marks Moore; Chief Justice Moore — William Moore recognised "a gold watch" on the Mississippi in the possession of the supposed Spanish servant. Wheeler's own caveat: "Mrs. Goodlett is, without doubt, I think, in error as to the servant and papers" (Pernier went to Jefferson, who wrote Madison for his $240 wages, 26 Nov 1809). Added: the Lewis-family tradition in print for the first time — the only source for "Lucy Marks accused Pernier", and it is a c. 1900 recollection of what she said c. 1820.

**1904-05. Reuben Gold Thwaites, *Original Journals*, 1:xxxviii** [QUOTATION ONLY, via Cutright 1986] — UNDECIDED/MURDER-leaning: "it was reported that he had committed suicide ... a theory which Jefferson accepted without question; but it was and still is believed by many that he was murdered." Added: printed Brahan to Stoddard, 18 Oct 1809 (`sources/1809-10-18_brahan_to_stoddard_thwaites.md`).

### 1b. From Bakeless to the coroner's jury: 1947-1996

**1947. John Bakeless, *Lewis and Clark: Partners in Discovery* (Morrow), ch. "Tragedy in Tennessee", pp. 409-428** [CHAPTER, whole; `bakeless_1947_partners_in_discovery_death_chapter.txt`] — MURDER-leaning, explicitly undecided: "The evidence for murder is not very strong, and the stories from Fort Pickering strongly suggest suicide, but none of the evidence is really conclusive"; "impossible to make a positive statement, either way". "The entire suicide story, therefore, depends entirely on what the people at Grinder's Stand that night told Neeley the next day, with some possible confirmation from Lewis's two servants" (p. 415).
Evidence: Neelly, Wilson, Jefferson, plus his own 1946-47 fieldwork (Tennessee State Library file T/920; Meriwether Lewis National Monument files; Webster and Frierson letters; Sallie Walker and Dye manuscripts). New material, all local tradition first set down 1900-1947: (1) the post-rider Robert O. Smith story — body "lying near the road, 100 or 150 yards from the Grinder cabins", a bullet "in the back", "a bit of wadding that looked as if it had come from a musket" between body and stable (n. 419.4: Smith "made no written report"); (2) names of a supposed coroner's jury — Samuel Whiteside (foreman), R. M. Cooper, George Vincent, Sharpe (or Shipp), Carroll, Johnson — with Frierson's report that Whiteside said "the Jury were Cowards and were afraid to bring in a verdict of murder ... the murderers had Indian Blood in their Veins" and a verdict, per Webster, of "He might have committed suicide"; (3) the Hardin County grand-jury story ("doubtful if there is any truth"); (4) Grinder's 1814 purchase of 100 acres on Duck River for $250 (Hickman Deed Book C, p. 31); (5) "Aunt Lindy" and Polly Spencer as household witnesses ("If I had not done it, some one else would"; Pernia said Lewis "was cutting the veins in his throat"); (6) the lost pocket-book and "only 25 cents in pockets"; (7) Lindy's story that Mrs Marks accused Pernia, who "cut his throat from ear to ear in Mrs. L's yard" ("Nobody in Albemarle ... ever heard this tale"); (8) Jefferson's alleged later opinion that "subsequent evidence tended to prove Meriwether Lewis did not kill himself but was murdered", in a lost volume "loaned out" by Dr Wilson Randolph ("rather dubious ground"); (9) Clark's niece: "he never believed he committed suicide" (Dye MSS). Added to the evidence: items (2) and (4), which later writers treat as fact, are here first, and Bakeless's own notes say how thin they are. Bakeless also introduced two errors that persist: "Alexander Neeley" and Neelly's servant as Reuben Lewis's slave "Captain Tom" (repeated by Kushner 1989).

**1956. Dawson A. Phelps, "The Tragic Death of Meriwether Lewis," *William and Mary Quarterly* 3d ser. 13, no. 3 (July 1956): 305-318** [SNIPPETS: 32 Google Books snippets in `phelps_1956_tragic_death_WMQ_gb_snippets.txt`; otherwise QUOTATION via Cutright 1986, Chuinard 1991, Guice 1998/2002, Fisher, Jenkinson; JSTOR blocked] — SUICIDE.
Phelps was the Park Service historian at Natchez Trace Parkway, Tupelo (his 12 Aug 1954 NPS memorandum preceded the article — Turnbow 2012). Thesis: "Such was the tragic story accepted by contemporaries"; "In the absence of direct and pertinent contemporary evidence to the contrary, of which not a scintilla exists, the verdict of suicide must stand" (p. 317); "That nearly thirty years passed before the possibility of murder was raised is in itself evidence of a high order that the original verdict of suicide was correct" (p. 318); "It is not remarkable, considering nineteenth-century moral attitudes, that many people in the vicinity should have dismissed the possibility of suicide, substituting instead the more exciting, dramatic, and perhaps more acceptable stories of murder which embellished and enlarged in the passage of time, became established folklore" (p. 306); the Trace "was not ... a dangerous road ... the most heavily traveled road of the southwest" (p. 317); of those who "passed the lonely grave not one reported rumors of murder or assassination. Meriwether Lewis in 1809 was a national figure"; Mrs Grinder's variations "probably" hysteria or lapse of memory (p. 316); Robert Grinder "settled in 1814, where, as a respected citizen, he raised a ..."; descendants of the supposed jurors "unanimously admitted that their ancestors had ..." [fragment ends]; "a man claiming to be the very post rider himself"; Neelly "failed to mention the highly significant remark which Lewis, after having fired the fatal shots, made as his servant entered"; Pernier is treated as an eyewitness (p. 313); "very little is known of" Neelly (p. 314). Evidence: the 1809-13 documents, the Missouri and Nashville newspapers, census and deed records on the Grinders, the WMQ's first use of Lewis's 31st-birthday journal entry as evidence of depression (p. 317). Added: the first documentary, footnoted treatment; the "Trace was safe" argument (Guice 1998: Phelps "had an axe to grind" against Coates's *Outlaw Years*); the observation that the murder story is not in print for nearly forty years. Standing: "Until the 1980s most present-day historians viewed the 1956 article by Dawson Phelps as the final word" (Guice 1998); Fisher's chapter title "Dawson A. Phelps: It Was Suicide".

**1961-62. Jonathan Daniels, *The Devil's Backbone: The Story of the Natchez Trace* (McGraw-Hill)** [QUOTATION ONLY] — MURDER (guess): "if Lewis was murdered, as good a guess as any is that Wilkinson ordered it, Bates arranged it, Pernia did it" (pp. 181-82, quoted by Guice 2016; also Dillon p. 348 and Guice 2006). The first Wilkinson-plot sentence in print, offered as a guess.

**1962. Vardis Fisher, *Suicide or Murder? The Strange Death of Governor Meriwether Lewis* (Swallow Press), 285 pp.** [SNIPPETS: ~1,100 fragments across all chapters, `fisher_1962_suicide_or_murder_fts_snippets.txt`] — MURDER-leaning, formally open: "in our opinion it points more to murder than to suicide" (p. 244); privately, "in all fairness to the evidence ... we still don't know how he died" (Merritt's review of Flora ed., WPO 28:1); Chuinard: "if pushed to express an opinion, he would lean toward murder."
Chapters include "James Neelly: Chief Witness", "Captain Brahan Writes Three [letters]", "Malinda's Story", "Mrs. Grinder's Third Version", "As the Old Timers Told It", "The Family Tradition", "Was There a Coroner's Jury?", "James D. Park: It Was Murder", "Vernon S. Pease: It Was Murder", "William J. Webster: It Was Murder", "Dawson A. Phelps: It Was Suicide", and Appendix A "Opinions Pro and Con". Thesis: the witnesses are unreliable and the documents suspect; "It may be that Lewis was attacked, wounded, robbed, and left to die, and that he did then crawl ..."; on Fort Pickering, "he may have made a clumsy attempt on his life, or, more probably, he may only have expressed his delirium in wild actions and talk which someone at the fort, possibly Russell, interpreted as an attempt." New material: (1) Russell's letters of 4 and 31 Jan 1810 read in the National Archives originals ("so many missing words that it was necessary to go to the originals") — the first publication of the 31 Jan accusations against Neelly and Pernier, which Jackson's *Letters* omitted; (2) the provenance of Russell's 1811 statement: "The Russell document is copied from a copy"; the copy "at the Univ. of Indiana library, made by Jonathan Williams. Why he made a copy of it we don't know"; "this amazing document"; (3) the 1839/1845 "Arkansas schoolteacher" version of Mrs Grinder's story (Draper MSS 29CC33; "About 9 o'clock first shot heard—five minutes later second shot. Three shots fired. Piece of skull blown off forehead at first fire, stuck to bullet & lodged over mantel ... 'O Lord! Congress, relieve me.' ... Mr. Pyrna and the servant rode up ... Mr. L's gold watch in his pocket ... Mr. P. buried him, and took possession of his effects"); (4) Malinda's story in full (Pernia said Lewis "was cutting the veins in his throat"; "Mrs. G told P to give him only a spoonful. Pernia did, and L gave up the razor"); (5) the post-rider tradition ("mortal wound in the back and with wadding at the stable"; "no powder stains on the clothing or on the flesh"); (6) the inquest tradition ("A Maury County Coroner's Jury supposedly met and gave verdict of death by gunshot without specifying the method. The page recording this verdict has been reported as torn from the Maury County records"; "If, as folklore has it, there was a coroner's inquest, no formal record of it survives"); (7) the Runnions tracks story ("print of the rifle in the dust"; from Pinkerton of Centerville and Milligan); (8) the grand-jury tradition ("October 7, 1810, Grinder was brought before a grand jury at Savannah"; Anthony/Spencer 1883 statement: "Grinder who was part Indian was charged with the killing"); (9) Grinder genealogy and census work (Robert Sr d. 1848; sons Albert and Noble "in fits of insanity, committed suicide by hanging"); (10) Lewis's cash: "about $120 in currency" plus the $99.58 check; Neelly gave Pernia fifteen dollars (Phelps: ten). Donald Jackson "not only read the book in manuscript but corrected ..." and wrote Fisher (1 Sept 1960): "You have convinced me that Neelly probably was a dishonest man and that Mrs. Grinder was a real nut" (Guice 1998, from the Fisher papers at Yale); Jackson's public verdict: "verbose and inexact ... like a detective following a very cold trail"; "Both Fisher and Dillon use a disquieting technique in the absence of hard evidence; they attempt to discredit contemporary witnesses" (*Letters* 2:748). Added to the evidence: items (1)-(3) are the durable ones — every later murder writer's document case rests on Fisher's discovery that Russell's 1810 letters differ from the 1811 statement and that the 1811 statement is a copy.

**1962; 2nd ed. 1978. Donald Jackson, ed., *Letters of the Lewis and Clark Expedition*** [QUOTATION ONLY for the editorial matter; the Russell excerpt read in the WPO 28:1 reprint of 2:573-74] — SUICIDE, hedged: "Thoughtful men still hold opposing views on whether Lewis killed himself or was slain ... I am inclined to believe that Lewis died by his own hand" (2:575n); later, *Among the Sleeping Giants* (1987) p. 21: "Most historians now believe Captain Lewis shot himself ... Nobody can ever be sure because there were no eyewitnesses" (Chuinard pt 3). Added: the standard printed texts of Neelly, Wilson, Russell 1811, Jefferson 1813, Clark 28 Oct 1809, Pernier's letter, the inventory (2:472), the Jefferson-Madison correspondence on Pernier's wages; the identification of "J. Williams" as Jonathan Williams, first superintendent of West Point; the omission of Russell's two January 1810 letters (Gale's standing complaint, WPO 42:4).

**1965. Richard Dillon, *Meriwether Lewis: A Biography* (Coward-McCann), 364 pp.; reprinted 1988 with a foreword by Ambrose** [SNIPPETS: ~400 fragments, `dillon_1965_meriwether_lewis_fts_snippets.txt`; long quotations in Guice 1998 and Jenkinson 2011] — MURDER.
"Was Meriwether Lewis murdered? Yes. Is there proof of his murder? No ..." (p. 344, quoted by Guice 1998); "Sensitive he was; neurotic he was not"; Lewis the "anti-suicide type" (Jenkinson's summary); the killer "either an unknown land pirate of the ilk of the Harpe brothers of bloody Natchez [Trace] notoriety, or the mysterious Runnion" (p. 348, via Cutright 1986) — the first appearance of Tom Runions in print that these notes found (C section 6); Clark's niece said Clark "insisted that his friend had not taken his own life" (p. 338); no evidence of family insanity (p. 342 — but see Chuinard on Dr John Marks, 1819); Pernier's death by laudanum, 29 Apr 1810 (p. 346, no source given — Chalkley 2004); Daniels's Wilkinson/Bates/Pernier guess reported (p. 348). Ambrose's 1988 foreword is reported by Gale (Starrs & Gale 2009) as saying "Yes it was murder" [not verified by me]. Added to the evidence: nothing documentary beyond Fisher; the Runions name.

**1976; 1986. Paul Russell Cutright, *A History of the Lewis and Clark Journals* (1976), p. 51; "Rest, Rest, Perturbed Spirit," WPO 12:1 (Feb 1986): 7-16** [WHOLE for 1986; `cutright_1986_rest_rest_perturbed_spirit_WPO12-1.txt`] — SUICIDE: "a victim, in my opinion, of his own hand" (1976); "To this writer, the murder/robbery theory of Meriwether Lewis's demise lacks legs to stand on" (1986, p. 16).
Three-part case (Lewis's 1807-09 troubles; the events; the posthumous controversy) and a list of the murder writers' "flaws": time lapse; "oral tradition has it"; contradictory wound traditions (three shots, cut throat, razor cuts); attempts to discredit Grinder, Neelly and Jefferson; failure to agree on a perpetrator. Added: the Dickerson diary line; the genealogy of the murder story from the 1848 committee through Park, Coues, Wheeler, Bakeless, Fisher, Dillon; Kushner summarised for WPO readers. (Cutright mis-titles Phelps's article and calls Moore "J. F. Moore".)

**1981. Howard I. Kushner, "The Suicide of Meriwether Lewis: A Psychoanalytic Inquiry," WMQ 3d ser. 38, no. 3 (July 1981): 464-481; reworked in *Self-Destruction in the Promised Land* (Rutgers UP, 1989), ch. "Two Strategies" (Lewis and Lincoln)** [1981: QUOTATION ONLY via Cutright (notes 70-72 = Kushner 470, 471-479, 481); 1989: SNIPPETS, 360 fragments, `kushner_1989_self_destruction_in_the_promised_land_fts_snippets.txt`; JSTOR blocked] — SUICIDE assumed; the question is why.
Historians are "ill-equipped to deal with the question [of suicide] because they appear to know so little about the dynamics of suicide"; "Suicides are more prevalent among those who as children experienced the death of a parent"; risk-seeking ("placing his life in constant danger, his addiction to alcohol"); conclusion (p. 481): "Suicide emerges as an alternative of last resort for the incomplete mourner when other ritualized alternatives prove either insufficient or unavailable. The life of Meriwether Lewis is suggestive of incomplete mourning and a psychoanalytic approach to his death can lead us to a fuller understanding of his life." The 1989 book repeats the narrative from Neelly and Wilson ("Mrs. Grinder's accounts remain the only eyewitness reports of Lewis's death"), cites "Gilbert C. Russell to Thomas Jefferson" for "Lewis's alcoholism", finds "insubstantial evidence that Lewis was murdered", and repeats Bakeless's "Neelly's slave Tom". Added to the evidence: nothing; an interpretive frame that Jenkinson, Peck and Holmberg later use in modified (bipolar) form.

**1991-92. E. G. "Frenchy" Chuinard, "How Did Meriwether Lewis Die? It Was Murder," WPO 17:3 (Aug 1991) 4-12, 17:4 (Nov 1991) 4-10, 18:1 (Jan 1992) 4-10** [WHOLE; three files `wpo_chuinard_*`] — MURDER (by Neelly, in his reconstruction; Grinder second suspect).
An orthopaedic surgeon and founder of WPO. "I agree with Richard Dillon that proof of Lewis not having committed suicide is lacking; but that there is reasonable doubt." Central claim (pt 2, pp. 7-8): "To me, a surgeon, it is unbelievable that Lewis could survive the second shot for two hours"; Russell's downward trajectory "explains a shot fired at a man rising from bed"; "Nothing is said of powder stains"; "no description of blood marking Lewis's trail". Nine questions (Neelly all night after horses; Mrs Grinder's three accounts; two-hour survival; why not reload; Russell's statement two years later; the woman afraid to give water; Mr Grinder and the inquest "as tradition holds"; the money). Sixteen-point summary in pt 3. Relies on: Neelly, Wilson, Russell 1811, Phelps (attacked point by point), Fisher, Bakeless's jury tradition, local lore ("Mr. Grinder was suspect from the first ... the statements of some of the jurors afterwards that they were afraid to render any other verdict than death by suicide"). New material: Dr Harper to Reuben Lewis, 12 May 1819 (Drumm ed., *Luttig's Journal*, 150-51) on the confinement of Lewis's half-brother Dr John Marks — the one documented case of mental illness in the family, contra Dillon p. 342; Jackson's letter to Fisher; Jackson's evolving view. Added: the medical argument for homicide in its standard form (see B section 5 for it in full, with the pathologists' answers) and the Neelly-as-killer scenario. Rogers (WPO 31:4, 2005) later showed that the head-then-chest sequence Chuinard reconstructs is itself an assumption first fixed by Russell 1811.

**1992. James J. ## 2. The homicide theories, each in its strongest form

For each: the theory as its best advocate states it; the documentary evidence actually cited; the earliest date I can find it in print; the strongest objections; and what evidence would confirm it. The physical setting, distances and the concordance of Mrs Grinder's versions are in C sections 2-3; the wounds and the forensic literature in B section 3; I do not repeat them.

### 2a. The Grinders (Robert Grinder, with or without his wife's complicity)

**Strongest form** (Coues 1893; Park 1891; Fisher 1962; Guice 1998/2006; Gale 2009-16). A well-dressed, well-armed traveller with money, a gold or silver watch, a rifle, a brace of pistols and two trunks arrives alone at a remote stand run by a family "of Indian blood"; the husband is said to be away; the only witness is his wife, whose story "does not hang together" (Coues) and changed three times (Neelly 1809; Wilson 1810/11; the 1839/1845 account with "two or three men" at dark, three shots and Pernier in Lewis's clothes) — see C section 3. She heard the shots, heard him call "O madam! give me some water, and heal my wounds", watched through the chinks, and gave no water and raised no alarm for "two hours" on a night with no moon, which means she could not have "seen" what she described (Guice 2002). The money was gone: Russell (31 Jan 1810) says Lewis had "more than One hund $ in Notes & Specia besides a Check I let him have of 99 58/100 none of which it is said could be found"; Fisher: "about $120 in currency"; D&J: "$220 in notes, specie, and a $99.58 treasurer's [check]"; the pocket-book vanished after supper (Bakeless). Local tradition says Grinder "was at once suspected of the murder, ran away, was captured on Cane creek, brought back, and tried; but the proof not being positive, he was released" (Park 1891); that the coroner's jury "were Cowards and were afraid to bring in a verdict of murder ... the murderers had Indian Blood in their Veins" (Whiteside per Frierson, in Bakeless); that a "blood-stained Masonic apron" was seen (Gale); that the Grinders prospered afterwards (Park; Pease: "plantations and slaves"). Two of Grinder's sons later "in fits of insanity, committed suicide by hanging" (Fisher's census work). Mrs Grinder's story "is a concoction on the part of an accomplice in crime, either before or after the event" (Coues). Chuinard: "I believe Mrs. Grinder was totally dishonest in her stories."

**Documentary evidence actually cited.** Neelly's and Wilson's transmissions of Mrs Grinder's account (the only 1809-11 evidence); Russell's 31 Jan 1810 letter on the missing money; the Hickman County deed of 1814 (100 acres, $250); the 1839/1845 newspaper account; the 1848 report's "lonely spot ... assassin". No court, inquest or grand-jury record exists: "no written records have ever been found" (Nashville Scene 2003, in C); Fisher: "If, as folklore has it, there was a coroner's inquest, no formal record of it survives"; Gale: "coroners inquests for this time period in Maury County went unrecorded".

**Earliest in print.** 1848 (the committee's unnamed "assassin", with the loneliness of the place as the ground); Grinder by name, Park, *Nashville American*, 6 Sept 1891; adopted by Coues 1893.

**Strongest objections.** (1) The whole case is built on the one witness it declares a liar; if her account is worthless there is no evidence that Grinder was absent, present, or anywhere. (2) The two servants, who slept "in the Stable loft some distance from the other houses", came in, heard Lewis speak ("I have done the business my good Servant give me Some water" — Neelly; "I am no coward ..." — Wilson), and neither then nor later accused the Grinders: Pernier went to Clark's family and to Monticello and told Jefferson; Clark reported Pernier's account (26 Nov 1809) without a hint of foul play. (3) The Grinders "were respectable Hickman County residents" by the public records (Holmberg 2006; Phelps: "a respected citizen"), and the "prosperity" is 100 acres for $250 five years later (Bakeless; Moore 1904's Hickman *Register* material in C). (4) Robbers do not leave two trunks of papers, a rifle, pistols, dirk, tomahawk, watch and the horses; the watch was "found in Lewis's trunk in 1810" (Danisi; Coles 5 Jan 1810 lists "His Watch"); Holmberg 2006: "Documents from Lewis's own records prove that he was not carrying enough money ..." [to be worth killing for]. (5) The arrest-and-trial story has no record and a contrary tradition of equal age: Col. R. M. Cooper (1854-55, via Wright 1894) said Grinder "was not suspected". (6) The 1845 "three men at dark" version is the latest, least attributable and most contradictory of Mrs Grinder's tellings (C table B). (7) The same witness's account contains the details (the request for water, the begging to be shot, "so hard to die") that the murder side must also discard.

**What would confirm it.** A Maury County inquest, grand-jury or court record naming Grinder (none found; C section 6 records the searches); the Grinders' later possession of an identifiable item (the $99.58 Treasury check, the "Pocket Pistol", the watch); on exhumation, non-contact-range wounds (a black-powder contact shot leaves soot and tattooing — B section 3d) or a wound from behind.

### 2b. John Pernier, the servant

**Strongest form** (Russell 31 Jan 1810; M. L. Clark 1848; the Lewis family via Wheeler 1904; Fisher 1962 "Malinda's Story"; Chandler 1994; Gale 2015; Mandrell 2018). Pernier — "a Spaniard or some sort of a 'furiner'" to the Grinders' servant Lindy, a "Frenchman" to M. L. Clark, in fact a free man of colour who had been a White House servant (Jones 2005) — was owed $240 in wages that Lewis could not pay; he was alone with the dying man; the 1845 account has "Mr. Pyrna" next morning wearing Lewis's clothes and carrying "Mr. L's gold watch in his pocket", and "Mr. P. buried him, and took possession of his effects"; Russell, who had spent two weeks with both men, wrote that "from the Statement of Grinders wife where he Killed himself I can not help beleiving that Purney was rather Aiding & abeting in the murder than otherwise"; Lewis's mother, who questioned Pernier at Locust Hill, "never entertained the idea for a moment that he had committed suicide. The theory that the family have ever advanced is that he was murdered by his Spanish servant" (Goodlett, in Wheeler); the family's WSJ statement in 2010: "She supposedly saw through" him; Lindy said Pernier "cut his throat from ear to ear in Mrs. L's yard"; Pernier in fact died seven months later of laudanum (Suverman to Jefferson, 5 May 1810; Jefferson to W. D. Meriwether, 21 Aug 1810: "poor Pierney his servant, who lately followed his master's example") — guilt, or silencing (Gale adds him to Wilkinson's victims; Mandrell has Wilkinson's son pay him). M. L. Clark's letter to Cressey: "murdered by his servant, a Frenchman, who stole his money and horses, returned to Natchez".

**Documentary evidence actually cited.** Russell 31 Jan 1810 (`sources/1810-01-31_russell_to_jefferson.md`); Jefferson to Madison 26 Nov 1809 (the $240 wage claim); Pernier to Jefferson 10 Feb 1810; Suverman 5 May 1810; the 1845 account; the 1848 report (M. L. Clark); Wheeler's 1904 family letters.

**Earliest in print.** As a private accusation, Russell's letter of 31 Jan 1810 (unpublished until Fisher 1962). In print, the 1849-50 committee report (M. L. Clark's letter). The family tradition of Lucy Marks first in Wheeler 1904.

**Strongest objections.** (1) Pernier rode 600 miles to Locust Hill and Monticello to report the death, carrying the news to Clark's family on the way, arrived by 21 Nov 1809 (Crosby 2013), and Jefferson — who talked to him, gave him $10 and wrote Madison for his wages — took him as an eyewitness of a suicide (Jefferson 1813; Ambrose: "gave him an eyewitness account"); Clark's family had his report that Lewis "would frequently Conceipt that he herd me Comeing on". A murderer does not carry his account to the victim's two closest friends. (2) The horses: Neelly's letter says one horse "was lost in the wilderness" and "the other I have Sent on by his Servant", so Pernier "stole his ... horses" is false on the only 1809 evidence. (3) The watch was in the estate's hands (Coles, 5 Jan 1810: "His Watch & one or two other articles of value, I will either carry to Virginia my se[lf]"), which disposes of the 1845 "gold watch in his pocket" and of the Mississippi "sighting" (Chalkley 2004). (4) Russell dropped the accusation in his 1811 statement (editorial note in the sibling file) and never repeated it. (5) The Lucy Marks tradition is a c. 1900 recollection of a c. 1820 opinion, recorded by Wheeler with his own doubt ("in error as to the servant and papers"); Holmberg 2006: "No contemporary ..." [source records it]; Chalkley: the Lewis-Marks papers show no such suspicion; Ravenholt claims the opposite (that she "readily accepted" suicide), also without a source. (6) The 1839/1845 account is the one that also has three shots, "O Lord! Congress, relieve me", and strangers at dark. (7) Pernier's laudanum death is consistent with a destitute, grieving servant "facing debtor's prison" (Gale's own phrase) as much as with guilt; "no documented confirmation of Pernier's actual suicide" beyond Suverman's letter (Chandler 315, per Chalkley).

**What would confirm it.** Any 1809-10 statement by Lucy Marks, Reuben Lewis or John Marks accusing Pernier (the UVa Lewis-Marks papers have been searched by Chalkley and Gale; Gale cites #29 for the watch, not for an accusation); Pernier's possession of Lewis's money or check; a deposition of Neelly's servant.

### 2c. James Neelly

**Strongest form** (Fisher 1962 "James Neelly: Chief Witness"; Chuinard 1991; Turnbow 2012/2015; Gale). Neelly is the sole source of the story that reached Jefferson and the press, and the sole authority for Lewis's "derangement" on the road; he was not there ("I came up Some time after"); his tale of two horses lost the night before, which kept him behind while a man he knew to be deranged rode on alone, is a pretext; he arrived to bury the body "as decently ... as I could" without any inquiry and took "in my Care his Rifle, Silver watch, Brace of Pistols, dirk & tomahawk"; he kept the pistols and dirk and "carries the dirk and pistols constantly with him" (John Marks, 1811), claimed a loan "which cannot be so for he had none himself" (Russell), and was "extremely fond of liquor, [and] instead of preventing the Govr from drinking or puting him under any restraint, advised him to it" (Russell 31 Jan 1810); Russell said that had he sent his own man instead of Neelly, Lewis would be alive. Chuinard's reconstruction: Neelly entered the cabin after midnight to rummage the trunk, Lewis woke, Neelly fired, the second shot to the chest killed him almost at once, and Mrs Grinder's story is a cover for robbery. Turnbow's evidence turns the theory into a cover-up: the Williamson County file shows Neelly at Franklin on 11 Oct 1809 answering *Masterson & Co. v. Neelly* under a capias for his body, seventy miles away; therefore the Jefferson letter's account of the lost horses and of his arrival is "a fiction created by someone other than Agent Neelly", written in Brahan's hand and signed with a signature unlike Neelly's; the "suicide account was contrived to shield Major Neelly" from the charge of having abandoned his charge and from the loss of Lewis's money and effects; Neelly was dismissed in 1812 on charges (Brown to Eustis) that Turnbow says included attempted murder.

**Documentary evidence actually cited.** Neelly's letter; Russell 4 and 31 Jan 1810; John Marks's note (Fisher p. 136; Carrick); the Williamson County court file (capias, note, bond, minutes) and the Agency letter of 18 Oct 1809 (NARA RG107 M221); Brown to Eustis 11 June 1812; the Hogg draft of 24 Sept 1809 (Danisi); the Chickasaw chiefs' Feb 1809 request (Danisi).

**Earliest in print.** Russell's private letter, 1810; Fisher 1962 (the "chief witness" chapter); Chuinard 1991 as killer; Turnbow 2012 as cover-up.

**Strongest objections.** (1) The best evidence against Neelly's account — the court record — also proves he was not at Grinder's Stand that night, which ends the Chuinard scenario; Turnbow's own theory has Neelly seventy miles away and someone else (an unnamed robber) killing Lewis. (2) Pernier's independent report to Clark's family and to Jefferson matches Neelly's on every point that matters (deranged on the road; two shots; the words; the wounds), and Pernier owed Neelly nothing. (3) Danisi 2014: Neelly was sixty-eight, was asked for by the Chickasaw chiefs as an old gentleman "not so fond of Speculation", had drawn $100 from Hogg (so Russell's "he had none himself" is wrong), had an attorney of record, and the minutes contain no verdict — the appearance may have been by attorney or by the son George. (4) Neelly's letter to Jefferson is candid about what he took and asks for instructions; he sent one horse and $15 with Pernier; Jenkinson: "not the villainous character most murderists make him out to be, but he did appropriate a number of Lewis's personal objects." (5) Even the House letter of 28 Sept 1809 — written before the death, from a man "immediately from the Bluffs" — reports the suicide attempts on the boat; the "derangement" story does not originate with Neelly. (6) Russell's charges against Neelly were made in the same letter in which he half-accused Pernier, by a man who had lost the pistols he wanted "held Sacrad" for Lewis's friends, and whose own two accounts of Fort Pickering disagree.

**What would confirm it.** Resolution of the Franklin record (whether the bond required and recorded personal presence on 11 Oct — the two readings in Danisi 2014 and Turnbow 2015 are both in print; the file itself is in Franklin, TN); the text of Brown's 1812 charges; the second 18 Oct 1809 letter's place of writing; recovery of the pistols; Neelly's estate papers.

### 2d. Tom Runions (Runnion/Runion) and other local men

**Strongest form** (Fisher 1962 from Pinkerton and Milligan; Dillon 1965 p. 348; Avery 1978 thesis via Gale 2016; Guice 2006). Local tradition names Runions, "an occasional land pirate" who "later married into the Grinder family" (Guice), or Grinder's nephew (Gale), as the man who shot Lewis "in the back ... while Lewis was out on a walk" (Guice 2006) or "from behind" (Gale); Whiteside's jury suspected him "because his moccasin tracks and the impression of the butt of his unusual rifle were found in the dirt near Lewis's cabin" (Dillon, per Open Library summary in C; Fisher: "print of the rifle in the dust"); the post-rider Smith found the body "some distance from the house with mortal wound in the back and with wadding at the stable" and "no powder stains on the clothing or on the flesh" (Fisher); Bakeless: "lying near the road, 100 or 150 yards from the Grinder cabins".

**Documentary evidence actually cited.** None from 1809-1850. Twentieth-century interviews (Fisher's Pinkerton and Milligan; Bakeless's Webster and Frierson letters; Avery 1978, Middle Tennessee State University thesis, pp. 65-67, per Gale).

**Earliest in print.** Dillon 1965 p. 348 (C section 6: not found in any pre-1965 printed source examined for these notes; not verified in Fisher 1962's snippets, where "Runnions" appears with the tracks story).

**Strongest objections.** (1) It contradicts every 1809-11 account on the one point they agree on: Lewis was shot in the cabin, spoke afterwards, and asked for water; nobody in 1809 reports a body by the road. (2) The post-rider story fails on the census: Robert O. Smith was born c. 1806 (Holmberg 2006). (3) Tracks and a rifle-butt print "in the dust" outside a stand on a public road prove nothing about a killing inside it; the story surfaces 130 years later. (4) The Runions-Grinder marriage, if real, is a fact about a family, not a night.

**What would confirm it.** On exhumation, a gunshot wound entering the back or the back of the skull (Starrs's own test: "If a bullet entered the back of Mr. Lewis's skull, for instance, suicide would be unlikely" — WSJ 2010); the 1809-10 Maury County records; documentation of Runions himself (census, marriage, indictments) — none produced by any writer.

### 2e. Robbery by Trace bandits

**Strongest form** (Bakeless 1947; Dillon 1965; Guice 1998/2006/2016; the 1924 Memorial Association; Turnbow 2012). The Natchez Trace in 1809 was a wilderness road on which robbery was a known trade (the Harpes and Mason a decade earlier; the Van Meter saddlebag theft of $671 in 1809 — Gale; Governor Willie Blount's 1811 request for policing of the Trace — Turnbow); a "person traveling in the style of Lewis" was "a prime target" (Guice 1995); the money was gone and "Robbery was the obvious motive" (Chuinard); an outlaw who found a feverish man alone in an outlying cabin, fired, and fled before the household stirred explains the shots, the missing money, the untouched trunks (too heavy to carry off in the night), and a frightened family's silence; the Memorial Association told President Coolidge in 1924 that "Investigations have satisfied the public that he was murdered, presumably for the purposes of robbery" (Gale 2008), and the national monument was created in 1925 on that understanding. Guice's settled position (2016): "he probably died at the hands of an unknown outlaw."

**Documentary evidence actually cited.** The missing money (Russell 1810; Fisher; D&J); the 1848 report's "lonely spot"; the 1924 petition; the general history of the Trace (Coates 1930, *The Outlaw Years*); the Van Meter theft and Blount's letter as evidence of conditions.

**Earliest in print.** The 1848 committee, by implication ("lonely spot ... assassin"); Pease 1894 (Murrell); Bakeless 1947 ("Pure hypothesis, of course, but not by any means an impossibility"); Dillon 1965 ("land pirate of the ilk of the Harpe brothers").

**Strongest objections.** (1) Phelps: by 1809 the Trace was the post road and "the most heavily traveled road of the southwest"; Ambrose: "No robbery had been reported for years"; C section 6: "No documented robbery or murder on the Trace in 1808-1810 near Grinder's was found in the sources consulted". (2) No 1809 account has a stranger: Mrs Grinder's two contemporary tellings have Lewis alone in the cabin, the servants in the loft, and the shots inside the room; Lewis's reported words claim the act. (3) A robber leaves nothing behind that he can carry: the rifle, the pistols (loaded and fired — by whom?), the dirk, the tomahawk, the watch and the horses were all there in the morning. (4) The 1924-25 petition is a fund-raising document, not an investigation; "Investigations" refers to nothing on record. (5) Jenkinson's point stands: the outlaw is where the murder case retreats "when the argument for conspiracy breaks down".

**What would confirm it.** A contemporary record of a robbery or an armed band on that stretch of the Trace in October 1809; the Treasury check turning up; on exhumation, wounds inconsistent with self-infliction (range, direction).

### 2f. Political assassination: Wilkinson, Burr, the Spanish silver, and Jefferson

**Strongest form** (Daniels 1961 as a guess; Chandler 1994; Gale 2009/2015/2016; Schrag & Haze 2011; Mandrell 2018; Stroud 2018 for the cover-up half). General James Wilkinson — commanding general of the army, Spanish "Agent 13", Burr's partner and betrayer, Lewis's predecessor as governor and the man whom Lewis's own August 1809 letter to Eustis invokes ("my Country can never make 'A Burr' of me") — had every reason to fear what Lewis was carrying to Washington: the vouchers and papers that Lewis said would explain his protested drafts and that he refused to send by sea "least they might fall into the hands of the British", the record of the Ste Genevieve lead-mine and land frauds (John Smith T., the Austins and John Rice Jones), and, in Gale's fullest form, Wilkinson's plan with Smith T. to seize the silver mines of Mexico. Lewis had removed Smith T. from office and had sided with Clark against Wilkinson's people. The machinery: Benjamin Wilkinson, the general's nephew, was on Lewis's boat; "Mr. X" (Paymaster Ambrose Whitlock) went from the fort to make arrangements; the "two attempts on the boat" and the fifteen days of derangement were invented at the fort and then fixed in the record by Russell's forged 1811 statement, which Wilkinson composed during his court-martial while Russell "goes AWOL to save his life"; Neelly, a Wilkinson appointee, delivered the victim and staged the lost horses; Robert Grinder killed him; Brahan wrote the "Neelly" letter at Nashville with a forged signature and three matching letters of his own; the forged Russell letters that Clark received in November 1809 sealed Clark's belief; Jefferson, who "knew the truth", said nothing, because an inquiry would have exposed Wilkinson's treason and his own dependence on him; Pernier, who "tells Jefferson what really happened", was dead of laudanum within five months. Supporting circumstances: no investigation was ever ordered by Jefferson, Madison, Eustis or Clark (Gale 2016: "Why Didn't Meriwether Lewis's Two Best Friends Investigate His Death?"); Clark never visited the grave; Lewis's papers reached Coles "so badly assorted" (Coles 10 Jan 1810); Jefferson's "hypochondriac affections" was an "obligatory reference" (Gale); Richards testified in 1996 that neither Russell nor Williams wrote the 1811 statement; Russell's two 1810 letters ("in about Six days he was perfectly restored") cannot be reconciled with the "15 days" Clark reported — "The same man could not have written both statements."

**What Lewis actually knew about Wilkinson, Burr and the silver** (as far as the sources here go). Lewis and Wilkinson had known each other for fifteen years and were fellow Masons and members of the Society of the Cincinnati (Danisi 2009); Lewis attended Burr's treason trial in Richmond in August 1807 (Guice 2006 fragment) and as governor prosecuted Smith T. and the lead-mine claimants; his 18 Aug 1809 letter to Eustis protests his loyalty against any imputation of Burrism. Whether he carried evidence against Wilkinson is Chandler's and Gale's inference; no such document is identified. The "Spanish silver" in Gale's 2016 letter concerns Joanne Trogdon's claim that Clark carried Spanish silver upriver to Sebastian and Wilkinson in June 1798 — Clark, not Lewis.

**Documentary evidence actually cited.** Russell's 1810 letters against the 1811 statement; Richards's and Dillon's 1996 testimony; Clark to Jonathan 26 Nov 1809 (the lost Russell letters); the House letter (read as a plant); the Brahan letters and the "Neelly" signature; the Agency letter of 18 Oct 1809; the Van Meter and Laneheart records; Coles 1810; Wilkinson's documented treason (Spanish pension; Burr). All of it is genuine; the connective tissue is inference.

**Earliest in print.** Daniels 1961-62 ("as good a guess as any is that Wilkinson ordered it, Bates arranged it, Pernia did it"); as a worked thesis, Chandler 1994.

**Strongest objections.** (1) Wilkinson was in New Orleans from March 1809 to February 1810 and letters took months; "remote control" murder was impossible (Danisi 2009); Holmberg 2024: "no way"; Jenkinson: conspiracies "frankly strain credulity". (2) The suicide-attempt story pre-dates the death and did not come from the fort's officers: House wrote to Bates from Nashville on 28 Sept 1809 that a man "immediately from the Bluffs" reported "several attempts to put an end to his own existence" prevented by "the patroon" — before Lewis reached Grinder's; and Clark's own reason for believing the first report was Lewis's lost letter from New Madrid, written before Fort Pickering (Clark 28 Oct 1809). (3) Pernier — no Wilkinson man — told Clark's family and Jefferson the same story, including Lewis's delusion that Clark was following; the theory must therefore make Pernier either a paid killer (Mandrell; Daniels) or a silenced witness (Gale), for which there is nothing. (4) The forgery claims rest on Richards's testimony that the 1811 statement and its signature are not in Russell's or Williams's hand — which is what Fisher already knew ("copied from a copy") and what a copy in a clerk's hand looks like; Richards did not testify to forgery of content, and the two Russell letters Clark received in 1809 have never been seen by anyone, so their "forgery" cannot be examined. (5) Gale's own description of her twenty documents as "a mixture of truth, lies, rumors and outright forgery" (Danisi 2009). (6) Holmberg's review of Chandler listed more than thirty factual errors; Guice, the leading murder advocate, called Chandler "totally without credibility" and, of Gale, kept to his "unknown outlaw". (7) The absence of an investigation is explained by the suicide side without conspiracy: everyone who could have ordered one believed the reports (Arlen Large 1996: the "most telling indicator" of suicide is the inaction of Jefferson and Clark).

**What would confirm it.** A Wilkinson, Smith T. or Brahan document ordering, paying for or reporting the killing; Spanish-archive material (Nicandri 2018); a handwriting examination of Brahan's four letters and the Agency letter side by side (Turnbow says two examiners matched the bond signature; nobody has published an examination of the Jefferson letter's signature); the missing Russell letters to Clark; Pernier's estate or a statement by him beyond his 10 Feb 1810 letter to Jefferson.

### 2g. A killing by Neelly's servant, or by Pernier, in a quarrel

**Strongest form** (Bakeless 1947; Dillon 1965; Kneifl 2008; the Horsley 1894 "old negro" story; Lindy's account in Moore 1904). Only two men were near enough to act: Lewis's own servant, unpaid and abused ("They have told lies on me and want to ruin me" — Lewis to Lindy, of unnamed persons), and Neelly's Black servant, whom Bakeless identifies (wrongly) as Reuben Lewis's slave "Captain Tom" and who "used to shake his woolly head and say that the whole affair was better not discussed". A deranged master who "drank a very little", paced for hours talking "like a lawyer", loaded his pistols and asked for powder, may have quarrelled with a servant who came to the cabin; a struggle over the pistols, two shots, and a servant's razor to finish it, then a story agreed with a terrified woman. Horsley (1894) reported an "old negro man" body-servant dying near Charlottesville c. 1886-87 saying it was murder (C section 6; Kneifl 2008 repeats a Virginia newspaper version). Russell's "Aiding & abeting" and Lucy Marks's suspicion (2b) apply here too.

**Documentary evidence actually cited.** None contemporary except Russell's phrase. Bakeless's 1946-47 informants; Horsley 1894.

**Earliest in print.** Russell 1810 (private, Pernier); 1849 (Pernier); the "old negro" 1894; Neelly's servant as a suspect, Bakeless 1947/Dillon 1965 (C section 6).

**Strongest objections.** Both servants slept in the stable loft "some distance from the other houses" (Neelly), "about two hundred yards off" (Wilson), were fetched by the children "as soon as day broke" (Wilson) or "awakined by her" (Neelly), and are the source, through Pernier, of Lewis's last words; both carried the news onward; no one in 1809-11 accused Neelly's servant, whose name is not even recorded; the "quarrel" has no witness and no motive in the record beyond the wage debt, which Pernier pursued lawfully through Jefferson and Madison.

**What would confirm it.** Identification of Neelly's servant and any statement by him; a deposition by Pernier beyond his letter; physical evidence of a struggle (none recorded).

## 3. The suicide case as its proponents put it

Assembled from Phelps 1956, Jackson 1962/78, Cutright 1986, Holmberg 1992/2006/2024, Ambrose 1996, Jenkinson 2011, Peck 2009/2022, West 2017, with the documents as transcribed in `sources/`. The medical side (what condition he was in; whether the wounds are consistent) is B sections 2, 4 and 5; the timeline is C section 1.

### 3a. The chain of evidence, in the order the proponents lay it

1. **Lewis's state before he left St Louis.** Clark to Jonathan, 26 Aug 1809: "I have not Spent Such a Day as yesterday for maney years ... if his mind had been at ease I Should have parted Cherefully"; Lewis had put his landed property in the hands of Judge Stuart, Carr and Clark to pay his debts (Holmberg 1992). William Carr to his brother, 25 Aug 1809: "I apprehend he will not return" (D&J 279; Jenkinson). Bates to Richard Bates, 14 July 1809 (the quarrel; `sources/1809-07-14_...`). Frick's ledger: debts of $4,709.22 against assets of $2,365.50 including the protested bills — pressed, not bankrupt. The Eustis letter of 15 July 1809 and Lewis's reply of 18 Aug ("my Country can never make 'A Burr' of me"). Jenkinson 2011 (p. 391, per Bartley 2013): "the proximate cause of Lewis's suicide was the affair of honor forced upon his sensitive soul by William Eustis, James Madison, and the War Department; coupled with a toxic mix of physical, mental, and spiritual illness; exacerbated by excessive use of alcohol".

2. **The will and the lost letter, New Madrid, 11 Sept 1809.** The will (recorded copy; Richards 1996: the recorded text is in a clerk's hand) leaves everything to his mother. Clark, 28 Oct 1809, gives his reason for believing the first report of suicide: "my reason for thinking it possible is founded on the letter I received from him at your house" — the New Madrid letter, now lost (Holmberg n. 24: Clark may have destroyed it). The suicide side reads the will as a man settling his affairs before he meant to die; the murder side (Guice; Kneifl) as ordinary prudence before a river-and-sea voyage.

3. **The two attempts on the boat, 11-15 Sept 1809.** Three witnesses, two of them before the death: (a) James House to Frederick Bates, Nashville, 28 Sept 1809 — Major Stoddard had met a man "immediately from the Bluffs" who reported that Lewis had arrived "in a state of mental derangement", "that he had made several attempts to put an end to his own existence" which "the patroon had prevented", and that the commanding officer kept "a strict watch over him to prevent his committing violence on himself" (`sources/1809-09-28_house_to_bates_FRAGMENTS.md`; Jenkinson: this "proves that the story of Lewis's suicide attempts ... did not surface after Lewis's death"); (b) Russell's letters to Clark, reported by Clark on 26 Nov 1809: Lewis "had attempted to kill himself before he got there"; (c) Russell's statement of 26 Nov 1811: "learning from the Crew that he had made two attempts to kill himself, in one of which he had nearly succeded". Ambrose: "whether by jumping overboard or with his pistol" — the method is nowhere stated. Objections the proponents acknowledge: Russell's 4 Jan 1810 letter does not mention attempts and says "in about Six days he was perfectly restored in every respect & able to travel"; Fisher: the "attempts" may have been "delirium in wild actions and talk which someone at the fort, possibly Russell, interpreted as an attempt"; Chalkley: of the attempts and the second will, "most likely neither of them ever happened". The *Missouri Gazette*, 2 Nov 1809: "extreme mental debi[lity]" — the St Louis paper's own gloss.

4. **The letters from Fort Pickering.** Lewis to Madison, 16 Sept 1809 (`sources/1809-09-16_lewis_to_madison.md`): "having taken medicine feel much better"; the editors note the double complimentary close and "troubled mannerisms" that "support" Neelly's "deranged in mind". Lewis to Stoddard, 22 Sept 1809: "lucid, coherent" (Chuinard), planning to return — the murder side's favourite document. The suicide side answers that lucid intervals are what Russell describes ("on the sixth or seventh day all symptoms of derangement disappeared ... for ten or twelve days") and what a relapsing illness or a mood disorder produces.

5. **The road.** Neelly: "on our arrival at the Chickasaw nation I discovered that he appeared at times deranged in mind, we rested there two days & came on"; Russell 1811: "in three or four days he was again affected with the same mental disease ... in the apprehension of being destroyed by enemies which had no existance but in his wild immagination, he destroyed himself"; Pernier, via Clark 26 Nov 1809: "on his way to Nashville, he would frequently 'Conceipt that he herd me Comeing on'" — an independent report of delusion from the one man who rode beside him. Mrs Grinder (Wilson): "sometimes he would seem as if he was walking up to her; and would suddenly wheel round, and walk back as fast as he could"; his face flushed "as if it had come on him in a fit"; "talking aloud, as she said, 'like a lawyer.'"

6. **The night.** Two shots (every source); "I have done the business my good Servant give me Some water" (Neelly, from the servants, within a week); "he had now done for himself" (the *Clarion*, 20 Oct 1809); "I am no coward; but I am so strong, so hard to die"; "He begged they would take his rifle and blow out his brains, and he would give them all the money he had in his trunk" (Wilson, from Mrs Grinder); "he had killed himself to deprive his enemies of the pleasure and honor of doing it" (Russell 1811, closing sentence, source unstated). Jenkinson on the words: "has the ring of truth", and the phrase "so hard to die" is Lewis's own from 11 May 1805 ("these bear being so hard to die reather intimedates us all"). The razor: *Clarion* ("cut his neck arm and ham with a razor"); Madison to Jefferson 30 Oct 1809 ("had recourse to his Dirk"); Russell 1811 ("busily engaged in cuting himself from head to foot"); the razor is missing from the Freeman inventory that lists its box (Turnbow). Peck & Peck: exsanguination from the two gunshot wounds and the cuts.

7. **The contemporaries' unanimity.** Everyone who wrote in 1809-1813 with any knowledge said suicide. The list, exactly: James Neelly (18 Oct 1809, "I am Sorry to Say by Suicide"); John Brahan (three letters, 18 Oct 1809); the *Nashville Democratic Clarion* (20 Oct 1809, "under the influence of a deranging malady"); the *Missouri Gazette* (2 Nov 1809); the *National Intelligencer* (13 and 27 Nov 1809); William Clark (28 Oct, 30 Oct, 8 Nov, 26 Nov 1809); Jefferson (to Conrad 23 Nov 1809; to Madison 26 Nov 1809; to Allen 18 Aug 1813); Madison (30 Oct 1809: "had recourse to his Dirk"); Eustis (25 Oct 1809); Frederick Bates (9 Nov 1809: "die in such a manner"); Clement Penrose (per Bates: "the mental derangement of the Governor ought not to be imputed to his political miscarriages; but rather to the barbarous conduct of the Secretary"); Mahlon Dickerson ("Poor Meriwether Lewis killed himself this night"); Gilbert Russell (4 and 31 Jan 1810; 26 Nov 1811); Alexander Wilson (1810/11); John Pernier (his account to Clark's family and to Jefferson, and his own letter of 10 Feb 1810, which asks for wages and says nothing of murder); Priscilla Grinder (through Neelly and Wilson); the anonymous friend who wrote the obituary (D&J 329). The nineteenth-century notices to 1874 follow (Cutright). Ambrose: "[Neither] Jefferson nor Clark ever doubted that Lewis killed himself"; Jenkinson: the murder list is "entirely blank, with the possible exception of Lewis' mother Lucy Marks"; Merritt: "unanimous".

8. **Jefferson's diagnosis.** To Allen, 18 Aug 1813: "Governor Lewis had, from early life, been subject to hypocondriac affections. it was a constitutional disposition in all the nearer branches of the family of his name, and was more immediately inherited by him from his father ... while he lived with me in Washington, I observed at times sensible depressions of mind"; Neelly "arriving there two days after, found him extremely indisposed, and betraying, at times, some symptoms of a derangement of mind"; "he did the deed which plunged his friends into affliction". The 1819 confinement of his half-brother Dr John Marks (Chuinard) and the Gilmer daybook on his father (Flick 2021) are the suicide side's corroboration of "constitutional".

9. **The absence of any contrary contemporary voice, and the lateness of the murder story.** Phelps: "not a scintilla"; "nearly thirty years"; Moore 1904: "comparatively new"; Cutright; Holmberg: M. L. Clark "planted the murder seed" in 1848; Ambrose: "no question about suicide for the first 50 years".

### 3b. The contemporary doubts, examined one by one

* **Frederick Bates.** No doubt about the manner of death; his 9 Nov 1809 letter is about his own conduct ("I had no personal regard for [him] ... die in such a manner") and reports Penrose's charge that "the mental derangement of the Governor" was due to Bates's "barbarous conduct" — i.e. St Louis in November 1809 argued about the cause of the derangement, not about whether there was one.
* **The Nashville press.** The *Clarion* of 20 Oct 1809, nine days after, printed suicide "under the influence of a deranging malady for about six weeks" and Gray's Elegy; no Nashville paper of 1809-10 is known to have printed a murder rumour (Phelps; Giesecke's survey of 1809 papers found none). The one contemporary editorial retreat runs the other way: the *National Intelligencer*, having reprinted the Russellville letter and the *Clarion* on 15 Nov 1809, said on 27 Nov that later information "induces us to think that statement in several respects incorrect" and substituted Brahan's letter — it dropped the razor and throat details, not the suicide (A section 3, item 21).
* **Russell.** The one contemporary who used "murder", against Pernier, in January 1810 — and only as "Aiding & abeting" a self-killing "from the Statement of Grinders wife". He dropped it in 1811.
* **Lewis's family.** Lucy Marks: no contemporary record of her opinion; the tradition (Goodlett, Quarles) is in Wheeler 1904, and the family repeated it in 2009-10 ("believed he was murdered"; "She supposedly saw through" the servant). The family's 1810-11 actions — John Marks recovering the horse and rifle from Neelly's wife, the estate settlement, the papers sent to Jefferson — contain no accusation (Chalkley; the Lewis-Marks papers). Ravenholt's contrary claim is unsourced. Reuben Lewis and John Marks: no recorded doubt.
* **William Clark.** In 1809: "I fear this report has too much truth"; "the weight of his mind has overcome him"; he passed on Russell's and Pernier's reports without demur and "would have written Jonathan voicing his objection" if he had one (Holmberg); Ambrose: he would "have gone to Tennessee". Later: Bakeless (Dye MSS) and Dillon p. 338 report a niece's saying that Clark "never believed he committed suicide"; Jenkinson's 2002 letter took this seriously ("retreated from his early acceptance"); Holmberg 2024: Clark "takes it as a given that Lewis killed himself" and there is no writing of Clark's to the contrary; Buckley (2006 p. 129): Jefferson and Clark "had a long talk ... It may be that Jefferson told Clark not to pursue the issue" — a conjecture.
* **Jefferson.** The "subsequent evidence" story (a lost volume "loaned out" by Dr Wilson Randolph) is "rather dubious ground" (Bakeless).
* **The 1848 committee.** Thirty-nine years later, on the loneliness of the spot and M. L. Clark's letter (section 5c).

### 3c. The "hesitation wounds" reading of the razor cuts

The suicide side reads the cuts as a change of method after two failed shots — what the forensic literature calls "unplanned complex suicide", in which "the mode of performance is changed after the first method chosen failed or was working too slow or proved to be too painful" (B section 3d, citing Bohnert & Pollak; about 4% of suicides). The phrase "hesitation wounds" in the strict sense (shallow tentative cuts beside a deeper one) is not applicable, because nobody described the cuts' depth, number or distribution beyond "neck arm and ham", "from head to foot" and "the arteries in his thighs and arms"; B: "Anything about the razor wounds' character (hesitation-type versus defensive) ... nobody described." The murder side reads the same cuts as either an assailant's (Chuinard: inconsistent with suicide; Kirkpatrick answers "consistent with murder?") or as invented ("It may be that Neelly invented the slashings" — Jenkinson, who notes that Mrs Grinder's two accounts do not mention them). What the record allows: cuts to the neck, arm(s) and thigh(s) are where a man seeking his own arteries cuts, and where an attacker rarely does; defensive wounds on the hands and forearms, the usual sign of an attack with a blade, are nowhere mentioned [INFERENCE from B section 4c].

### 3d. "I am no coward; but I am so strong, so hard to die" — how independent are the sources?

Three 1809-11 texts carry Lewis's last words. Neelly (18 Oct 1809), from the servants: "I have done the business my good Servant give me Some water." The *Clarion* (20 Oct 1809): "he had now done for himself" [per Giesecke's transcription]. Wilson (May 1810 or 1811), from Mrs Grinder alone: "O madam! give me some water, and heal my wounds"; "He begged they would take his rifle and blow out his brains, and he would give them all the money he had in his trunk. He often said, 'I am no coward; but I am so strong, so hard to die.' He begged the servant not to be afraid of him, for that he would not hurt him." Russell (26 Nov 1811), source unstated but presumably Neelly and/or Pernier: "he lay down and died with the declaration to the Boy that he had killed himself to deprive his enemies of the pleasure and honor of doing it." Ambrose runs Wilson and Russell together ("He said he had tried to kill himself to deprive his enemies of the pleasure and honor of doing it"). So: the "so hard to die" sentence has one source (Mrs Grinder to Wilson); the "done the business / done for himself" declaration has the servants as source (Neelly; the *Clarion*, presumably from Neelly or the same Nashville informants); the "deprive his enemies" declaration is Russell's, two years on. They are independent in the sense that Mrs Grinder and the servants are different people, and consistent in substance; they are not independent of the household at Grinder's Stand, which is the murder side's point. Jenkinson's stylistic argument — that "so hard to die" is a Lewis phrase from 1805 that a Tennessee innkeeper's wife could not have invented — is the strongest internal evidence for authenticity [and, I note, also consistent with a woman repeating what she actually heard: INFERENCE].

## 4. The accident hypothesis, and what the wounds allow

### 4a. What "accident" has meant in this literature

Nobody argues a simple accident (a pistol discharging while being loaded or cleaned): two shots from two pistols followed by razor cuts exclude it, and no writer since 1809 has proposed it. "Accident" in the debate means Danisi's thesis — self-inflicted, not suicidal — first stated in WPO 28:1 (Feb 2002), restated in his Aug 2002 letter ("I argue that Lewis intended to alleviate his suffering by wounding himself—not by killing himself"), developed in Danisi & Jackson 2009 (ch. "malaria as cause of, 307-20"; "an accident that looked like suicide"; "That was an accident as unfathomable as a crash on the highway"), in *Uncovering the Truth* (2012; "an act of desperation"; "non-suicide") and in the WPO articles of 2010-14. The mechanism: chronic malaria with a "severe intermittent paroxysm" produced delirium in which Lewis, like the patients of Pringle, Alibert, Senac and Macculloch, attacked "the offending part" — "self-surgery, not suicide"; the fits at Fort Pickering (five days, then "perfectly restored") and the relapse "three or four days" out of the fort are the waxing and waning of the fever; Jefferson's "hypocondriac affections" is the eighteenth-century physical disease of the hypochondrium (liver and spleen), not a temperament; and the moral and legal category "suicide" therefore does not apply. Holmberg 2024 summarises it fairly: "Thomas Danisi concludes that Lewis unintentionally took his own life."

A second, older version is Fisher's: that the Fort Pickering "attempts" were "delirium in wild actions and talk which someone at the fort, possibly Russell, interpreted as an attempt" — i.e. that the record of intent is a misreading of fever. And a third is Peck's and Jenkinson's incorporation of malaria as one ingredient of a suicide ("a toxic mix of physical, mental, and spiritual illness"; Peck: "milder malaria" plus opium, alcohol and "other psychological problems").

### 4b. The objections in print

Loge 2002 (physician): "ague" was nonspecific in 1803; the journals show no relapse pattern; febrile delirium "does not lead to mental illness or dementia during the intervening periods"; malaria sufferers are bed-bound and could not have ridden alone to Grinder's; there are no modern reports of self-destructive idiosyncrasies in proven malaria; "Danisi's contention is opinion and speculation." Peck 2009/2013: cerebral malaria is excluded (a victim could not ride ten days); vivax fever is "less likely" to have triggered an attempt; falciparum has no latent liver stage, so a "chronic" infection is "virtually impossible"; Rush's "hypochondriasis" was a mental category; the malaria-and-"hypochondriac affections" theory is "devoid of credibility". Rogers 2010: Jefferson's usage of "hypochondriac" 1787-1816 means "a pessimistic disposition or temperament". Crosby 2013: a lexicographic survey 1661-1812; Danisi & Jackson "falsified Pringle's text at least four times". Jenkinson 2011: the distinction is one "no competent historian" needs — "Lewis killed himself but did not commit suicide"; the World Trade Center jumpers analogy; "Over time, Danisi's argument will fade away". The medical note's own review (B section 2a) finds the 1809 fever season and Lewis's history make malaria plausible as an illness, and the "self-surgery" mechanism unsupported.

### 4c. What the wounds allow

From B sections 3d and 4 (the forensic case series, and the sequence step by step): the described sequence — a contact or near-contact shot to the forehead that furrows the skull without entering the brain, a second shot to the lower chest passing "downward thro' his body" to exit "low down near his back bone", ambulation, speech and a request for water for some hours, then cuts to neck, arm and thigh with his own razor, death at sunrise — "is the recognised shape of a failed-then-completed suicide"; "Nothing in the described wounds is impossible or even extraordinary; equally, nothing in them excludes an assailant"; the number and sites of wounds are "directionally unstable" indicators; and what would decide it — "range (soot, singeing, tattooing on a black-powder wound), trajectory, the state of the hands, the character of the cuts — was never recorded". Applied to the accident hypothesis: nothing in the wounds distinguishes a man who meant to die from a man who meant only to "alleviate his suffering by wounding himself"; the distinction is one of intent, and the only evidence of intent is what he is reported to have said ("I have done the business"; "he had now done for himself"; begging the servants to "blow out his brains"; "so hard to die"; "to deprive his enemies of the pleasure and honor of doing it") — every one of which is the language of a man who meant to die, and every one of which Danisi must read as delirium. The "point-blank miss" argument (Danisi 2002: "how could he have missed two large vital organs") is answered in B: in multiple-shot suicides the first head shot commonly fails to enter the brain, and torso shots predominate; Rogers adds that the order of the shots is itself an assumption. The two-hour survival, "the point on which the homicide case has rested medically since Chuinard, is the least remarkable part" (B section 4d).

### 4d. Summary

The accident hypothesis is a reading of the same self-inflicted wounds with a different account of the mind behind them. It accepts every document the murder side rejects (Jenkinson's point) and differs from the suicide side only on the meaning of "hypochondriac" and on whether a feverish delirium can produce purposeful self-wounding without suicidal intent. No physical evidence can settle that; the documentary evidence of intent (the reported words) is against it, unless the words are discounted, and the medical literature cited by its critics is against the mechanism.

## 5. Provenance disputes over the key documents

### 5a. Russell's statement of 26 November 1811

**What it is.** A document in the papers of Jonathan Williams (1750-1815; first superintendent of West Point) at the Lilly Library, Indiana University, printed by Jackson (*Letters* 2:573-75) as "Gilbert C. Russell: Statement, November 26, 1811". It is in Williams's hand (so Jackson and Fisher); it carries at the foot a signature "Gilbert C. Russell" and an endorsement, printed in Guice 2006's documents section: "The above was received by me from Major Gilbert Russell of the [blank] Regiment of Infantry U.S. on ... November 1811 at Fredericktown in Maryland. Signed J. Williams". Its content: Lewis "landed him at Fort Pickering in a state of mental derangement, which appeared to have been produced as much by indisposition as other causes"; the Crew's report of "two attempts to kill himself, in one of which he had nearly succeded"; five days of derangement, recovery "on the sixth or seventh day", "ten or twelve days" well; departure 29 Sept; relapse "in three or four days"; "in the apprehension of being destroyed by enemies which had no existance but in his wild immagination, he destroyed himself, in the most cool desperate and Barbarian-like manner"; the two shots with the forehead "furrow" and the downward chest wound exiting "low down near his back bone"; the walk to Mrs Grinder's door; the razors from the portfolio; "cuting himself from head to foot"; the declaration "to the Boy that he had killed himself to deprive his enemies of the pleasure and honor of doing it" (this last sentence in Chuinard's reprint; the WPO 28:1 excerpt cuts before it). It is the only source for the trajectory of the second shot, for the phrase "two attempts", and for the razors coming "from a port folio".

**Is it signed?** Not in the autograph sense. Fisher 1962: "The Russell document is copied from a copy"; the copy "at the Univ. of Indiana library, made by Jonathan Williams. Why he made a copy of it we don't know"; "this amazing document in which Russell did ..." [fragment]. Richards (1996): "it became very obvious that Russell did not write the Russell statement that's purported to describe what happened"; "Williams did not write the Russell signature. ... Neither he [Williams] nor the purported writer of it who signed at the bottom wrote those particular documents"; Williams "probably did not write that signature Jonathan Williams. Someone else wrote it." Duayne Dillon, the second examiner, concurred (Starrs & Gale; Guice 2006 pp. 83-84 per the index). So on the two examiners' evidence the Lilly document is a copy of the statement and of its endorsement made by a third hand — a clerk's or secretary's copy — and the only thing "forged" about it, in the technical sense, is that the signatures were written by the copyist [INFERENCE from the testimony as quoted; Richards is nowhere quoted as saying the content was fabricated]. Whether an original in Russell's hand ever existed is unknown; none has been found.

**What Guice, Danisi and Jackson say.** Jackson printed it without reservation as Russell's statement, dated and placed it, and in 1978 still "inclined to believe that Lewis died by his own hand"; his one recorded doubt is the 1960 letter to Fisher about Neelly and Mrs Grinder. Guice (2006) prints Richards's testimony and concludes that it "vindicates Vardis Fisher's opinion of the Russell statement", adds that "Russell's 1811 statement must have been based on Neelly's report" (2002), and in 2016 treats Gale's forgery thesis as possibly right while keeping his own outlaw. Danisi (2012, per Jenkinson's review) "does not discredit the legal affidavit of Gilbert Russell, recorded on November 26, 1811", and (May 2013) claims his pp. 154-77 "prove who wrote the letters and for what purpose"; Danisi & Jackson use it as the record of the malaria relapse. Gale: "WILKINSON COMPOSED THE FORGED RUSSELL STATEMENT during his first court [martial]" — Williams and Russell were both at Frederick for Wilkinson's court-martial in November 1811, which is also the innocent explanation of how Williams came to take a statement from Russell there [INFERENCE]. Holmberg 1992 n. 51: Clark's report of 26 Nov 1809 ("15 days in a State of Derangement"; "attempted to kill himself before he got there") "is consistent with Russell's 1811 statement", which answers the objection that the statement was written two years after the event; Jenkinson adds the House letter of 28 Sept 1809 as a pre-death witness to the same story. The unresolved discrepancy is internal to Russell: 4 Jan 1810, "in about Six days he was perfectly restored in every respect & able to travel", and the indisposition "attributed Solely to the free use he made of liquor" (31 Jan 1810), against 1811's "mental derangement ... produced as much by indisposition as other causes" and the "two attempts". Guice, Gale and Fisher make this the crux; Holmberg, Jenkinson and Danisi read the 1810 letters as Russell's account of the recovery and the 1811 statement as the fuller record.

### 5b. Wilson's letter: reliability, date, and hostility to the Grinders

**Text and transmission.** Alexander Wilson (1766-1813), the ornithologist, who had known Lewis in Philadelphia and drawn birds for the expedition, rode the Trace in the spring of 1810, stopped at Grinder's, "took down from Mrs. Grinder the particulars", paid Grinder to fence the grave, and wrote it up in a letter to Alexander Lawson. Printed in the *Port Folio* (Jan 1812) dated "May 28th, 1811"; in Ord's 1828 life of Wilson and Grosart's 1876 edition dated 18 May 1810; Coues dated it 18 May 1811; the calendar settles it: Wilson says he "left Nashville" on "Friday morning, May 4th", and 4 May was a Friday in 1810 but a Saturday in 1811, so the *Port Folio*'s "1811" is wrong and the letter belongs to May 1810, seven months after the death (A section 3, item 22; B section 6 had left it open). Kushner cites "Wilson to Joseph Dennis [Dennie], 28 May 1811"; Ambrose "May 28, 1811, in Elliott Coues". It is the only account with Mrs Grinder's speech reported at length, and the only 1809-11 source for the pacing, the flushed face, "like a lawyer", "O Lord", "O madam! give me some water, and heal my wounds", the stagger and the stump, the bucket and the gourd, the two hours, the children sent at daybreak, "blow out his brains", "so hard to die", and death "just as the sun rose".

**Reliability.** For: written within seven to nineteen months, by an educated man with no stake, from the witness herself, at the place; unfavourable to the teller in a way a fabricated cover story would not be (Jenkinson prefers Wilson's timing over Neelly's for that reason); consistent with Neelly on the shots, the words and the wounds. Against: it is Mrs Grinder's second telling and differs from the first (Neelly: "no person there but a woman" and the servants "awakined by her" at once; Wilson: children present, servants sent for "as soon as day broke and not before"); it reports her seeing, through the open logs, things a moonless night would not have let her see (Guice 2002); Coues found it "wildly improbable"; Chuinard: "theatrics ... did not happen"; and Wilson's hostility to the household is on the page — "this cooling element was denied the dying man!"; "the terror of the woman having permitted him to remain for two hours in this most deplorable situation"; "a few loose rails thrown over his grave"; the fence money and Grinder's "written promise". Ord's footnote in 1828 speaks of "the rash deed" and of "the base imputations and cruel neglect" Lewis had suffered — the friends' view of the cause, not a doubt of the act. Both sides use Wilson: the murder side for the neglect and the impossible seeing, the suicide side for the words. [INFERENCE: the hostility runs against the Grinders' conduct, not against the suicide account; Wilson evidently believed what he wrote down, and so did Ord.]

### 5c. The 1848 committee's "assassin" sentence

Text, membership, enabling act, what was examined and Coues's verification are in C section 4 and section 1a above. The points at issue: (1) The sentence is an opinion ("It seems to be more probable") whose only stated grounds are that the spot was "lonely ... wild and solitary" and M. L. Clark's letter; the report does not say that the bones showed anything about the manner of death, and "the upper part of the skeleton" was examined for identification ("such evidences found as to leave no doubt of the place of interment"). (2) The tradition that the skull showed the forehead wound (Pease 1894, from Nixon; Cooper's "the wound in the head, the coffin and the nails corresponded") is consistent with every account. (3) The "in the back" inference (Gale 2016) has no source in the report or in any 1848 witness. (4) The committee wrote at a moment when the murder story had a champion in the family (M. L. Clark's "the only stigma upon the fair name I have the honor to bear"), and Moore 1904 and Holmberg 2006 date the murder tradition's spread from this publication; against that, Cooper's and Park's informants describe a local suspicion of Grinder from 1809, which cannot now be tested. (5) Turnbow's 1905 Maury County Court resolution (a Masonic reburial in 1848; an inquest "over the body" in 1809) is a 1905 recollection and adds nothing to what was examined. Net: the sentence is the earliest printed statement of murder and carries the weight of a committee's impression, not of an examination.

### 5d. Mrs Grinder's changing story

The concordance is C section 3 (tables A and B). The versions and their carriers: (1) via Neelly, 18 Oct 1809 — alone, deranged, two shots at "about three OClock", servants roused, "I have done the business", died "a short time" after; (2) via Wilson, 1810/11 — children present, pacing and talking, two shots, "O Lord", the request for water and the two hours, "so hard to die", died at sunrise; (3) the "Arkansas schoolteacher" account of 1839, printed 1845 (*New York Dispatch*; Draper 29CC33) — "two or three men" rode up at dark, Lewis met them with pistols, "About 9 o'clock first shot heard—five minutes later second shot. Three shots fired", "O Lord! Congress, relieve me", "Mr. Pyrna" in Lewis's clothes with the gold watch, "Mr. P. buried him, and took possession of his effects", the "mentally deranged" description retained; (4) Christina Anthony's and Lindy's/Polly Spencer's tellings (Park 1891; Moore 1904; Bakeless 1947), with "They have told lies on me", "If I had not done it, some one else would", the bleeding wound near the heart, and death at noon; (5) Fisher's "Mrs. Grinder's Third Version" (the 1845 text) and the post-rider/jury traditions. Readings: Phelps — the variations are "probably" hysteria or lapse of memory (p. 316); Chuinard — "totally dishonest"; Coues — "concoction"; Gale — "inadmissible", but "the third Mrs. Grinder story may be more truthful" (after Robert's death in 1827); Jenkinson — uses only (1) and (2), which agree "so far", and treats (3) as late and contaminated; Holmberg 2006 — the public records contradict "Spencer's and Anthony's" pictures of the Grinders; Fisher — treats (3) as evidence of Pernier's guilt or the Grinders' fear. What is stable across all five: two (or more) shots inside the cabin at night; Lewis alive and speaking afterwards; his servant present at the end; Mrs Grinder awake, frightened and unhelpful. What varies: the hour (three o'clock; "just before day"; nine o'clock); who was present (no one; children; strangers); the number of shots (two; three); the words; the time of death (soon; sunrise; noon).

## 6. The 2010 Interior decision, the arguments on exhumation, and what an exhumation could and could not show

### 6a. The decision

The full chronology 1992-2026 is C section 5; the two documents of 2010 are transcribed in `sources/scholarship/lewis_family_2010_appeal_letter_wsj.txt` (Col. Thomas McSwain's appeal of 19 Apr 2010, with the Tennessee letter of 9 Oct 2009) and `sources/scholarship/interior_strickland_2010-08-25_final_decision_letter.txt`. In outline: Starrs's proposal to the Natchez Trace Parkway superintendent (1992); refusals (1995, 9 July 1996); the coroner's jury (3-4 June 1996) and the State's petition (16 Dec 1996); the ARPA application by Starrs and Bass (June 1997), denied by the Regional Director (24 Sept 1997); *In re Exhumation of Meriwether Lewis*, 999 F. Supp. 1066 (M.D. Tenn. 24 Mar 1998): the State's petition dismissed, "ARPA is simply the only remedy", "The bones of Captain Lewis will remain undisturbed, at least for the present"; Senator Murkowski's intervention (2002); Assistant Secretary Lyle Laverty's letter of January 2008 finding the exhumation "appropriate and in the public interest" given "the unique circumstances of the death of Meriwether Lewis" and the "overwhelming support" of descendants; the family's ARPA application of 26 Jan 2009 (Starrs as applicant; Hugh Berryman as principal investigator; Kevin Smith of Middle Tennessee State University as field director; SWCA environmental assessment; $8,145 spent); the NPS letter of June 2009 promising an environmental assessment; the family's press conference and SolveTheMystery.org (June-July 2009); Interior's re-review "after a reported Congressional query" (April 2010); the denial of 2 Apr 2010; the appeal; and Assistant Secretary Thomas L. Strickland's letter of 25 Aug 2010: the NPS "had denied requests for exhumation for over a decade prior to the 2008 decision by then-Assistant Secretary Laverty"; Little Bighorn and NAGPRA cases are distinguishable; $3.5 million of Recovery Act funds were being spent on the site; "no compelling arguments exist that are sufficient to overturn [park service] management policies ... The information proposed to be gained (determination of cause of death) does not add to the commemoration of Governor Lewis' accomplishments. While a dispute over the circumstances of his death might be a matter of some public interest, it is not necessarily a sufficient reason to ignore [park service] policy" (as quoted 27 June 2010, C section 5); "Please consider this a final decision." The policy: NPS Management Policies 5.3.4 — graves are not disturbed "unless threatened with destruction by park development, operational activities or natural forces" (quoted by the family and by the WSJ). NPS spokesman William Reynolds: "It's not clear that the exhumation would result in a clear understanding of his death and the could result in harm to other graves during the process"; the pioneer cemetery around the monument holds "109 other graves, including some that have yet to be located". No later application, suit or bill has been found (C section 5).

### 6b. The arguments for exhumation, as made

* **The family** (McSwain 2010; Bowen and Henley in WPO 2009 and the WSJ): more than 200 collateral descendants signed; "Our family wants to put an end to the mystery hanging over how Uncle Meriwether died"; Lucy Marks "believed he was murdered"; Bowen: the suicide hypothesis is "preposterous"; a "Christian reburial"; precedents on federal land — Zachary Taylor (1991), Little Bighorn (1984), the Unknown Soldier (1998), Blackburn Cemetery on the Parkway itself (1985); the grave was already opened in 1848 ("examined the upper portion of the skeleton"), so the policy's premise of an undisturbed grave fails; the 1996 jury's unanimous finding of "very little tangible evidence"; the governors of Missouri, Tennessee and Virginia and the Tennessee district attorney supported it; Laverty had approved it.
* **Starrs**: "There is a solid and reasonable possibility that he did not die by his hand" (1995); "I want to give voice to Meriwether Lewis through his bones" (1997); bullet trajectories could yield clues — "If a bullet entered the back of Mr. Lewis's skull, for instance, suicide would be unlikely" (WSJ 2010); his 1992 radar survey "indicates that there is a vault underneath the monument".
* **The 1996 witnesses**: Francisco — "The bones are still there. The bones are still examinable"; Bass on preservation in that soil; the jury's recommendation that "exhumation is necessary for closure".
* **Guice**: the 2000 Jenkinson book "illustrates why the integrity of American scholarship demands a forensic examination of Lewis's remains" (2001); "Forensic science may well provide evidence to eliminate the possibility of suicide" (1998); the NPS refusal "leaves room for the assumption that these guardians of our history do not want the truth uncovered" (2013).
* **Ravenholt**: "If Lewis's skeleton is exhumed, it may be possible to discern evidence of syphilitic osteitis", and mercury or DNA might be recovered — an argument for exhumation from the suicide side, to test a cause.
* **Buckley** (2006): "Exhume the body! Oh, the story those bones might tell". **Chalkley** (2009): the inquest record is "a sober legal finding in favor of exhuming". **Peck & Peck** (2021-22), suicide proponents, want exhumation; the forensic pathologist Glenn Wagner: "really good chance that his remains will not only be skeletal but with connective soft tissue". **Gale, Turnbow** and the Danisis' allies also support it.

### 6c. The arguments against, as made

* **NPS policy and practice**: graves not "threatened with destruction" are left alone; the site is a cemetery of 110 graves, some unlocated; "no purpose in resurrecting his body to prove how he died" (Sara Amy Leach, NPS, 1996); the January 1998 refusal by Regional Director Jerry Belson; the 2010 letters above; the funds committed to interpretation of the site rather than to its disturbance.
* **Ambrose** (1996): "Let the man rest. There is nothing we can prove." **Chalkley** (2002): "let sleeping heroes lie". **The Headwaters Chapter** of the LCTHF (1995 resolution, per Haraden) opposed. **Anne Barnes**, a cousin through the mother's line, opposed (1996). **Dave Borlaug** (North Dakota): "some things better left unsolved". The *Oregonian* (Nov 1996) backed the refusal. The **LCTHF board** (May 2008) voted "to decline to take a position". **Nicandri** (2018): a "cease-fire" until new sources appear.
* **What is left to examine**: Wilson found the grave with "a few loose rails thrown over his grave" to keep off the hogs; Fisher and Janoff: hogs got into the grave; the 1848 opening found "little more than a few small bones and some buttons" (Kirkpatrick 2002, from Fisher p. 191: "a few small bones, a couple buttons and an ornament"), against the report's "upper part of the skeleton examined"; local tradition (C section 4) that three bodies were opened before Cooper identified the grave by his nails, and that coins, buttons and an insignia were sealed between two flat rocks and reburied. The monument of 1848 sits on the grave; Starrs's radar "vault" is unconfirmed.
* **Starrs's critics on the suicide side**: exhumation would answer trajectory and range questions only if the skeleton is intact and the shot geometry preserved in bone; it cannot answer intent; and the documentary case for suicide would stand even if the bones were mute (Large 1996: the "most telling indicator" is documentary, not physical).

### 6d. What an exhumation could and could not show

From B section 3d (point 6) and section 4, with the specific claims made by the advocates:

Could, in principle, show: (1) whether the grave under the monument holds one individual, male, aged about 35, of Lewis's stature, and (if a maternal-line reference exists — the family "DNA" Starrs holds is unspecified in the sources) whether he is a Lewis; (2) the frontal bone: a tangential groove or "furrow" (Russell) versus a penetrating defect; and, decisively for the Runions/"from behind" tradition, whether there is any entry wound in the back of the skull; (3) the trunk: rib, sternal or vertebral damage fixing the path of the second ball and whether it is "downward" and "front to back" as Russell says, with the exit "low down near his back bone"; (4) a retained ball or a felt wad, either of which can indicate range (Smędra-Kaźmirska, in B); (5) fracture patterns as an index of energy and, therefore, of a pistol versus a rifle; (6) cut marks on cervical vertebrae or arm bones if the razor cuts reached bone [INFERENCE]; (7) syphilitic osteitis (Ravenholt's test) and mercury in bone; (8) if soft tissue or textile survive (Wagner's "connective soft tissue"), possibly soot or powder tattooing at a contact wound — the single most diagnostic fact for suicide versus homicide (B: contact range in 89% of suicides against 7.5% of homicides). Peck & Peck and the murder side agree on (2)-(5); they disagree on what they expect to find.

Could not show: (9) intent — the difference between Danisi's accident and everyone else's suicide is not in the bones; (10) who held the pistols, unless (2) or (8) produce a wound that could not be self-inflicted; (11) range, in the ordinary case where only bone survives (soot on bone at contact wounds is reported in the forensic literature but is not reliable after two centuries in a disturbed grave [INFERENCE]); (12) anything at all if the 1848 opening, the hogs and the wolves left "a few small bones", or if the monument's foundation destroyed the grave; (13) whether Mrs Grinder lied, whether Neelly was in Franklin, or what Russell wrote in 1811 — the documentary disputes are untouched by any finding. The most that the best case could establish is that Lewis was shot from behind (which no 1809-11 account allows and which would settle it for murder), or that the wounds are exactly as Russell describes (which would confirm Russell's account and, with it, the suicide narrative's physical side, without proving who fired).

## 7. Closing: what is settled, what is open, and where the recent authors stand

### 7a. Settled, on the documents (no serious writer on either side disputes these)

1. Meriwether Lewis died at Grinder's Stand, Tennessee, in the early morning of 11 Oct 1809, of gunshot wounds to the head and the trunk, and was buried there by Neelly's party the same day; the grave was reopened in 1848 and the monument placed on it.
2. He had been ill and, by the reports of everyone who saw him from 11 Sept 1809 onward, at times "deranged": Russell (1810, 1811), the man "immediately from the Bluffs" reported by House on 28 Sept 1809, Neelly, Pernier (via Clark), Mrs Grinder (via Neelly and Wilson), the *Missouri Gazette*, Jefferson. The "derangement" was reported before his death, not invented after it.
3. Every person who wrote in 1809-1813 with any knowledge of the event called it suicide (section 3a.7); no one ordered an inquiry; if a Maury County inquest was held, no record of it survives.
4. Money was missing according to Russell (Jan 1810); the trunks, papers, rifle, pistols, dirk, tomahawk, watch and horses were not; Neelly kept the pistols and dirk and did not return them.
5. The 1811 Russell statement is a copy in another hand (Fisher 1962; Richards and Dillon 1996); the two Russell letters Clark received in November 1809 are lost; Russell's own 1810 letters differ from the 1811 statement in emphasis and in the "two attempts".
6. The murder story first appears in print in the Tennessee committee's report (written 1848, printed 1849-50), whose only stated grounds are the loneliness of the spot and M. L. Clark's letter; the named suspects (Grinder 1891; Pernier 1849/1904; Neelly 1962/1991; Runions 1965; Wilkinson 1961/1994) all enter print long after the event.
7. James Neelly's name is on a Williamson County court file for 11 Oct 1809 (Turnbow 2012); what that proves about his whereabouts is disputed (Danisi 2014; Turnbow 2015).
8. The Grinders' documented later holdings were modest; no court record of an arrest or trial of Robert Grinder exists.
9. The Department of the Interior refused exhumation in 2010 as a "final decision"; nothing has changed since.

### 7b. Open

* The range and direction of the two shots, and whether they could have been fired by anyone but Lewis — unrecorded, and recoverable only from the bones, if at all.
* The razor cuts: real (the *Clarion*, Madison, Russell) or a Nashville embellishment (Jenkinson's doubt); their character.
* How long he lived and what he said: the household's word.
* Where Robert Grinder was; what Mrs Grinder saw on a moonless night; why no one helped for two hours.
* Where Neelly was, and what the "lost horses" were.
* What Russell wrote to Clark in November 1809, and whether the 1811 statement is Russell's own composition.
* What Lucy Marks believed, and on what basis.
* Who Neelly's servant was.
* Lewis's underlying condition (malaria, a mood disorder, alcohol and opium, neurosyphilis, some combination) — B sections 2 and 4.
* Whether the remains are intact enough to answer any of the first point.

Two qualifications. First, the split is not between the informed and the uninformed: of the six recent researchers who have found or worked most with new documents (Holmberg, Jenkinson, Danisi, Guice, Turnbow, Gale), two say suicide, one says accident and three say murder — in three incompatible versions (an outlaw; a robber with Neelly's cover-up; Wilkinson's conspiracy). Second, the public does not follow the historians: at the 2009 bicentennial panel the historians put suicide at 96% and the audience voted 350 to 20 for murder (Holmberg and Jenkinson, WPO 50:3), and the family's position has not changed since 1993.

**My own reading of the evidence, marked as such [INFERENCE]:** the documentary case for a self-inflicted death is contemporaneous, multiple, mutually consistent, and partly earlier than the death (House, 28 Sept 1809); the murder case consists of (a) legitimate doubts about the two witnesses on whom the narrative depends, (b) a real and unexplained loss of money, (c) Neelly's conduct, and (d) traditions first recorded forty to 140 years later, and it has never named a killer whom the 1809 documents permit. The one new document since 1992 that bears on the night — Turnbow's court file — takes the leading named suspect away from the scene. What would change this is what the Department of the Interior has declined to allow anyone to look at.
