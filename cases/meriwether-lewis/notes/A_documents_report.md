# A. Documents report — the death of Meriwether Lewis (Grinder's Stand, 10–11 October 1809)

Everything quoted below is copied from a file in `sources/`; the file name is given with every quotation so the reader can check it. Spelling in quotations is the original's (or the OCR's, where so marked). Superscripts in the Founders Online texts are flattened ("Oct r" = Oct^r, "11 th" = 11^th).

Reliability classes used in section 1:
- **SE** — scholarly-edition text copied verbatim from Founders Online (Princeton *Papers of Thomas Jefferson, Retirement Series* or *Papers of James Madison, Presidential Series*), including the editors' source note (RC/PoC/Dupl, endorsement, SJL) and footnotes. Highest reliability; the only loss is superscript formatting.
- **FR** — facsimile-read: transcribed by the researcher from page images (Chronicling America IIIF crops, or the Missouri Gazette microfilm on archive.org), with OCR used only as a draft. Reliable for words; doubtful letters are flagged [?] in the file.
- **OCR-PE** — uncorrected OCR of a printed scholarly edition or of the period printing (Thwaites 1904-05; Marshall 1926; Ord 1828; Grosart 1876; Coues 1893; Wheeler 1904; The Port Folio 1812). Wording reliable, but OCR errors (e.g. "i8th", "JeiFerson", "Missisififi") are left as they came.
- **SEC** — secondary transcription: passages of a manuscript quoted (with the original spelling) in a modern article, because the scholarly edition itself (Jackson 1978; Holmberg 2002) was not reachable. Quote with care; ellipses are the modern author's.
- **FRAG** — fragment: only phrases quoted by secondary authors were obtainable; the file says so and summarises what could not be seen.


**Documents of note** (for orientation): the complete text of the Nashville Clarion's notice of 20 Oct. 1809 (from the Kentucky Gazette's reprint, facsimile-read: no. 13); the Russellville letter of 20 Oct. 1809 with the Lexington editor's comment (from the National Intelligencer's reprint: no. 16) — the source of the "cut his throat" story Clark read; the Intelligencer's first notice of 13 Nov. (no. 15); the Missouri Gazette's three notices of 4 Oct., 19 Oct. and 2 Nov. 1809 read from microfilm (no. 5); Russell's letter of 4 Jan. 1810 with Lewis's memorandum and the inventory of what he carried (no. 24); the Pernier dossier — his own account, Süverman's two letters on his death by laudanum, Jefferson's letter to the executor (nos. 28, 34, 46, 37); Jefferson's 1813 sketch with the Princeton editorial note (no. 42); Lewis to Stoddard and Brahan to Stoddard from Thwaites (nos. 3, 7); Russell's 1811 statement and Clark's letters in the only reachable (secondary) transcriptions (nos. 41, 23); and the Neelly material (nos. 45, 48).

The sub-folders `sources/medical/`, `sources/scholarship/` and `sources/setting/` were made by the other research agents and are not listed here.

### (a) The wounds

- Neelly to Jefferson, 18 Oct. 1809 (`1809-10-18_neelly_to_jefferson.md`): "he had shot himself in the head with one pistol & a little below the Breast with the other".
- Brahan to Jefferson, 18 Oct. 1809 (`1809-10-18_brahan_to_jefferson.md`): "when they came in they found him weltering in his blood. he had shot himself first it was thought in the head. the ball did not take effect. the other Shot was a little below his breast, which proved Mortal: he lived until Sun rise & expired— the Maj r had him decently buried." (Editors' n. 2: the six words "he lived until Sun rise & expired" are interlined.)
- Brahan to Stoddard, 18 Oct. 1809 (`1809-10-18_brahan_to_stoddard_thwaites.md`, OCR of Thwaites): "found the unfortunate Governor weltering in his blood ; he had shot himself in the head and just below his breast — he died in about three hours". The same letter as printed by the National Intelligencer, 27 Nov. 1809 (`1809-11-27_national_intelligencer.md`): "He had shot himself in the head, and just below his heart; he died in about three hours."
- Nashville Clarion, 20 Oct. 1809, via Kentucky Gazette 31 Oct. (`1809-10-31_kentucky_gazette_reprint_of_clarion_20oct1809.md`): "he had shot a ball that grazed the top of his head and another through his intestines, and cut his neck arm and ham with a razor."
- Russellville letter, 20 Oct. 1809, via Lexington 28 Oct. and National Intelligencer 15 Nov. (`1809-11-15_national_intelligencer_lexington_28oct_russellville_letter.md`): "he took his pistols and shot himself twice, and then cut his throat." … "It is added, that Governor Lewis, in addition to shooting himself twice in the body, and cutting his throat, shot himself in the head, and cut the arteries in his thighs and arms."
- Madison to Jefferson, 30 Oct. 1809 (`1809-10-30_madison_to_jefferson.md`): "He first fired a pistol, at his head, the ball of which glancing, was ineffectual. With the 2 d he passed a Ball thro' his body, w ch being also without immediate effect, he had recourse to his Dirk with w ch he mangled himself considerably. After all he lived till the next morning, with the utmost impatience for death."
- Missouri Gazette, 2 Nov. 1809 (`1809-10-04_to_11-02_missouri_gazette_notices.md`): "before he could be prevented, discharged the contents of a brace of pistols in his head and breast, calling to his servant to give him a bason[?] of water; he lived about two hours and died without much apparent pain."
- Wilson, Port Folio 1812 (`1811-05-28_wilson_letter_portfolio_1812.md`, OCR): "on going in they found him lying on the bed; he uncovered his side and shewed them where the bullet had entered; a piece of the foreiiead [OCR: forehead] was blown off, and had ex- posed the brains, without having bled much. He begged they would take his rifle and blow out his brains, and he would give them all the money he had in his trunk. He often said, "I am no coward; but I am so strong, so hard to die.""
- Russell, statement of 26 Nov. 1811 (`1811-11-26_russell_statement_excerpts_wpo.md`, reprint A): "discharged one against his forehead without much effect—the ball not penetrating the skull but only making a furrow over it. He then discharged the other against his breast where the ball entered and passing downward thro' his body came out low down near his back bone." … "He then in returning got his razors from a port folio which happened to contain them and siting up in his bed was found about day light, by one of the servants, busily engaged in cuting himself from head to foot."
- Jefferson to Allen, 18 Aug. 1813 (`1813-08-18_jefferson_to_paul_allen_lewis_biography.md`) gives no wounds: "about three aclock in the night, he did the deed which plunged his friends into affliction".
- Monument committee report, 1849-50 (`1849_lewis_monument_committee_report.md`, Copy B): "to make assurance doubly sure the grave was reopened and the upper part of the skeleton examined, and such evidences found as to leave no doubt of the place of interment. Wit- nesses were called and their certificate, with that of the Sur- veyor, prove the fact beyond dispute." No wound or skull description is given.

### (b) The sequence of events on the night of 10–11 October

- Neelly, 18 Oct. 1809: "one days Journey after crossing Tennessee River & where we encamped we lost two of our horses, I remained behind to hunt them & the Governor proceeded on, with a promise to wait for me at the first houses he Came to that was inhabited by White people; he reached the house of a M r Grinder about Sun Set, the man of the house being from home, and no person there but a woman who discovering the governor to be deranged gave him up the house & slept herself in one near it, his Servant and mine Slept in the Stable loft some distance from the other houses, the woman reports that about three OClock She heard two pistols fire off in the Governors Room: the Servants being awakined by her, came in but too late to save him. he had shot himself … when his Servant came in he Says; I have done the business my good Servant give me Some water. he gave him water, he Survived but a short time, I came up Some time after, & had him as decently Buried as I could in that place". (Dupl. variants, editors' nn. 2-4: "house" for "houses" twice; "my" for "his" Servant.)
- Brahan to Jefferson, 18 Oct. 1809: "the Governor proceeded on with his Servant & Maj r Neellys—to the house of a M r Grinder where he arrived about sun set—no person being at home but the wife of M r Grinder—the woman discovering the governor to be deranged gave him up the house, and Slept herself in another house near it—the two Servants Slept in a Stable loft Some distance off: about three oClock the woman heard two pistols fire off. being alarmed She went & waked the servants when they came in they found him weltering in his blood."
- Brahan to Stoddard, 18 Oct. 1809 (OCR): "it is reported that he arrived there the evening before — the man of the house was from home — the governor went to bed in a room alone, about three o'clock the woman of the house who slept in a house near the other heard two pistols fire — she awoke the servants, and they rushed into the Room … in a few hours Major Neeley Agent to the Chickasaws came up, who had remained behind to hunt two horses which they had lost the night before — he had him intered".
- Clarion, 20 Oct. 1809 (Kentucky Gazette reprint): "came to the house of Mr. Grinder near the Indian line in this state—called for hs supper and some spirits, of which he partook, and gave some to his servants. ### (f) Neelly's conduct

- Neelly, 18 Oct. 1809: "I arrived at the Chickasaw Bluffs on or about the 18 th of September, where I found the Governor … in Very bad health" … "I furnished him with a horse to pack his trunks & c on, and a man to attend to them" … "we lost two of our horses, I remained behind to hunt them & the Governor proceeded on, with a promise to wait for me at the first houses he Came to that was inhabited by White people" … "I came up Some time after, & had him as decently Buried as I could in that place—if there is any thing wished by his friends to be done to his grave I will attend to their Instructions" … "I wish to be informed what arrangements may be considered best in Sending on his trunks &c". Editors: "James Neelly served as United States agent to the Chickasaw Nation from 8 July 1809 until 4 June 1812. He apparently served in the military during the War of 1812, and in 1817 Chickasaw leader Tishomingo recommended that he be reinstalled as agent (Madison, Papers … Pres. Ser., 4:592n; Jackson, Papers … 4:476)." Source note: "RC (DLC); in John Brahan's hand, signed by Neelly".
- Brahan to Jefferson: "he informs me that he left the Chickasaw Bluffs in Company with the Governor the last of Sep r on their way to this place" … "the Governor proposed to the Maj r to remain behind and find the horses, & that he would proceed on his Journey and wait for him at the first house from there inhabited by White people. to which the Major agreed" … "the Maj r had him decently buried."
- Madison, 30 Oct. 1809: "had set out under the care of a friend on a visit to Washington" … "he took advantage of the neglect of his Companion, who had not secured his arms, to put an end to himself."
- Jefferson to Madison, 26 Nov. 1809: "would it not be advisable that Major Neely should recieve an order on your part to forward them to Washington addressed to you, by the stage, & if possible under the care of some person coming on?" Madison, 11 Dec. 1809: "I have written to Maj r Neele, according to your suggestion, and shall follow it also as to the distribution of Gov r Lewis's papers when they arrive." (Editors' note to Conrad 11 Dec.: Clark's journal, 7 Dec. 1809, "Spoke much of the af s of Gov Lewis".)
- Russell, 4 Jan. 1810: "he set off with a Major Neely who was going to Nashville" … "presume they must be in the care of Major Neely near Nashville."
- Russell, 31 Jan. 1810: "James Neely the Ag t to the Chickasaws with whom Gov r Lewis set off from this place has detain'd his pistols & perhaps some other of his effects for some claim he pretends to have upon his estate—He can have no just claim for any thing more than the expences of his interment unless he makes a charge for packing his Two Trunks from the Nation—And for that he can not have the audacity to make a charge after tendering the use of a loose horse or two which he said he had to take from the Nation & also the aid of his Servant—He seem'd happy to have it in his power to serve the Gov r & but for his makeing the offer which was accepted I should have employ'd the man who packed the Trunks to the Nation to have taken them to Nashville & accompanyed the Gov r —Unfortunately for him this arrangement did not take place, or I hesitate not to say he would this day be living—" … "this Ag t being extremely fond of liquor, instead of preventing the Gov r from drinking or puting him under any restraint, advised him to it, & from every thing I can learn gave the man every chance to seek an opportunity to destroy himself" … "This Neely also says he lent the Gov r money which cannot be so for he had none himself".
- Russell, 1811: "On the 29th of the same month he left Bluffs, with the Chickasaw agent the interpreter and some of the Chiefs" … "He had no person with him who could manage or controul him in his propensities".
- Jefferson to Allen, 1813: "altho' he appeared somewhat relieved, mr Neely kindly determined to accompany & watch over him. unfortunately, at their encampment, after having passed the Tennisee one day's journey, they lost two horses; which obliging mr Neely to halt for their recovery, the Governor proceeded". (Editors' n. 49 records the wording "kindly" as an insertion — see file.)
- The Clarion (20 Oct.), the Russellville letter (20 Oct.) and the Missouri Gazette (2 Nov.) do not name Neelly at all; the Clarion's "the servants" are Pernier and Neelly's servant.
- Appointment and end of service, per Danisi (`neelly_james_dossier_SECONDARY.md`): "In August 1809, Robertson personally handed Neelly his commission as Chickasaw Agent, which ran for a term of three years, ending on June 4, 1812." … "There is no evidence Neelly's service as Chickasaw Agent ended for any reason other than the expiration of his three- year term." (Danisi's n. 61: Secretary of War to Neelly, 4 June 1812, M15 roll 3 p. 134; Neelly to Eustis, 3 July 1812, M221 roll 47.) The Princeton editors' "from 8 July 1809" is the date of appointment; Danisi says the commission was delivered in August and Neelly "wrote his first report to the Secretary of War on August 27".

## 3. Discrepancies noticed between the documents

1. **Where the balls struck.** Neelly: "in the head with one pistol & a little below the Breast with the other". Brahan to TJ: "first it was thought in the head. the ball did not take effect. the other Shot was a little below his breast, which proved Mortal". Brahan to Stoddard: "in the head and just below his breast"; the Intelligencer's printing of the same letter (27 Nov.) reads "just below his heart". Clarion: "a ball that grazed the top of his head and another through his intestines". Russellville letter: "twice in the body … shot himself in the head". Madison: "at his head, the ball of which glancing, was ineffectual … a Ball thro' his body". Missouri Gazette: "in his head and breast". Wilson (from Mrs. Grinder): the bullet entered "his side"; "a piece of the forehead was blown off, and had exposed the brains, without having bled much". Russell 1811: "against his forehead … the ball not penetrating the skull but only making a furrow over it"; "against his breast where the ball entered and passing downward thro' his body came out low down near his back bone". Jefferson 1813: no wounds at all. In short, "head" is common to every account, but "grazed/glancing/furrow" (Clarion, Madison, Russell) contradicts "a piece of the forehead was blown off … exposed the brains" (Wilson); and the body wound is "below the breast" (Neelly, Brahan), "through his intestines" (Clarion), "thro' his body" (Madison), "in his side" (Wilson) or breast-to-backbone (Russell).
2. **The cutting wounds.** Absent from Neelly, Brahan (both letters), the Missouri Gazette, Wilson and Jefferson. Present in: the Russellville letter ("cut his throat … cut the arteries in his thighs and arms"), the Clarion ("cut his neck arm and ham with a razor"), Madison ("had recourse to his Dirk with w ch he mangled himself considerably" — a dirk, not a razor), and Russell 1811 ("got his razors from a port folio … cuting himself from head to foot"). Turnbow's paraphrase "throat and 'hams'" (WPO 38:2) is not what the Clarion says.
3. **Hour of the shots.** Neelly, Brahan (both) and Jefferson 1813: "about three OClock"/"about three aclock in the night". Clarion: "some time before midnight". Wilson: she listened to him "for several hours" after "dusk" before the first shot. Russell 1811: "Some time in the night".
4. **How long he lived / when he died.** Neelly: "he Survived but a short time". Brahan to TJ: "he lived until Sun rise & expired" (an interlined afterthought, editors' n. 2). Brahan to Stoddard: "he died in about three hours". Clarion: "expired about 7 o'clock in the morning of the 11th". Missouri Gazette: "he lived about two hours". Madison: "lived till the next morning, with the utmost impatience for death". Wilson: left "for two hours" until daybreak, then "expired in about two hours, or just as the sun rose above the trees" — i.e. roughly four hours after the shots. Russell 1811: found "about day light" still cutting himself.
5. **When the servants came.** Neelly: "the Servants being awakined by her, came in but too late to save him" and Brahan: "She went & waked the servants" — both imply she roused them at once. Clarion: "she called to the servants without effect—and at the appearance of day light the servants came". Wilson: "As soon as day broke and not before … she sent two of her children to the barn … to bring the servants". Russell 1811: "found about day light, by one of the servants".
6. **Where the servants slept.** Neelly/Brahan: "Stable loft some distance from the other houses". Clarion: "a stable about three hundred yards distant". Wilson: "the barn, which stands about two hundred yards off". Russell 1811: Lewis "ordered the servants to go to the stables and take care of the Horses, least they might loose some that night".
7. **Where Mrs. Grinder slept.** Neelly: "slept herself in one [house] near it" (Dupl.: "house"). Brahan: "another house near it". Clarion: "retired to the kitchen with her children". Wilson: "the kitchen … only a few paces from the room". Jefferson: "an outhouse". Russell 1811: "the house where Mrs. Grinder and her children were lying".
8. **Lewis's words.** Neelly: "I have done the business my good Servant give me Some water". Clarion: "said he had now done for himself; they asked what, and he said he had shot himself and would die". Missouri Gazette: "calling to his servant to give him a bason of water". Wilson: "O Lord!", "O madam! give me some water, and heal my wounds", "I am no coward; but I am so strong, so hard to die", and the request that they "take his rifle and blow out his brains". Russell 1811: "the declaration to the Boy that he had killed himself to deprive his enemies of the pleasure and honor of doing it".
9. **Arrival.** Neelly, Brahan and Wilson: "about sun set"; Wilson adds "alone", the servants "behind". Clarion: no time. Russellville: "After a stay of a few hours at the above house".
10. **Drink that evening.** Clarion: "some spirits, of which he partook, and gave some to his servants"; went to bed "in good order". Wilson: "called for some spirits, and drank a very little". Russell 1811: "a little Meal & drink". Neelly and Brahan say nothing of drink at Grinder's. Russell (31 Jan. 1810) nevertheless attributes the death "Solely to the free use he made of liquor" and says Neelly "advised him to it"; Jefferson (18 Apr. 1810) speaks of "the habit into which he had fallen". Brahan attributes a "propensity" to drink to Pernier, not to Lewis.
11. **Recovery at Fort Pickering.** Russell, 4 Jan. 1810: "in about Six days he was perfectly restored in every respect & able to travel", then "waited six or eight days" for Russell. Russell 1811: "on the sixth or seventh day all symptoms of derangement disappeared … thus continued for ten or twelve days", but "in three or four days [after 29 Sept.] he was again affected with the same mental disease". Neelly: "recovered his health in Some digree". Clark (reporting Russell's lost letters, 26 Nov. 1809): "detaind by him 15 Days in a State of Derangement most of the time". Stoddard's informant (before 28 Sept.): "no alteration for the better had taken place". Jefferson 1813: Neelly found him "extremely indisposed, and betraying, at times, some symptoms of a derangement of mind".
12. **Earlier suicide attempts.** House (frag.): "several attempts"; Russell 1811: "two attempts to kill himself, in one of which he had nearly succeded" (learned "from the Crew"); Clark: "had attempted to kill himself before he got there"; National Intelligencer 27 Nov.: "made some attempts on his life; but the people in the boat secured him". Neelly, Brahan, Russell's own letters of Jan. 1810, and the Missouri Gazette say nothing of them; the Missouri Gazette of 4 Oct. had him leaving New Madrid "in good health".
13. **Onset and nature of the "derangement".** Clarion: "a deranging malady for about six weeks" (i.e. from about the start of September, before he left St. Louis on 4 Sept.); Neelly dates it to the Chickasaw nation (after 29 Sept.); Brahan "some days thereafter"; Russell 1811 "three or four days" out; Madison "latterly repeated symtoms of a disordered mind"; the Missouri Gazette blames "fever, which never failed of depriving him of his reason"; Russell (Jan. 1810) blames liquor; Jefferson (1810, 1813) hypochondria "increased by the habit"; Bates "became insane".
14. **Will and executors.** Coles (5 Jan. 1810): a will found in the memorandum book "is not his last, & … there is another in the hands of a Gentleman soon expected to arrive". Clark (26 Nov. 1809): "Capt. Russell sais he made his will at the Bluffs and left Wm. Merriwether & myself Execiters". Jonathan Clark (3 Feb. 1810): "I have heard that the Governor made a will and devised the papers to William". Jefferson (26 Nov. 1809) names "his administrator, who is John Marks, his half brother", but on 11 Dec. says Clark "is named one of his executors by the Governor"; on 18 Apr. 1810 he calls W. D. Meriwether "one of his executors". The New Madrid will of 11 Sept. 1809 itself was not obtained (section 4).
15. **The effects.** Neelly: "Rifle, Silver watch, Brace of Pistols, dirk & tomahawk". Brahan: "Silver watch—his Brace of pistols, his Rifle & Dirk" (no tomahawk). Russell (4 Jan.): "a Rifle gun pistols Pipe Tommy hawk & dirk … two horses two saddles & bridles … about two hundred & twenty dollars, of which $99 ⁵⁸⁄₁₀₀ was a Treasurers Check". Coles: "His Watch" reached Washington. Marks (Jan. 1812, per Gale): Neelly still had "two pistols, dirk" and "Lewis's gold watch" — gold, where Neelly and Brahan wrote silver; Mrs. Neelly gave up "horse and rifle". Wilson: "all the money he had in his trunk". Russell (31 Jan.): "more than One hun d $ in Notes & Specia besides a Check … none of which it is said could be found". Dickson: the miniature "overlooked" at the Nashville inventory.
16. **Money given to Pernier and owed to him.** Neelly $15 (Neelly, Brahan); Brahan $5 more; Jefferson $10 (26 Nov.). Pernier's claim: "240.D. for his wages" (Jefferson, 26 Nov. 1809) versus his written account of 10 Feb. 1810: $290 wages + $85 clothes − $60 − $43.50 = "$271.50" due. Neelly "says he lent the Gov r money" (Russell) — the only claim that Lewis borrowed from Neelly.
17. **Pernier's character and role.** Brahan: a "propensity" to drink, "perhaps … from distress of mind at the death of the Governor". Russell (31 Jan. 1810): "Aiding & abeting in the murder" (withdrawn in 1811). Princeton editors: "one contemporary blaming the suicide on Pernier's negligence" (the editors do not name him; Madison's "neglect of his Companion, who had not secured his arms" more naturally means Neelly). Wilson: one of the two servants "was a negro"; the editors call Pernier "a free mulatto". M. L. Clark (1848): "murdered by his servant, a Frenchman, who stole his money and horses, returned to Natchez, and was never afterwards heard of" — but Pernier went to Monticello and Washington and died there on 29 Apr. 1810 (Süverman).
18. **Neelly's absence and responsibility.** Neelly, Brahan and Jefferson: he stayed behind for the strayed horses at Lewis's own proposal ("the Governor proposed", Brahan) or by necessity ("obliging mr Neely to halt", Jefferson, who adds that Neelly "kindly determined to accompany & watch over him"). Madison: "the neglect of his Companion, who had not secured his arms". Russell: Neelly "advised him" to drink and "gave the man every chance to seek an opportunity to destroy himself"; had he not offered, "he would this day be living". Russell 1811 omits these charges. Gale (WPO 42:4) reads the Van Meter advertisement (dated 3 Oct.) as proof Neelly had other business; Danisi (WPO 40:4) defends him. Nothing primary was found on Neelly's side of the pistol dispute beyond Russell's "pretended Claim".
19. **Date of burial.** Only the Russellville letter gives one: "buried on the 12th inst." Neelly: "I came up Some time after, & had him … Buried"; Brahan to Stoddard: Neelly "came up" "in a few hours".
20. **Distance from Nashville.** Brahan: "about seventy five miles"; Wilson: "seventy-two miles"; Russell 1811: "only Seventy miles"; Russellville: "about 40 miles beyond Nashville"; Missouri Gazette: "about 40 miles east of the river Tennessee" (the last two are measuring different things and are both rough).
21. **The Intelligencer's retraction.** On 27 Nov. 1809 the Intelligencer said of the "circumstantial statement" it had "republished from a Western print" (its 15 Nov. column = Russellville letter + Clarion) that later information "induces us to think that statement in several respects incorrect", and substituted Brahan's letter — i.e. the razor/throat details were dropped in Washington within six weeks.
22. **Date of Wilson's letter.** The Port Folio (Jan. 1812) dates it "Natchez … May 28th, 1811"; Ord (1828) and Grosart (1876) "May 18th, 1810". Wilson says he "left Nashville" on "Friday morning, May 4th": 4 May was a Friday in 1810, a Saturday in 1811; the Port Folio's year is wrong.
23. **State of the journals.** Clarion: "three very large volumes, with an immense number of paintings—and all was ready for the press"; Monthly Anthology (Apr. 1810): "left in a very confused imperfect state"; Princeton editors: Lewis "had in fact done no significant work" on them.
24. **Neelly's dates.** Princeton: agent "from 8 July 1809 until 4 June 1812"; Danisi: commission handed over in August 1809, first report 27 Aug. 1809, term expired 4 June 1812, successor's letter received 3 July 1812. The 8 July date is the appointment, not the start of duty; the brief's "dismissal 4 June 1812" is, on Danisi's reading of the War Department files, the expiry of a three-year term, though a complaint by James Brown (11 June 1812) was pending.
