# The modern scholarly debate over the authorship of "A Visit from St. Nicholas" (Clement Clarke Moore vs. Henry Livingston Jr.)

Compiled from full texts and excerpts saved under `docs/sources/` (each file carries its source URL on line 1). This document is meant to let a new stylometric/documentary study engage the existing literature precisely: who claimed what, on what evidence, who answered, and what remains open.

- **[SECONDARY]** I did not see the work; what is reported comes from other scholars' quotations or summaries (named in each case). Treat page numbers and wording as second-hand.

## 0. The state of the question in one page

1. The poem was printed anonymously in the *Troy Sentinel* on 23 December 1823 with an editorial headnote by Orville L. Holley ("We know not to whom we are indebted..."). Holley hinted at Moore in the *Sentinel* of 20 January 1829 ("a gentleman of *more* merit as a scholar and a writer than many of more noisy pretensions") and stated in the *Ontario Repository and Freeman* of 28 December 1836 that "not many months afterwards we learnt that they came from the pen of a most accomplished scholar and estimable man, a professor in one of our colleges." Moore's name was attached in print from 31 December 1836 (*New York American*) and January 1837 (*American Monthly Magazine*; Hoffman's *New-York Book of Poetry*, to which Moore supplied four poems). Moore publicly claimed the poem in a letter dated 27 February 1844 (*New York American*, 1 March 1844) and printed it in his *Poems* (1844); he wrote out at least four autograph copies (1853, 1856, 1860, 1862), the last accompanied by a cover letter from T. W. C. Moore (15 March 1862) giving the composition story. [FULL transcriptions of all of these in Norsworthy's posts; see A1.]

2. Livingston (1748-1828) never claimed the poem. His descendants' belief that he read it to his children "about 1804 or 1805" (Henry Livingston of Babylon, 1900), "1808" (Jeanne Denig reporting Eliza Brewer Livingston, 1917-18) or "between 1780 and 1800" (Cornelia Goodrich, 1900) is documented in letters beginning 4 March 1879 (Eliza Livingston Thompson Lansing) and was first argued publicly in 1899-1900 (Long Island newspaper letters), 1919 (W. S. Thomas), 1920 (Tryon; West). The manuscript "with many corrections" is said to have burned in Wisconsin in 1847-48 (Babylon Henry) or 1859 (Van Deusen timeline). [FULL: Thomas 1919; Tryon 1920; West 1920; Stevenson 1923; MacCracken 1958; witness-letter transcriptions on henrylivingston.com.]

3. Don Foster's *Author Unknown* (2000) revived the claim with character arguments against Moore and stylistic arguments for Livingston; it was answered by Nissenbaum (2001), Lowe (2000), Nickell (2002-03) and Kaller (2004). Foster's most concrete charge (the Merino-sheep "plagiarism" inscription) was shown not to be in Moore's hand. [Nissenbaum FULL; Kaller FULL; NYT report FULL; Foster SECONDARY.]

4. MacDonald P. Jackson's *Who Wrote "The Night Before Christmas"?* (McFarland, 2016) applied computational stylistics (phoneme pairs, high- and medium-high-frequency words, articles, attributive adjectives, rhymes, trigrams, favourite expressions) and concluded that every test places the poem with Livingston's verse, while conceding that the external evidence, "taken at face value, favours Moore". A follow-up article (*Style* 51.4, 2017) tested Livingston against a corpus of his periodical contemporaries and again associated the poem with Livingston. [Book SECONDARY/EXCERPT; Jackson's 2017 replies FULL; *Style* abstract only.]

5. Since 2016 the documentary case for Moore has been strengthened (Norsworthy's discovery of the 1844 *New York American* letter, the ~25 pre-1844 printed attributions, Holley's 1836 statement, Moore's second anonymous *Troy Sentinel* poem of 20 February 1824, and the identification of T. W. C. Moore as Livingston's first cousin once removed), and two independent computational tests have pointed the other way from Jackson: Tom Jerman's JGAAP runs (16 of 17 tests for Moore, 2023) and Shlomo Argamon's random-feature-subset similarity tests for Bloomberg (December 2023: "Moore is much more likely to be the author than Livingston"). No peer-reviewed replication of Jackson's tests by a third party was found; no Livingston-side rebuttal of Argamon or Jerman was found; Jackson himself wrote in 2017 that he "still cannot reconcile the external evidence for Moore's authorship ... with the internal evidence for Livingston's" and that "more research is needed."

### A2. The Livingston claim in print, 1919-1958

- **William S. Thomas, "Henry Livingston," *Year Book of the Dutchess County Historical Society* (1919), pp. 32-46.** [FULL, via henrylivingston.com transcription.] Biography of Livingston (Revolutionary major, surveyor, contributor to the *New York Magazine* and *Poughkeepsie Journal*, keeper of a manuscript verse book of 44 pieces, 1784-89) by his great-grandson. On the poem it says only that the family are "unanimous in their belief in a family tradition taught them from infancy", that "the loss of the manuscript by fire" and the first reading "years before" 1823 are "matters of common talk among his posterity", and that "a critical comparison of the 'Visit' with the acknowledged verses ... adds internal evidence" (noted specifically: the anapestic verse letters, the adverbial "all", the word "gave"). Ends: "in the hope that its publication may elicit new evidence."
- **Winthrop P. Tryon, *Christian Science Monitor*, 4 Aug 1920** (title given by Norsworthy as "'Twas the Night Before Christmas", by Wikipedia as "79 Mile to N. York"). [FULL, via henrylivingston.com.] First extended public argument, written from Thomas's papers. Reports the 1879 Lansing letter ("as sure ... as that Burns was the author of 'Tam O'Shanter'"), the 1900 Babylon letter about the corrected manuscript, Lossing's 1886 inquiry "without success", the manuscript book (one-third anapestic), the "R." signature, the 1829 pun and the 1838 *Troy Budget* naming of Moore; concedes "Moore's long possession put the burden of proof on the Livingston claimants" (quoting an unnamed professor) and that Livingston "regularly omitted" to seek credit for his writings.
- **Henry Litchfield West, "Who Wrote 'Twas the Night Before Christmas'?", *The Bookman* 52.4 (Dec 1920): 300-305.** [FULL.] Admits "the evidence is purely circumstantial. There is not extant a single written document which shows that Henry Livingston himself ever laid claim to authorship." Prints the Montgomery "governess" letter for the first time, the 1879 letter, the 1900 Babylon letter (reading c. 1804-05; manuscript burned at Waukesha c. 1847-48), notes the contradiction (Moore unmarried in 1804-05), Gertrude Thomas's reconciliation (the governess was connected with Livingston's family), Denig's 1808 dating, the 1865-70 Sing Sing schoolroom argument between Thomas's father and a Moore grandson, and Lossing's 1886 "gallows" letter; hopes "the question of authorship will be definitely settled."
- **Burton Egbert Stevenson, *Famous Single Poems and the Controversies Which Have Raged Around Them* (1923), chapter "A Visit from St. Nicholas" (pp. 47-80 in the 1924 Harrap edition read).** [FULL.] The first balanced treatment. Presents the Moore documentary chain (Holley 1823/1829, Hoffman 1837, *Poems* 1844, the 1862 N-YHS manuscript and T. W. C. Moore letter, Pelletreau's 1897 facsimile), then the Livingston case as supplied by Thomas (family belief "for more than a hundred years", the Lansing 1879, Babylon 1900 and Denig letters, the Montgomery governess story, the 1784-89 manuscript book, the Beekman verse letter, the "Mama"/'kerchief/Dutch-milieu argument). Verdict: on internal evidence alone the poem "might fairly be held to resemble the work of Henry Livingston much more closely", but "there is no single written document extant to show that Henry Livingston ever claimed the authorship", it is "strange" he never sent it to a paper between 1805 and 1823, "The Pig and the Rooster" proves Moore "could ... write very passable anapaests", and Moore's explicit 1844 claim at 63 makes theft "of the basest and meanest sort" incompatible with his "unblemished reputation". Proposes that Livingston "really did write a Christmas jingle ... in 1805 -- and so did Clement Clarke Moore seventeen years later", the two being confused in family memory.
- **Samuel White Patterson, *The Poet of Christmas Eve: A Life of Clement Clarke Moore, 1779-1863* (Morehouse-Gorham, 1956), pp. 17-19, 112-13, 118, Appendix C pp. 167-71.** [EXCERPT.] Moore's biographer: "The controversy ... should have been settled when this volume [Poems 1844] was published... The effort was commendable but futile ... Better proof will be needed." Appendix C summarises Thomas's thirty-year quest, the Jennie Hubbard statement ("Father ... read it at home before he sent it to be published in a Poughkeepsie paper. Father had the paper carefully preserved, but ... it is now lost"), West and Tryon, and notes "there is not a word from his own pen upon the authorship" and "No one, outside his family circle, appears to have heard of his having written the poem." Patterson also noted that Moore "contributed several of his pieces to *The New-York Book of Poetry* ... It was in this volume ... that he first acknowledged his authorship" (pp. 112-13, quoted by Norsworthy). Patterson is also the source (per Fox 2021, SECONDARY) of the later-debunked claim that Moore was an enslaver opposed to emancipation.
- **Henry Noble MacCracken, *Blithe Dutchess: The Flowering of an American County from 1812* (Hastings House, 1958), pp. 370-390.** [FULL.] The former Vassar president's advocacy for Livingston, written with the Thomas papers (then held by W. Stephen Thomas, Rochester). Ridicules Moore's *Poems* ("the protracted yawn"), lists the witnesses (Jane Paterson Thomas; Sidney M. Livingston "about ten years old" at the first reading; the 1879 Lansing letter, quoted in full; Goodrich 3 Jan 1900, including the claim of a Poughkeepsie printing "between 1780 and 1800"; Babylon 10 Jan 1900; Denig 23 Dec 1918 on Eliza Brewer at Christmas 1808), and constructs the transmission route "with some degree of probability": Locust Grove -> the children of Judith Livingston Moore (Livingston's first cousin and neighbour, married to John Moore) -> an album left at C. C. Moore's house -> a Miss Butler (Lydia or Harriet) who copied it to Troy in 1823 -> Holley's guess. Dates Lossing's inquiry to "seventy-five years ago". Jackson calls the route "pure speculation" (p. 119) while paraphrasing it; Norsworthy (2018) points out that one of Judith Livingston Moore's children, T. W. C. Moore, is precisely the witness who in 1862 attested Moore's authorship.
- **Niels H. Sonne, "'The Night Before Christmas': Who Wrote It?", *Historical Magazine of the Protestant Episcopal Church* 41.4 (Dec 1972): 373-80 (JSTOR 42973358).** [SECONDARY: cited by Kaller (eulogists' "quiet, sparkling humor", p. 379), Norsworthy ("rock solid defense of Moore's authorship on the biographical evidence"), Jackson's bibliography (with the year misprinted 1971).]
- **Tristram Potter Coffin, *The Book of Christmas Folklore* (1973), pp. 87ff.** [SECONDARY; listed on henrylivingston.com's historical-articles page.]
- Also on the Livingston side's own chronology (henrylivingston.com "Historical Authorship Articles", FULL): Cooper 1899 and 1920 (*South Side Signal*), Thomas 1919, Tryon 1920, West 1920, Benchley and Starrett 1920 (dismissive; referred to by MacCracken as "the whim of a few old ladies"), Helen Wilkinson Reynolds 1942, a 1949 cartoon, Helen Myers 1963, Fleetwood and Dr. Thomas 1977, and MacCracken's 1970 Vassar involvement.

### A3. The modern debate, 1996-2016

- **Stephen Nissenbaum, *The Battle for Christmas* (Knopf, 1996).** [SECONDARY.] Historical reading of the poem as a knickerbocker invention (Pintard, Irving, the St. Nicholas Society) that domesticated Christmas; the NYSL exhibit (2015) notes it "omits mention of Livingston entirely"; p. 345 n. 85 cites the Lee diary (1834). Norsworthy calls its reading "the most thorough and satisfying academic reading" of the poem.
- **Don Foster, *Author Unknown: On the Trail of Anonymous* (Henry Holt, 2000), ch. 6 "Yes, Virginia, There Was a Santa Claus", pp. 221-75.** [SECONDARY; reconstructed from Kirkpatrick's NYT report (FULL), Nissenbaum 2001 (FULL), Kaller (FULL, with page references), RPO's 2005 notes, Norsworthy and Jackson.] Page references reported by others: text of the 1823 poem pp. 222-24; "a free spirit and all-round merry old soul" (Livingston) p. 227; family testimony "settled on Christmas morning 1808" p. 237; composition "Christmas 1807 or 1808" p. 238; Moore's 1813 letter (misquoted, "pretended to suppose" elided) p. 247; "snug"/Ramsay/O'Keeffe pp. 259-60; "The coast was clear" p. 271; Merino sheep pp. 273-74; "The words on the handwritten or printed page are more indelible than fingerprints" p. 282. Arguments (all as reported): Moore a grouchy pedant obsessed with noise and "dread"; condemned "immodest verse" (1806 Juvenal preface) and tobacco ("opium's treacherous aid"); "did not know the original names of his own Dutch reindeer"; Merino inscription = prior plagiarism; Hoffman had the wrong poem (Moore's was "Old Santeclaus", 1821, with "dread", "various", passive "seen"); the 1844 Tuttle letter as clearance; the poem belongs to the bawdy anapestic tradition of William King and Christopher Anstey which Livingston imitated and Moore did not (Moore's only anapest, "The Pig and the Rooster", "modeled on the anapestic animal fables of Henry Livingston"); adverbial "all"; "all snug" from ribald anapests/O'Keeffe; "Happy Christmas" (Livingston's 30 Dec 1773 letter); flying images, Lapland reindeer, Thor; "extraneous exclamation points" in the roll call; Moore's "sticky-fingered Muse" borrowing phrases; Moore's poem appeared in the *Poughkeepsie Journal* on 16 Jan 1828 (Foster's find, per Norsworthy). Foster retracted his Shakespeare "Funeral Elegy" attribution in 2002 and settled an anthrax-letters libel suit in 2007 (Fox 2023); Fox calls the chapter "almost entirely fictional" and reports that Juola cites Foster's methods as "how not to do authorship attribution".
- **David D. Kirkpatrick, "Whose Jolly Old Elf Is That, Anyway? Literary Sleuth Casts Doubt on the Authorship of an Iconic Christmas Poem", *New York Times*, 26 Oct 2000.** [FULL, two web pages.] The contemporary summary of Foster's case (quoted above) with Foster's "would a man of God ... tell a lie? ... he did", Kaller's "like someone coming forward and saying that he wrote 'The Star-Spangled Banner'", and Clement Clarke Moore II's "It is the poem itself that is important".
- **James Lowe, "A Christmas to Remember: A Visit from St. Nicholas", *Autograph Collector* (Jan 2000): 26-29.** [SECONDARY: cited by Kaller and Wikipedia for the finding that the Merino inscription is not in Moore's hand.]
- **Stephen Nissenbaum, "There Arose Such a Clatter: Who Really Wrote 'The Night before Christmas'? (And Why Does It Matter?)", *Common-place* 1.2 (Jan 2001).** [FULL.] Answers Foster point by point: Livingston "never took credit for the poem himself, and there is ... no actual historical evidence"; the Livingston claim "was first made in the late 1840s at the earliest (and possibly as late as the 1860s), by one of his daughters, who believed that her father had written the poem back in 1808"; textual analysis "doesn't prove anything", especially since Foster himself calls Moore a "literary sponge"; Foster's own markers ("noise", "dread") occur in the poem; "A Trip to Saratoga" is satire in a known genre and "shows Moore at his most child-centered"; Moore was a high-Federalist patrician, not a fundamentalist; "The Pig and the Rooster" was written from a son's school assignment (Foster's Livingston-modelling claim "curiously self-defeating"); the Merino notation is read differently; and the poem is a knickerbocker product (Pintard 1810, Irving 1809/1812/1821, the St. Nicholas Society) whose "Dutch" persona Livingston too may have adopted self-consciously. Conclusion: Moore "had the means, the opportunity, and even the motive".
- **Jill M. Farringdon, letter to Mary Van Deusen, 17 Jan 2001.** [FULL, on henrylivingston.com.] Applied "cusum analysis" to the texts printed in the NYT and Foster: the poem "was clearly distinguishable from the utterance of Moore" and "INDISTINGUISHABLE from the utterance of Major Livingston". (Cusum/QSUM as a technique has been widely rejected in forensic linguistics; that is general knowledge, not something verified from a source read for this study.)
- **Mary S. Van Deusen, *Dunder and Blixem! It's Henry Livingston* (2001)** [SECONDARY; NYSL call no. 808.0072 qV243] and her website **henrylivingston.com** [FULL for the pages saved: timeline, witnesses, quest, scholars, arguments, data, historical articles]. The site is the source for the Livingston canon used by Jackson, for the transcriptions of Moore's manuscript poems (MCNY), and for the witness letters. Its timeline claims: reading 1807-09; a governess carried the poem to a Moore family "down south" via Chelsea before 1822; Moore read it "as his own" at Christmas 1822 and told his children "not to let it out of the house"; Eliza Brewer Livingston was shown Moore's attribution in 1859; Sidney's family learned of it in 1862; the manuscript burned in Wisconsin in 1859 (the Babylon letter says 1847-48); the family stayed silent because Moore was "high in the seminary of the Episcopalian Church".
- **Nancy H. Marshall, *The Night Before Christmas: A Descriptive Bibliography of Clement Clarke Moore's Immortal Poem* (Oak Knoll, 2002).** [SECONDARY; used by Kaller's Appendix D and by Jackson (item 20 = Keese's *Poets of America*, 1840).]
- **Joe Nickell, "The Case of the Christmas Poem", *Manuscripts* 54.4 (Fall 2002): 293-308, and "Part 2", *Manuscripts* 55.1 (Winter 2003): 5-15.** [SECONDARY; known through Kaller (quotation from 55.1 p. 10: "It is easy to fall into the trap of starting with the desired answer and working backward to the evidence"), Kaller's Appendix E (Nickell's unpublished "Comparison of Phraseology", FULL), Norsworthy (Nickell's ballad-vs-verse-epistle argument at Part 2 pp. 9-10; the simile point, 2003), Jackson (who "argues with Joe Nickell" throughout), Kingery's 2013 NIU dissertation, and Nickell's CFI blog post of 19 Dec 2019 (SECONDARY, from earlier notes: four manuscripts plus Mary Moore's 1855 copy; "From Saint Nicholas"; Holley 1829; Tuttle/Sackett; Occam's razor; Jackson's phonemes ignore cadence and rhyme; Jackson concedes the documentary evidence favours Moore).] Appendix E pairs phrases of the *Visit* with Moore's other verse: adverbial "all" (six Moore examples), "snug and tidy", "visions", "kerchief, cap", "brain(s)" (five examples), "clatter/spatter", "flash", "objects shone", "silent snow", "with wonder"/"wond'ring", "coursers", "dash", "prancing steeds", "twinkle of the eye", "rosy cheeks, the dimpled smile", "droll", "wink", "secret dread", "ere long", plus the *Children's Friend* reindeer and Irving's "laying his finger beside his nose".
- **Seth Kaller, "The Authorship of The Night Before Christmas" (sethkaller.com, chapters I-V and appendices A-E; adapted from "The Moore Things Change...", *New-York Journal of American History*, 2004 -- NYSL catalogues the issue as fall 2004).** [FULL.] Four "fallacies" of Foster: (1) Moore a plagiarist -- the Merino inscription is not Moore's hand (App. A; Lowe; Nickell); (2) a Grinch -- Moore's 1813 letter is misquoted, Strong's diary misread (William, not Clement, Moore), an 1849 verse says "I long to hear you romp and riot", Sonne's eulogists; (3) a Scrooge -- the tobacco point rests on "The Wine Drinker", which satirises temperance hypocrites; (4) stylistic evidence -- mixed iambs/anapests, exclamation marks, adverbial "all", "all snug" all occur in Moore; OED gives Steele 1714 and Anstey 1766 for "snug", three pre-1789 "all snug"s; Scott's *Guy Mannering* (1815: "snug ... donner and blitzen") and Irving's "snug elbow chair"; Irving's 1821 addition "laying his finger beside his nose" dates the poem to 1822, "not earlier"; "Donder" and "Blitzen" are "authentic German"; Moore corrected "Blixem" to "Blitzen" on the Tuttle broadside. On the Livingston claim: based on Eliza Brewer Livingston's "recovered memory" "In 1848, 1861, or even later (no one in the family knows)", most likely prompted by the 1863 *Harper's Weekly* Nast printing; "The written Livingston family claims begin around 1870"; earlier family datings (1780-1800, 1805, 1807) contradict the 1808 story; Catherine Livingston Breese died 21 Aug 1808; Moore was a bachelor until 1813 and needed no governess until 1815; Livingston published New Year's addresses yet never claimed the poem through its many reprints 1823-28; "over 40 years of searches" found no printing with Livingston's name. Appendix C chronology and Appendix D early-publication timeline (1823-1863). Kaller's pages do not discuss Jackson; his reaction to Jackson is in CBC 2017 (below).
- **Ted Mann, "Ho, Ho, Hoax", *Scarsdale Magazine*, 30 Nov 2006.** [SECONDARY; Wikipedia citation for Foster's challenge.]
- **Emily A. Kingery, doctoral dissertation (Northern Illinois University, 2013)** citing Nickell. [SECONDARY; Norsworthy post of 31 Mar 2017.]
- **Mock trials, Troy NY**: "The Trial Before Christmas" (Duncan Crary and Jack Casey), 2013 (hung jury) and 2014 (verdict for Livingston); on hiatus 2015 for a stage play (NYSL 2015; CBC 2017). Per earlier notes the Troy mayor proclaimed a "Henry Livingston Jr. Day" (23 Dec) [SECONDARY]. A Hallmark film based on the trials aired in 2022 (Fox 2023).
- **MacDonald P. Jackson, statement of 17 Dec 2013 (henrylivingston.com "Scholars").** [FULL.] Explains his entry into the question after reading Foster ("Moore's champions ... seemed to me not to have satisfactorily answered Don's most telling points"), his impression of Livingston's "warmth, empathy, bonhomie, eye for detail" versus Moore the "satirist and moralizer", and announces: "Every test, so far applied, associates 'The Night Before Christmas' much more closely with Livingston's verse than with Moore's... It will surprise me if all reliable kinds of testing do not reach the same conclusions." Data were supplied from December 2011 by Van Deusen, Paul Kosinski (programs) and Lyn Bates (who suggested the CMU pronouncing dictionary for phonemes).

## C. Jackson (2016; 2017): data, methods, results, and the methodological criticisms

Sources for this section: Jackson's own summary on the henrylivingston.com data page [FULL], his replies of 3 Feb and 13/16 Mar 2017 [FULL], his 2013 statement [FULL], the *Style* abstract [EXCERPT], the book's table of contents [FULL], and page-referenced quotations in Norsworthy's posts [FULL]. Numbers not attributed to one of these are not available to me.

### C.1 Corpora

- **Livingston corpus**: "the Livingston corpus that had been established on Mary Van Deusen's 'Henry Livingston' website several years before I began my investigations", excluding the site's "Henry Plus" (possibly-Livingston) group. Listed in *Who Wrote*, pp. 164-72. Sixty-five poems are attributed to Livingston on the site (N); Jackson's Table 13.1 (pp. 65-67) lists 54 poems of >=100 words after excluding eleven shorter ones, totalling 12,303 words; the whole Livingston set is 12,599 words (N's figure from Jackson's data), 1,892 lines (data page). Only ten of the 54 exceed 300 words and six exceed 400 (the 1803 and 1819 Carrier's Addresses, "Crane & Fox" 460, "Vine & Oak" 428, "Letter to my brother Beekman" 408, "American Eagle" 530). Most survivors date from 1776-90 (the 1784-89 manuscript book) because, Jackson says, Livingston's autographs of 1791-1828 "perished in a fire (except those copied into his daughter Jane's poetry book)". The set includes the 1803 and 1819 Carrier's Addresses (Norsworthy: the 1803 address is probably by Isaac Mitchell, editor of the *Political Barometer*; only the 1787 address is in Livingston's manuscript book; attributions by Gertrude Thomas are "unsubstantiated") and verse paraphrases of Job, Habakkuk, Isaiah, Hezekiah and Aesop.
- **Moore corpus for developing the tests**: *Poems* (1844) -- "the texts he approved for final publication" -- plus the manuscript pieces "From Saint Nicholas", "To Fanny" and "To Clem" (Mar 2017 reply), minus three verse translations (two from Italian, one from Greek: the Petrarch sonnet, the Aeschylus *Prometheus* chorus, and per N the "Ode to Nice"); "The Mischievous Muse -- Translated" (from Da Ponte) was left in by "oversight". Norsworthy's arithmetic from Jackson's tables: 32 published poems totalling 20,556 words (2,873 lines per the data page).
- **Moore manuscript poems held back "for checking the tests"** (ch. 18): twelve manuscript poems of 1843-52 from Moore's MCNY notebook, "including the long 'Charles Elphinstone'" (blank-verse narrative, 13,670 words, datable 1851), "Irish Valentine" (590 words, anapestic), "For a Kiss" (anapestic), "Caroline's Album", "Newport Beach", "To Eliza, in England", "Valentine for Fanny French", "To Little Clem", and others; the Petrarch translation excluded. Held-back total per N: 38,714 words for 47 Moore poems including "Sand" and the MSS. **Omitted**: "Biography of the Heart of Clement C. Moore" (Jackson used only the 72% printed by Patterson, then agreed to add the whole once Norsworthy transcribed it, Jan 2017).
- **Text of the disputed poem**: the *Troy Sentinel* 1823 printing (ch. 1), which Jackson defends as closest to the author's copy; using *Poems* 1844 would change one Moore-favoured "that" and one Livingston-favoured "was" and nothing in the phoneme results (Mar 2017).
- **Transcriptions**: Van Deusen's, with some corrections by Jackson; "The" and "the", "And" and "and" counted as distinct words in the book (merged in the *Style* follow-up). Known transcription errors: Moore's "clad" as "glad", Livingston's "sempstress" as "seamstress" (N); Jackson: immaterial to the tests.
- ***Style* 2017 control corpus**: "a similarly-sized corpus of verse by contributors to the newspapers and journals in which he [Livingston] published" (Mar 2017 reply; abstract).

### C.2 Methods and headline results (in the book's order)

1. **Reindeer names, exclamations (ch. 3)**: documentary/orthographic argument that the Sentinel's "Dunder and Blixem", exclamation placement and nasal half-rhyme are authorial and Livingstonian (Table 3.1 of blitzen/blixem usages; Norsworthy shows the table omits Scott 1815/1820 and many blixen/blitzen examples).
2. **Sources (ch. 4)**: denies that Irving's *History* must be a source; the resemblances "not conclusive".
3. **Meter (ch. 5)**: Livingston wrote anapestic tetrameter far more than Moore (one-third of the manuscript book); Moore's four anapests serve as controls throughout.
4. **Attributive adjectives (ch. 7, Table 7.1, blocks of >=500 words)**: Moore uses far more; the poem has 16.236% attributive adjectives per total words; Moore's anapests 39.4 ("Pig and Rooster"), 36.0 ("From Saint Nicholas"), 27.8 ("For a Kiss"), 28.0 ("Irish Valentine"); at least five Livingston pieces 10.5-19.2 (p. 98). Jackson concedes meter "militates against the liberal use of adjectives" (pp. 97-98).
5. **Rhyme links (ch. 8)**: rhyme pairs of the poem found in each corpus (details not available; "Irish Valentine" snow/below recorded at p. 99).
6. **Shared three-word sequences (ch. 9)**: trigrams exclusive to one candidate; initially "tended to favour Livingston", "though the data were sparse"; after Norsworthy's additions Jackson's recount gives Livingston 3 vs Moore 9 (= the 3:1 corpus ratio) and he concludes the test is "ineffectual".
7. **Phoneme pairs (chs. 10-12)**: every word transcribed into Arpabet phonemes via the CMU pronouncing dictionary (work done by Van Deusen, Kosinski and Bates); a "phoneme pair" = last phoneme of one word + first phoneme of the next within a line. Chi-square tests over all pairs in either poet's top 100 by frequency yielded ten Moore-favoured (T/DH, T/F, T/S, Z/W, S/W, Z/T, IY/T, D/P, S/S, Z/CH) and ten Livingston-favoured pairs (AH/N, AH/F, AH/S, AH/B, AH/K, AH/L, AH/P, N/AH, Z/AO, Z/IH). Statistic: Livingston-favoured / (Livingston- + Moore-favoured) per poem; poems with fewer than 12 favoured pairs dropped (25 Livingston, 6 Moore), leaving 1,483 Livingston lines and 2,750 Moore lines. Means: Livingston 66.654, Moore 42.912, *Visit* 64.912 (Table 12.1; Figure 3, p. 132, shows the poem "around the middle of the Livingston distribution and outside the range for Moore"). Jackson grants that AH-initial pairs are "largely determined by rates of use of the definite and indefinite articles".
8. **Articles (ch. 13)**: "the"+"a" as a percentage of words: poem 11.439; Moore's anapests 3.271-9.322; some non-anapestic Livingston poems 11.6-12.5; Moore's highest rates in late non-anapestic MSS ("Caroline's Album", "Newport Beach").
9. **Very high-frequency words (ch. 14, Table 14.1, 500-word blocks)**: "that" (Moore-favoured; the poem has 17.347 "that" per 1,000 words in *Biography* -- a Moore-typical rate -- for comparison) and other stand-alone words; "to", "I", "is" excluded from stand-alone testing because t-tests showed no significant between-poet difference; Table 14.1 as printed misplaces four Livingston-block rows that belong to Moore (Jackson's correction, Feb 2017).
10. **Favorite expressions (ch. 15, Table 15.1)**: some, oft, many a, in vain, at length, that's -- 160 Moore vs 9 Livingston; "Moore averages one of his favorite locutions to every 129 words"; none in the poem. Criteria unstated (N).
11. **Common words that discriminate (ch. 16, Table 16.2)**: each poet's top-50 words; 26 words present in both and >=2 times in the poem ranked -- Spearman rho poem-Livingston .7638, poem-Moore .6633; then words used >=1.2x more by one poet (excluding those used in earlier tests) -- Livingston-favoured: I, his, my, her, on, as, is, was, at, thy, will, day, When, me, Where, While; Moore-favoured: to, from, your, for, they, be, With, this, our, not, which, so, would, For, it, heart, Of, are, we. Percentage of Livingston-favoured tokens per poem separates the corpora (t-test); the poem lies within Livingston's range and at the extreme of Moore's. Anapestic Livingston pieces average 53.4708 vs 64.8487 for the rest (significant, in the direction that weakens a meter explanation).
12. **Medium-high-frequency words (ch. 17, Table 17.1)**: the next 60 words by rank; 34 Livingston- and 37 Moore-favoured at the 1.2x criterion (only two between 1.2 and 1.3); poems with >=10 test words: Livingston mean 60.814 (range 30.000-89.474), Moore 29.489 (14.583-53.333), poem 53.704. Includes "like" and "should" as Moore-favoured. Anapestic vs other Livingston: 61.7204 vs 63.2948 (n.s.). Note 1 (p. 183): "a fair proportion" of these are content words.
13. **Combined Figure 4 (p. 133)**: all words in either author's top 110 meeting the 1.2x criterion, combining Tables 16.2, 17.1, 18.1, 18.2, 18.5 and per-poem "that" counts; the poem sits "around the middle of the Livingston distribution and outside the range for Moore". Later additions: *Biography of the Heart* scores 42.105 (Moore-typical); "The Writing of Hezekiah" 64.286 (Livingston-typical).
14. **Checking the tests (ch. 18)**: the twelve held-back Moore MSS "associated unequivocally with Moore"; "none was, on the full combination of tests, so consistently Livingston-like as 'The Night Before Christmas'"; the "regression effect" is invoked for the slight loss of discrimination on new data; shared-trigram checking was omitted (admitted, Mar 2017). No Livingston poems were held back, "because what mattered ... was to find out whether [the tests] yielded 'false positives' for Livingston".
15. **External evidence (chs. 19-21)**: the Moore "creation myth", the Livingston family version (pp. 110-12), the Odell copy (pp. 116-18), MacCracken's route (p. 119), the Tuttle broadside as printer's copy (pp. 173-4); Jackson's stated position: external evidence "taken at face value, favours Moore", but "the long-standing belief among descendants of Henry Livingston ... [need not] be groundless".
16. **Summary (ch. 23, p. 128)**: "No experienced reader of poetry who was familiar with the verse of the two rival candidates could fail to recognize that 'The Night Before Christmas' is as uncharacteristic of Moore as it is characteristic of Livingston"; rates of common words and phoneme pairs "are largely beyond a writer's conscious control. They distinguish Moore's verse from Livingston's, and they classify The Night Before Christmas with the latter" (CBC quotation); for Moore to be the author he must have "slipped into a style that was not only atypical of his own verse but utterly typical of the very man who, according to his descendants, was the true author".
17. ***Style* 2017**: the same two discriminators applied Livingston-vs-contemporaries; poem again with Livingston; capitalisation merged.

## D. Open questions the scholars themselves say would settle it

Quoted or closely paraphrased, with source.

1. **A written claim or a manuscript from Livingston's side.** Stevenson (1923): "there is no single written document extant to show that Henry Livingston ever claimed the authorship ... even after its appearance in the Troy Sentinel"; West (1920): "There is not extant a single written document which shows that Henry Livingston himself ever laid claim to authorship"; Patterson (1956): "Better proof will be needed ... not a word from his own pen"; Kaller (2004): "no record ... despite over 40 years of searches -- of any printing of the poem with Livingston's name"; Fox (2023): "unless somebody finds some pretty compelling documentary evidence pointing to Livingston".
2. **The pre-1823 Poughkeepsie newspaper printing that the family remembered** (Goodrich 1900: published "in a Poughkeepsie paper ... between 1780 and 1800"; Jennie Hubbard: "he sent it to be published in a Poughkeepsie paper ... the paper carefully preserved, but ... now lost"). W. S. Thomas "searched through old newspaper collections hoping to find the published version" (Van Deusen); Thomas (1919) published "in the hope that its publication may elicit new evidence"; Tryon (1920) hoped "further information ... may be drawn from hiding"; West (1920) hoped the testimony would let the question be "definitely settled". Kaller and Norsworthy treat the failure to find it after a century as decisive; Norsworthy adds that the 16 Jan 1828 *Poughkeepsie Journal* reprint could itself be the clipping Charles Livingston kept in his desk.
3. **Resolving the family's dates.** Stevenson: the 1804-05/1808 stories cannot both be true and Moore had no children then; Kaller: Catherine Breese died in Aug 1808; Jackson (2017) takes the letters seriously but offers no reconciliation. A dated, contemporaneous Livingston-family document from before 1844 would change the picture; none is known.
4. **Whether the 1823 accidentals ("Dunder and Blixem", the exclamation marks) are authorial.** Jackson's chapter 3 depends on it; Norsworthy's counter depends on two intermediate copies. A copy in Harriet Butler's or the album-keeper's hand, or Sarah Sackett's copy, would settle it; none is known.
5. **Re-running the stylometry with the fixes both sides accept**: Jackson (Mar 2017) agreed to add *Biography of the Heart*, to try excluding the 1803/1819 Carrier's Addresses ("It would be worth seeing how a Mitchell poem fares"), to merge capitalised forms, and named untried techniques (word-adjacency networks; Elliott-Valenza modal distance, "did well on 500-word blocks of verse"). Norsworthy (Feb 2017) asked for: all Moore verse including translations and MSS; a purged Livingston canon; corrected transcriptions; explicit handling of versions; counts of "everything, including all nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs", published so others can evaluate; and (May 2017) genre-matched subsets (Moore's anapests and child-directed poems vs Livingston's). Norsworthy (Dec 2023): "Future investigations using JGAAP can confirm if Jerman's findings are repeatable ... with more realistic corpora, minus unsubstantiated attributions to Livingston"; and "determine if Major Livingston of Poughkeepsie ever wrote more than one New Year's Day 'Carrier's Address'."
6. **Prevalidated, independently run methods.** Jerman (per Norsworthy/Fox): protocols "prevalidated" in Juola's sense, validity testing, independent researchers, an author profile; Jackson (2013): "when they are published I hope other attribution scholars will try their own different approaches ... It will surprise me if all reliable kinds of testing do not reach the same conclusions" -- a prediction now contradicted by Jerman and Argamon and therefore itself a testable open question.
7. **Reconciling internal and external evidence.** Jackson (Feb 2017): "I still cannot reconcile the external evidence for Moore's authorship ... with the internal evidence for Livingston's"; (CBC 2017): "quite likely that the matter can never be settled". Nissenbaum (2001): "By itself ... textual analysis doesn't prove anything", especially for a "literary sponge"; his proposed test is historical fit (Pintard/Irving/the 1820s), which the Livingston date fails.
8. **The chronology of sources.** Kaller/Nickell/Jerman: any Livingston date must explain the poem's dependence on Irving's 1821 "finger beside his nose" and the 1821 *Children's Friend* reindeer; Jackson denies dependence. A demonstration either way (e.g., a pre-1821 text with a reindeer-drawn St. Nicholas, or a rigorous source study) would settle the dating question.
9. **Stevenson's "two poems" hypothesis** (Livingston wrote a Christmas jingle c. 1805 and Moore wrote this one in 1822; the family conflated them) and Norsworthy's variant (a Carrier's Address remembered as the poem). Recovery of any Livingston Christmas or New Year piece read to the children c. 1805-10 would test it.
10. **What Holley knew and when** (MacCracken: "what evidence did the editor of the Troy Sentinel ... go upon?"). Holley's 1836 statement answers the "when" (within months); the "from whom" (Sackett/Butler) rests on Tuttle 1844 and Pelletreau 1897.

**Synthesis.** Nobody has produced a Livingston-family assertion datable before 4 March 1879, and that letter itself says the family's "astonishment" came "many years after my father's decease". The only earlier *dated* events are (a) a schoolroom argument c. 1865-70 remembered by a participant's son, and (b) the family's own later statements that Eliza Brewer Livingston saw Moore's name in 1859 and Sidney's family in 1860/1862. Kaller's "around 1870" for the first written claims is consistent with the surviving letters; Nissenbaum's "late 1840s at the earliest" reflects Kaller's/Foster's "1848" option (the Wisconsin fire date, 1847-48, seems to have been conflated with the discovery date in some family accounts). All of these are decades after Moore's 1837 and 1844 claims and after Livingston's children had had twenty years (1823-44) of anonymous reprints and seven (1837-44) of attributed ones in which to object.
