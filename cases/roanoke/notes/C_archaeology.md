## 0. Summary of findings (read this first)

1. **Roanoke Island (Fort Raleigh NHS).** Seventy-nine years of excavation (Harrington 1947-53, 1965; Ehrenhard/NPS 1980s; Noël Hume/VCF 1991-95; FCF 2006-2021) have produced (a) a small earthwork, (b) a metallurgical/"science centre" workshop of Lane's 1585-86 colony immediately west of it, (c) two cache pits with European copper and beads and a slot trench in Harriot Woods (2008-2010), and (d) scattered Elizabethan artifacts along the eroding north shore. **No house, palisade line, cemetery, or midden attributable to the 1587 "Cittie of Ralegh" has been found.** Harrington's 1962 verdict — "the settlement site has not been located" — still stands; FCF's own overview page says "no evidence for the settlement of the remains of the colonists has yet been found" (VERIFIED [FCF_virtual_earthwork.txt]). FCF now (2022-2024) dates the earthwork firmly to Lane's colony and calls it a "sconce" attached to an outwork/observation post; Noël Hume's suggestion that the earthwork could be later (even 18th-century) is explicitly rejected by FCF on stratigraphic grounds (assay debris runs under the rampart), but the FCF re-analysis exists only as foundation-hosted PDFs (2022-24), not as a peer-reviewed publication.
2. **The White map patch (BM 1906,0509.1.3).** The British Museum's 2012 technical study (Ambers, Russell, Saunders & Sloan, *BM Technical Research Bulletin* 6: 47-54) established that patch 2 conceals a bold blue-and-red fort-like symbol and that a fainter fort-like symbol was drawn on top of the patch. The BM authors state explicitly that interpretation "within a wider Elizabethan context ... is beyond the scope of this study" and that it is "not clear if this shape is intended as a symbolic representation or as an actual depiction of the ground plan of a fort or of an intended, but unbuilt, fort" (VERIFIED). The "concealment" reading (Lane, Horn) and the "correction" reading are both inferences; the map is a plan/intention document made in 1585-86, before the 1587 colony existed. It proves nothing about where anyone went in 1587-90.
3. **Site X and Site Y (Bertie County, Salmon Creek/Chowan River).** FCF's own published counts: Site X, 53 2-m units dug 2012-2017, "40 sherds representing perhaps 6 or 7 vessels of 16th century Surrey-Hampshire Border ware; 8 sherds representing a North Devon plain baluster jar", two tenter hooks, an aiglet, priming pans, a buckle, and (announced 2026) a late-16th-century Augsburg lead cloth seal, all in a plough zone mixed with c. 275 lb of Native pottery; Site Y (31BR49), 72 5-ft test squares dug from December 2019, a larger and more varied English assemblage (Border ware, North Devon baluster jar, London redware, Essex fine redware, Spanish olive jar, Frechen and Martincamp stoneware) but no published sherd count. **No structures, features, burials, or sealed 16th-century deposits at either site.** FCF's dating rests on the Border ware/pipe-stem argument (Border ware "declined precipitously after about 1630"; no large-bore 17th-century pipe stems). Critics (Ewen, Wright, Horton, Noël Hume) say a mixed plough-zone assemblage cannot be dated to within a generation, that a c. 1607-1650 Jamestown-related origin cannot be excluded, and that no domestic features have been found. FCF's rhetoric escalated from "we do not have the smoking gun" (2017/2023) and "Site Y does not solve the Lost Colony mystery" (Dec 2020) to "The Lost Colony is Found! How We Did It." (web statement, 4 June 2026). **No peer-reviewed site report for Site X or Site Y has been published**; the fullest accounts are an edited popular volume (Klingelhofer ed., *Excavating the Lost Colony Mystery*, UNC Press, 7 Nov 2023) and FCF web excerpts. The FCF "Dig Reports" page lists Fort Raleigh reports only — nothing for Bertie County (VERIFIED [FCF_dig_reports_page.txt]).
4. **Hatteras / Cape Creek (31DR1, Buxton).** Phelps (ECU) 1993-98 found a rich Croatoan town site he dated 1650-1720, plus a "gold" signet ring (1998) and a snaphaunce lock; Phelps never published. The ring was shown by pXRF in 2017 to be brass (Ewen & Farrell 2019, *NC Historical Review* 96(4): 408-25) and lay in a 1670-1720 context. Horton (Bristol, later RAU) and CAS dug 2009-2018+ and found further 16th/17th-century European items (rapier hilt, slate tablet, Nuremberg token, gun parts, coin weights of 1644/1648) mostly in 17th-century contexts. In 2024-25 they announced "buckets" of hammerscale in a pit "underneath a thick shell midden that contains virtually no European material" with radiocarbon dates on associated animal teeth "from the 1580s" (announced Jan-Jun 2025 in press; 4 dates reported June 2026). **As of 14 Sept 2026 none of the Horton/CAS work, including the hammerscale, has appeared in a peer-reviewed publication**; Horton said in June 2025 that his team "plan to move forward with a publication", and the March 2026 CSI talk was titled with a question mark ("The Smoking Gun?"). Critics (Ewen, Leeson, Heath, Luccketti/Evans) want a hearth/slag/anvil context, a report, structures, and European burials.
5. **Other claims.** Elizabethan Gardens (2023-24): FCF found the Algonquian village of Roanoac (pottery, a drawn-copper wire ring of European copper) — it is a 1584-86 contact site, abandoned 1586, not a 1587 colonist site. Lumbee/"Croatan" descent (McMillan 1885/1888) has no archaeological support and is rejected by Lumbee scholarship (Lowery) and by Thomas (1976). The Lost Colony DNA Project (Estes, founded 2007) is now "inactive" on FTDNA and has never announced a confirmed colonist match; 2025 "DNA breakthrough" web stories cite no study. At Great Neck (44VB7, Virginia Beach), the best candidate for Chesapeake/Chesepioc, Hodges' 1998 VDHR report states that no European-made artifact earlier than c. 1850 was recovered from any context; Quinn's Chesapeake-massacre thesis therefore has no archaeological corroboration at present.
6. **Bottom line.** No find anywhere is yet diagnostic of English *residence* in 1587-c.1600 as opposed to 1585-86 activity or later trade/curation. The two live candidates (Bertie Sites X/Y; Hatteras 31DR1) each rest on artefact typology/dating arguments made by their own excavators, without peer-reviewed site reports, without structures, without burials, and (for Bertie) without sealed contexts. What would settle it is set out in §6.3.

## 1. Fort Raleigh National Historic Site and the north end of Roanoke Island

### 1.1 Chronology of fieldwork (VERIFIED unless marked)

Kelly conducted a small survey of the future Fort Raleigh National Historic Site property. 1947/48 National Park Service archaeologist J. C. Harrington directed the excavation of a series of test trenches in the earthwork and Fort Raleigh National Historic Site property. 1950/51 J.C. Harrington completed the excavation and reconstruction of the earthwork. 1953 Harrington excavated test trenches in Elizabethan Garden. 1965 Harrington excavated the "outwork" at Fort Raleigh National Historic Site. Harrington conducted salvage excavation of Native American ossuary at Dough's Point. 1980 East Carolina University archaeologist David S, Phelps conducted investigations at 31DR48 'Indian Hole' to relocate mass grave noted by Williams in 1895 and at Fessenden National Memorial. 1980 Kenneth Hartsell under the direction of David Phelps conducted a surface survey of the shoreline for the entire north end of Roanoke Island from Northwest Point to Baum Point. ... Komara of the Southeast Archaeological Center of the National Park Service conducted soil resistivity and magnetometer surveys west of the earthwork ... National Park Service ranger Phil Evans discovers a barrel in the shallow water near the Prince House; the barrel is likely the bottom of an eroded barrel-lined well that may be related to one of Raleigh's settlements. 1983 Ehrenhard and Komara initiated 'ground truthing' excavations of anomalies detected by 1982 remote sensing survey ... Subsequent phases (each documented below): NPS/SEAC remote sensing and testing 1982-85 (Ehrenhard, Athens & Komara; Athens 1984 soil resistivity — VERIFIED as cited in [NPS_HRS_FortRaleigh_HistoricResourceStudy.txt] and in the reference list of [Springer_European_ceramics_elusive_Cittie.txt]); Noël Hume/VCF 1991-93 (science centre) and 1994-95 (Luccketti survey); FCF 2006 (Prince House Woods), 2007-2010 (Watts underwater surveys; Harriot Woods cache pits 2008; slot trench 2009-10; Time Team America 2009), 2016-17 (Deetz), 2021 (watchtower/workshop re-excavation), 2023-24 (Elizabethan Gardens = Roanoac village, see §5.1), and a Fort Raleigh dig "slated ... later in the spring" of 2024 for which I found no published results (see §7).

### 1.2 J. C. Harrington, 1947-53 and 1965; the 1962 report

Harrington's monograph is *Search for the Cittie of Ralegh: Archeological Excavations at Fort Raleigh National Historic Site, North Carolina* (NPS Archeological Research Series No. 6, Washington, 1962 — series number UNVERIFIED; title VERIFIED from the archive.org record [Harrington1962_SearchForCittieOfRalegh_fulltext.txt]). Luccketti's 1996 survey report summarises the scale: Harrington "excavated at least 38 separate five-foot wide trenches" with a "total length of 3,320 linear feet. Harrington found only 18 artifacts that could be attributed to the 16th-century" and "concluded that the habitation, or town, site was elsewhere" (VERIFIED [FCF_report_FortRaleigh_1994_1995_survey.txt], lines 317-321).

Harrington's own conclusions (all VERIFIED [Harrington1962_SearchForCittieOfRalegh_fulltext.txt]):

- On the artefacts and the earthwork's date (pp. 23-24): "It tells us that the fort in question was almost certainly built by Englishmen about the period of the Raleigh colonizing ventures. Actually, the artifacts in themselves point only to late 16th- or 17th-century occupation, but on the historical evidence can be reasonably narrowed to the earlier century. They do not tell us, however, whether this earthwork was built in 1585, or during the second colonizing attempt."
- On the 1590 palisade: "The palisaded enclosure found by White's party in 1590 was almost certainly a distinct structure from the small earthwork, and presumably was built by the second group that came in 1587."
- Conclusions chapter: "The one important conclusion from the explorations carried on thus far is that the habitation site has not been located."
- Final chapter ("The Search Goes On", p. 47): "Two important conclusions have been presented in the preceding pages: (i) The excavated earthwork was built by the Raleigh colonists, and (i) the settlement site has not been located. The basis for the first conclusion is positive archeological findings, much more satisfactory to an archeologist than negative findings on which the second conclusion is based."
- On the absence of a Native village at the fort site he cites Haag: "nothing was found that suggested concentrated village midden ..." (Haag, 1958, p. 63).

Harrington's 1965 "outwork": Luccketti describes it as "what he termed an outwork: a 9-ft. square structure" (VERIFIED [FCF_report_FortRaleigh_1994_1995_survey.txt], line 325), published as J. C. Harrington, *An Outwork at Fort Raleigh: Further Archeological Excavations at Fort Raleigh National Historic Site* (Philadelphia: Eastern National Park and Monument Association, 1966) (VERIFIED as cited in [NPS_HRS_FortRaleigh_HistoricResourceStudy.txt], lines 3196 and 3711). The NPS Historic Resource Study adds: "Years later, Ehrenhard, Athens, and Komara attributed this feature to subsequent colonial fortification activities. See John E. Ehrenhard, William P. Athens, and Gregory L. Komara, 'Remote Sensing Investigations at Fort [Raleigh ...]'" (VERIFIED, lines 3198-3199).

### 1.3 Ivor Noël Hume / Virginia Company Foundation, 1991-95: the "science centre"; Skowronek & Walker 1993

Luccketti's summary of the VCF 1991-93 excavations west of the earthwork: Noël Hume set out to test whether "the outwork was a flanker to a wooden fort, but instead, discovered the partially preserved remains of a scientific workshop floor. More than 100 artifacts were found, consisting principally of crucible sherds, Normandy flask sherds, fragments of chemical glassware, worked and unworked copper, antimony (an essential ingredient in separating silver from copper), and sherds of delftware ointment pots. These all point to the distilling experiments of Thomas Hariot and the metallurgical work of Joachim Gans" (VERIFIED [FCF_report_FortRaleigh_1994_1995_survey.txt], line 394 ff.). This is the Harriot/Gans metallurgical and distilling workshop of 1585-86 — the one securely identified Elizabethan activity area on the island. Contemporary press: "Birthplace of American science found at scene of Lost Colony" (Tampa Bay Times archive, 1 Dec 1991) [TampaBay_1991_science_center.txt].

The NPS Historic Resource Study records Noël Hume's dating challenge to the earthwork: "However, based on Harrington's find of two pieces of metal beneath the remaining earthworks, Noel Hume has argued that the fortification is of a later date than the science center, perhaps even dating to the eighteenth century.289" — footnote 289 cites Noel Hume, "Roanoke Island: America's First Science Center," p. 14 (VERIFIED [NPS_HRS_FortRaleigh_HistoricResourceStudy.txt]). (The article appeared in *Colonial Williamsburg: The Journal of the Colonial Williamsburg Foundation*, Spring 1994 — venue UNVERIFIED; Noël Hume's book *The Virginia Adventure: Roanoke to James Towne* (Knopf, 1994) covers the same ground — UNVERIFIED.)

Luccketti's 1994-95 survey (VCF; report "Fort Raleigh Archaeological Project: 1994/1995 Survey Report", title VERIFIED [FCF_dig_reports_page.txt]) concluded: "No European artifacts or features associated with the 16th-century English settlements at Roanoke Island were found" in the 1994 survey (VERIFIED, line 79), and, of the science centre: "Despite this remarkable discovery, no remains of the people, houses, or forts of either the 1585 Lane Colony or John White's 1587 'Lost Colony' have yet been found." (VERIFIED, line 415 ff.)

Peer-reviewed re-analysis of the older ceramic collections: Russell K. Skowronek and John W. Walker, "European ceramics and the elusive 'Cittie of Raleigh'", *Historical Archaeology* 27(1) (March 1993): 58-69, DOI 10.1007/BF03373559. Abstract (VERIFIED [Springer_European_ceramics_elusive_Cittie.txt]): "Since 1947 archaeologists have sought to identify the location of the 'Lost Colony's' settlement, the 'Cittie of Raleigh.' However, the exact location of this 16th-century habitation site has remained elusive due in part to the greater antiquity and transitory nature of this site's occupation vis-à-vis later English New World settlements. ... This article reviews and reevaluates the ceramic collections made during past excavations at Fort Raleigh National Historic Site, North Carolina, and suggests approaches to future research at the site." (Full text paywalled; not read.)

**Interpretation of the cache pits.** The copper necklace, beads and aiglets are trade goods in Native cache pits; they show English-Native exchange in the 1580s (either 1584-86 or 1587), not English habitation. The slot trench is undated.

### 1.6 The 2022-2024 reinterpretation: is the earthwork Lane's fort, an outwork, or something later?

FCF has posted two working papers and a set of appendices (all VERIFIED as read):

- Eric Klingelhofer, with Eric Deetz and Carter L. Hudgins, *Reinterpreting Fort Raleigh Outwork-Science Center. Part 1: Structural Reinterpretation* (FCF PDF; "MARCH 2022, rev. APRIL 2024"; byline dated March 2024) [FCF_Reinterpreting_FortRaleigh_Part1.txt]. Key sentences: "assay material from the earliest phase of activity on the site extended eastward beneath the earthwork's ramparts, which resolves the question raised by Ivor Noël Hume about the Fort Raleigh earthwork being an Elizabethan construction" (line 63); the structure was "not ... a corner tower of a larger fort. And INH was correct in identifying the assaying taking place, but not in questioning the earthwork's Elizabethan construction" (line 246); the "outwork" is reinterpreted as "an observation post serving Governor Lane's yet-to be located [fort]" (line 244). The paper groups the features "into three phases. For convenience, they are given the names of the three successive colonial authorities on Roanoke Island: Sir Richard Grenville, Captain Lane, and Manteo, Lord of Roanoke." (VERIFIED) — i.e. an initial 1585 phase, Lane's 1585-86 workshop phase, and a post-Lane reoccupation (the firepits) named for Manteo.
- *Part 2: Science and Industry* ("February 2023, rev. April 2024") [FCF_Reinterpreting_FortRaleigh_Part2.txt] — on the assaying/distilling evidence; not central here.
- *Appendices for Excavating Fort Raleigh*, "published in 2024 by The History Press for Ivor Noël Hume" (i.e. the posthumous Noël Hume volume *Excavating Fort Raleigh: Archaeology at England's First Colony*, The History Press, 2024 — title VERIFIED from [FCF_dig_reports_page.txt]) [FCF_Appendices_Excavating_FortRaleigh.txt]. Appendix 5, "Critique of Previous Excavations at Fort Raleigh", opens with an FCF editor's note: "[Editor's note: Noël Hume's questioning the date of the earthwork construction was based on the stratigraphic relationship of artifacts to structural features, i.e., ditch and rampart. This was correct, but more recent fieldwork by First Colony Foundation reveals that the earthwork, while not the first Tudor activity at the site, nevertheless can be firmly assigned to Lane's colony." (VERIFIED, line 989 area). Noël Hume's text also sets aside "Gans scholar Gary Grassl's arguments that the earthwork was erected by [a later garrison]" (VERIFIED fragment, line 993; footnote 3 cites Gary Carl Grassl, *The Search for the First English Settlement in America: America's First Science Center*), and voices the tourist's question "Why so small a haven for so many people?" (VERIFIED, line 1059). (Grassl's specific proposal — that the earthwork was built by a Captain Page's detachment in 1758-66 — is from my earlier reading of the same appendix; wording UNVERIFIED.)
- FCF "The Earthwork" virtual-site page (undated; VERIFIED [FCF_virtual_earthwork.txt]): "Archaeologists have found some clues to the location of Sir Walter Raleigh's 'Virginia' colonies, but no evidence for the settlement of the remains of the colonists has yet been found. The most important piece of verifiable evidence is the small Elizabethan earthwork fort ..." and it poses the unresolved question "To determine why there was a small earthen fort with no nearby settlement, we need to understand its function."

Clay Swindell (FCF) to Popular Archaeology, 21 May 2025 (VERIFIED [PopArch_Lost_and_Found_evidence.txt]): "None of the artifacts or features here are evidence of domestic activity ... Harrington's work, Hume's work, and our (more recent) work didn't show any kind of habitation here (that would be characteristic of a domestic settlement, such as at Jamestown)." He argues the "traditional 'Fort Raleigh' location identified with the earthwork ... acted like a 'red herring'", and that the settlement should be sought near a usable landing on Shallowbag Bay, where "back in the 30's, there was a young man who found an English sixpence in that area. Other (unprovenanced) small finds have cropped up in the area. None of them have been purs[ued]".

### 1.7 Current state of the Roanoke Island question

- **1587 settlement, palisade, houses, burials:** none identified anywhere on the island. This is the consistent verdict of Harrington (1962), Luccketti (1996), Klingelhofer (2008), FCF's current web pages, and Swindell (2025) — all VERIFIED above. The only features ever proposed as post-1586 are the three firepits in the watchtower (NPS 2021; explicitly speculative) and the undated Harriot Woods slot trench.
- **The earthwork:** built by Englishmen c. 1585-87 on artefactual grounds (Harrington); Noël Hume doubted it and floated an 18th-century date (NPS HRS); Ehrenhard et al. attributed the 1965 "outwork" to later colonial fortification; FCF (2022-24) now argues stratigraphically that assay debris underlies the rampart, so the earthwork post-dates the start of the 1585 workshop but belongs to Lane's colony — a "sconce"/gun position, not the colony's main fort or town. This FCF conclusion is asserted in foundation PDFs; I found no peer-reviewed publication of the 2021 stratigraphy.
- **Where the town was:** unknown. Candidate explanations in the sources: erosion of the north shore (FCF landscape studies; Watts 2008 "the north end of Roanoke Island has eroded extensively since the sixteenth century", VERIFIED [FCF_report_roanoke_2007.txt]); a location on Shallowbag Bay (Swindell 2025); or under later development. American Heritage summarised in 2010: "Over the past 50 years, archaeological digs at the Fort Raleigh National Historic Site on Roanoke Island have failed to turn up evidence of the palisades of the English fort of 1585 or the building of the lost colonists' settlement of 1587." (VERIFIED [AmericanHeritage_2010_new_clues.txt], vol. 60 no. 1).

## 2. The patches on John White's "La Virginea Pars" (British Museum P&D 1906,0509.1.3)

### 2.1 What was done, by whom, when

Brent Lane (FCF board member; UNC Kenan-Flagler adjunct) asked the British Museum to look under two paper patches on White's manuscript map of the Carolina sounds; per the Outer Banks Voice he "contacted the museum on Feb. 23, and had his answer on Feb. 24" (2012) (VERIFIED [OBXVoice_2012_05_03_map_clue.txt]). The BM Department of Conservation and Scientific Research produced an internal report, CSR Report no. AR2012-21, dated 09/03/12, "Examination of patches on a map of the E coast of America by John White ('La Virginea Pars'; 1906,0509.1.3)", authors Janet Ambers, Joanna Russell, David Saunders and (imaging) Antony Rugheimer — "Analysis requested by K Sloan (P&D)"; the report carries the notice "This report contains unpublished research. Its contents should not be published without the permission of the Keeper of the Department of Conservation and Scientific Research" (all VERIFIED [BM_findings_via_FCF_2012_wayback.txt]; the PDF was hosted by FCF at firstcolonyfoundation.org/documents/british_museum_findings.pdf and is now only reachable via the Wayback Machine — the live URL returns 403 [BM_findings_via_FCF_2012.txt]). The findings were announced by FCF and the BM on 3 May 2012 [FCF_news_hidden_images_map.txt; OBXVoice_2012_05_03_map_clue.txt; NBC_2012_map_clue.txt] and published as: Janet Ambers, Joanna Russell, David Saunders and Kim Sloan, "Hidden history?: examination of two patches on John White's map of 'Virginia'", *British Museum Technical Research Bulletin* 6 (2012), pp. 47-54 (VERIFIED: article begins on p. 47 of the volume PDF [BM_TRB_Volume6_full.txt]; end page from my earlier reading of the same PDF).

Methods (VERIFIED, TRB 6): transmitted-light visible imaging, infrared reflectography, ultraviolet-reflected imaging and image processing, plus XRF and Raman spectroscopy for pigments. The patches "are securely fixed to the underlying sheet and could not be removed without risk to the surface of the object" and "It was not possible to identify the adhesive used to hold the patches in place as destructive sampling would have been required." (VERIFIED [BM_findings_via_FCF_2012_wayback.txt]).

### 2.2 What is under the patches — the BM's own words (all VERIFIED [BM_TRB_Volume6_full.txt] unless noted)

- Summary: "Technical imaging and non-invasive analysis of the areas covered by two paper patches on an Elizabethan map of the coast of North Carolina ('La Virginea Pars': P&D 1906,0509.1.3), drawn by the gentleman artist John White, has revealed a number of changes made to the original design. One patch was used to make minor alterations to the representation of the coastline. The second patch covers a large symbol, which apparently represents a fort or fortification, and bears on its surface a faint version of a similar symbol."
- Patch 1 (southern, near Secotan/Pamlico): coastline correction; the "readiest remedy was to cover them with a patch."
- Patch 2 (northern, at the Chowan/Roanoke confluence, west end of Albemarle Sound): "The transmitted visible image ... revealed an earlier version that was very different from the current appearance of the map, featuring a symbol in the shape of a large lozenge or four-pointed star, outlined in blue and filled with red, with a small circle to the right adjacent to the coastline. When examined closely, the two leftmost points of the large symbol can just be seen on the surface of the main map protruding from under the left edge of the patch. ... a square dark feature can be discerned in the centre of the symbol."
- Interpretation, hedged: "While the detailed interpretation of this symbol is beyond the scope of this study and is best left to experts in the field, it seems certain to represent a fort or fortification [11]. The use of signs on maps at this period was very much in flux [12] and it is not clear if this shape is intended as a symbolic representation or as an actual depiction of the ground plan of a fort or of an intended, but unbuilt, fort. The shape revealed seems to have parallels with another of White's drawings in the British Museum collections – the plan of an entrenchment near Cape Rojo, Puerto Rico (P&D 1906,0509.1.5: Figure 8)." (VERIFIED)
- The surface symbol on top of patch 2: it "appears to have a central cross shaped feature (although interpretation of this is complicated by a horizontal fold line which passes through the centre of both patch and symbol) and to be surrounded by two concentric squares, incomplete on the coastal side" (VERIFIED [BM_findings_via_FCF_2012_wayback.txt]). The lines are faint brown; XRF "showed no detectable elements present at levels above those expected from the underlying paper, paper patch and concealed symbol. Taken together, these findings suggest that some form of organic compound or a material based on very light elements was used, although it cannot be pure carbon as it does not show up in the infrared reflectograms." (VERIFIED, TRB 6). Pigments on the map: blue = indigo, red = red lead/vermilion (VERIFIED, both BM documents).
- Conclusions (VERIFIED, TRB 6): the patches "appear to be contemporary or near contemporary with it. The first patch may fulfil the expected purpose ... altering and correcting a flawed initial layout. The second is more unusual, concealing a boldly coloured symbol apparently indicating a fort or signifying the intended location of one. Even more surprisingly, having once covered this sign, it was then redrawn, albeit at a smaller scale and in slightly different outline format on the surface. While the surface depiction is now barely visible, in the absence of a definitive material identification it is difficult to know just how clear it would have been originally. However, the fine lines used suggest that it could never have dominated the map in the way the first, now covered, symbol must have done. The interpretation, within a wider Elizabethan context, of the symbols that were made or covered is beyond the scope of this study, as are any connections they may have with the intended site of the 'Cittie of Raleigh' or with the 'Lost Colony', the abortive first attempt at a European settlement in North America."
- The AR2012-21 report also states: "There is no evidence of any damage underlying the patches and they seem to have been used to alter or correct features in the original layout, a common practice at this period." (VERIFIED [BM_findings_via_FCF_2012_wayback.txt]).

### 2.3 "Correction" vs "concealment" — who says what

- **Brent Lane (FCF), 3 May 2012:** "This is really the best new clue in 150 years ... This is really a good solid lead. But it's not conclusive, and it won't be until we find something." and "It's certainly possible that Raleigh and his team covered it up ... Whether they changed their mind or just concealed it has yet to be determined." (VERIFIED [OBXVoice_2012_05_03_map_clue.txt]).
- **James Horn (then Colonial Williamsburg; FCF board), AP/NBC 3 May 2012:** "We believe that this evidence provides conclusive proof that they moved westward up the Albemarle Sound to the confluence of the Chowan and Roanoke rivers ... Their intention was to create a settlement. And this is what we believe we are looking at with this symbol — their clear intention" (VERIFIED [NBC_2012_map_clue.txt]). Note that "conclusive proof" here is a historian's inference from an intention-symbol, not an archaeological finding.
- **Kim Sloan (BM curator), as recounted by Andrew Lawler (*The Secret Token*, 2018; excerpt at CrimeReads):** "I said to Alice, 'I think we just discovered the intended site of the Cittie of Raleigh.' Then I swore. ... I had that 'Oh. My. God.' moment. As in 'Oh my God' there are going to be so many people asking about this damn thing. I knew the first question would be about the Lost Colony." (VERIFIED [CrimeReads_2018_Lawler_secret_maps.txt]). Lawler's caption describes the surface symbol as "likely outlined in urine-based invisible ink" — this is Lawler's/journalistic characterisation; the BM report only says an organic or light-element material that is not carbon (VERIFIED, both).
- **FCF's later position:** the Site Y report's concluding statement (Dec 2020) says "Site Y has substantiated the FCF's suspicion that the patch on the British Museum's Virginea Pars map indicates an unsuspected goal in Sir Walter Raleigh's plans." (VERIFIED [FCF_news_new_clues_lost_colonists.txt]). FCF's June 2026 statement calls its header image an "Enhanced fort image showing the location, fifty miles west of Roanoke Island." (VERIFIED [FCF_news_lost_colony_is_found.txt]).
- **Horton (CAS), 2016:** "The water was muddied by discovery of an Elizabethan map ... This, however, has not deterred another group of archaeologists to go to where the fort was located." (VERIFIED [CoastalReview_2016_05_back_to_square_one.txt]).

### 2.4 What the map does and does not show (assessment)

1. The map was drawn from the 1585-86 surveys (Lane's expedition); it is not a 1587-90 document. A fort symbol on it — hidden or visible — records a plan or proposal current in 1585-86, i.e. Lane's known interest in the Chowan ("goodly high land" on the west bank; FCF quotes this, VERIFIED [FCF_news_new_clues_lost_colonists.txt]). It cannot by itself show that anyone went there in 1587.
2. The BM authors deliberately withheld interpretation; "conclusive proof" (Horn) and "best new clue in 150 years" (Lane) are FCF-side claims. The BM found no damage under either patch, so the "repair" explanation is out; "correction of a flawed layout" (patch 1) and "concealment or revision of a fort symbol" (patch 2) are the BM's own descriptions.
3. The re-drawn fainter symbol on top of patch 2 is compatible with either (a) an intentional secret marking ("invisible ink" — Lawler) or (b) an ordinary redrawing in a faint medium that has since faded. The BM could not identify the medium.
4. The patch's chief effect was heuristic: it sent FCF to the Salmon Creek/Chowan area, where Site X (already known from a 2007 CRM survey of the Merry Hill/Salmon Creek tract — per NYT 2015: the foundation decided "to re-examine the 2007 findings from Merry Hill and other dig sites in the region", VERIFIED [NYT_2015_08_11_lost_and_found.txt]) was re-investigated. Whether Site X/Y confirm the map depends entirely on the dating arguments in §3.

### 3.3 FCF's dating argument (Border ware and pipe stems)

- Border ware chronology: "A key to identifying the earliest colonial life was a type of ceramic known as Surrey-Hampshire Border ware, which was no longer imported to the New World after the Virginia Company dissolved in the early 17th century." (VERIFIED [NYT_2015_08_11_lost_and_found.txt], reporter's paraphrase). Luccketti 2015: "Our research into the two kinds of pottery shows they are only found on very early 17th-century sites in Virginia." (VERIFIED [WUNC_2015_SiteX.txt]). Site Y report conclusion (Dec 2020): "any one of the Roanoke Colony Period Ceramics types found at Site Y could be found on historic sites dating to the mid-17th century; indeed, Surrey-Hampshire Border ware has been found on a couple of sites in Virginia that date to the second quarter of the 17th centu[ry] ... But FCF research, which examined the occurrence of Surrey-Hampshire Border ware on twenty Virginia sites dating to the first half of the 17th-century shows that it declined precipitously after about 1630, and therefore almost certainly does not date to the time of the earliest English settlement in the region in 1655 by Nathaniel Batts." (VERIFIED [FCF_news_new_clues_lost_colonists.txt]).
- Pipe stems: "The absence of colonial tobacco pipe stems with large bore holes presents clear evidence that there was no prior undocumented 17th-century English settlement at the site. First Colony Foundation research comparing key ceramics from Site Y to their occurrence on more than 20 pre-1650 sites in Virginia produced results indicating that the Site Y ceramics cannot date to the Batts period." (VERIFIED [BertieCounty_2020_FCF_press_release_SiteY.txt]). NatGeo 2020: "One intriguing clue that points to Roanoke colonists rather than Jamestown traders is the lack of early 17th-century clay pipes at Site X and Site Y." (VERIFIED [NatGeo_survivor_camp.txt]).
- Trade-goods argument (FCF report as quoted by Popular Archaeology, VERIFIED [PopArch_Lost_and_Found_evidence.txt]): "missing at Site X are the most common trade goods, like beads.......fragments of European pottery do occur in a few VA/NC Indian site assemblages that probably date as early as the 1660s, but they are not common, and it seems that ceramics were not routinely carried into Indian country by traders until the mid-1700s." NatGeo 2015: "Luccketti adds that, unlike the Cape Creek site, there are no obvious trade goods that suggest exchange instead of resident colonists." (VERIFIED).
- Documentary gap 1607-1655 (Phillip Evans, FCF, "English in 'ye Chowan country' between 1584 and 1665", undated FCF page): "There is likewise no documentation of English traders becoming established in the region before the arrival of Nathaniel Batts in the 1650s. There is nothing however to preclude trade goods entering the Chowan through undocumented activities, but English interest in the Chowan between 1607 and 1655 would seemingly limit such goods to those types identified through historical and archaeological research as common to the Anglo-Indian trade of the period." (VERIFIED [FCF_news_english_in_chowan_country.txt]).
- Outside ceramic opinion: Jacqui Pearce (Museum of London Archaeology): "I have no problem with their interpretation of the ceramics in question as possibly late 16th century and potentially associated with the Lost Colony ... [she] says it seems unlikely this particular collection of pots was made after 1650" (VERIFIED [NatGeo_survivor_camp.txt]). Ivor Noël Hume, 2015: "I couldn't date artifacts between 1590 and 1630 ... Did someone keep something for six weeks or six years? It is very hard to know." (VERIFIED [NatGeo_2015_Lawler_clues.txt]).

- **Henry Wright (Michigan), 2020:** "One must find artifacts of known 16th-century date in a stratigraphically sealed context" (VERIFIED [NatGeo_survivor_camp.txt]).
- **Jamestown-era trade alternative (c. 1607-1650):** NatGeo 2020: "The ceramics at Site X and Site Y conceivably could have been left by later English traders who came from Jamestown" (VERIFIED). FCF's rebuttals are the pipe-stem absence, the Border ware decline after c. 1630, and the absence of beads/trade goods (§3.3). - **Lane's 1586 expedition alternative:** Lane's party ascended the Chowan to Chawanoac in spring 1586 (documentary). Kitchen ceramics (Border ware, baluster jar) are equally at home on a military expedition's boats; nothing published excludes a 1586 origin for the small Site X assemblage.
- **Adjacency to Mettaquem:** "Clay Swindell, an archaeologist associated with the First Colony Foundation who examined Mettaquem, said the palisaded village was home to some 80 to 100 people. Just outside its wall, at a place they called Site X, Luccketti's team found no fort, but they did uncover two dozen pieces of English pottery" (VERIFIED [NatGeo_survivor_camp.txt]). FCF reads Site X as colonists living beside Native hosts and Site Y (no Native material) as "a more independent English settlement"; the alternative reading — English goods discarded at a Native town — is exactly the trade hypothesis FCF rejects on the grounds above.
- **Horton (CAS):** "no Border ware pottery — a telltale sign of habitation — has been found on the Hatteras site" (NYT 2015, reporter's summary, VERIFIED); Dawson: "there's no science to back up what they're saying" (2016, VERIFIED [CoastalReview_2016_05_back_to_square_one.txt]).
- **Peer review status:** NYT 2015: the findings "have been submitted for peer review" (VERIFIED); NatGeo 2015: "none of the groups have yet published detailed scholarly articles analyzing and cataloguing their discoveries" (VERIFIED). As of Sept 2026 I found no journal article on Site X or Y. The fullest publication is *Excavating the Lost Colony Mystery*, ed. ### 3.7 Assessment of Sites X and Y

What is solid: a small, typologically coherent group of English kitchen/storage ceramics of late-16th/early-17th-century types (Border ware, North Devon baluster jar, plus at Site Y London/Essex redwares, Frechen, Martincamp, Spanish olive jar), with a few metal dress/gun items, at two loci on the Chowan shore; an absence of the bead-and-pipe signature typical of later trade; a nearby Native town (Mettaquem) at Site X and none at Site Y. What is missing: any structure, hearth, pit, well, fence line or grave of English type; any sealed 16th-century deposit; any published quantified assemblage for Site Y; any independently dated organic material; any peer-reviewed report. The dating window FCF proposes (1587-c.1600) is bracketed against alternatives (1586 Lane party; 1607-1650 trade or undocumented visitors; the 1655+ Batts period) by typological frequency arguments only. The excavators' own language in the technical/edited-volume register ("no smoking gun"; "circumstantial"; "does not solve") is more reliable than the 2026 web statement. Verdict: **plausible but unproven English presence, probably short; residence vs. trade/visit not yet distinguishable; attribution to the 1587 colonists specifically is an inference from the historical context, not from the archaeology.**

## 4. Hatteras Island: Cape Creek / Buxton (31DR1, the "Croatoan" town site)

### 4.1 Discovery and Phelps's ECU excavations, 1993-98

- The midden at Cape Creek was (re)discovered after Hurricane Emily: Fred L. (VERIFIED [CoastalReview_2016_05_back_to_square_one.txt]); field seasons ran to 1998.
- Phelps's dating of the site: "Phelps had dated what he called 'the Hatteras site' from 1650 to 1720." (VERIFIED [CoastalReview_2024_10_maps.txt]).
- The "Kendall" signet ring (Sept 1998) and snaphaunce lock: Popular Archaeology's summary of Phelps's 1998 season lists "lead shot, Native and European pipe fragments, nails, glass beads, copper items, bricks, ceramic ware, and glass fragments and bottles, they uncovered a late 16th century snaphouse gunlock and a well-preserved 16th century style bronze signet ring. (VERIFIED [CoastalReview_2016_05_back_to_square_one.txt], Catherine Kozak, May 2016, reprinted from Island Free Press 20 Apr 2016 [IslandFreePress_2016_all_colonists_hatteras.txt]). ### 4.2 The ring re-examined (2017) — Ewen & Farrell 2019

### 4.5 Assessment of Hatteras

What is solid: a large, long-lived Croatoan/Hatteras Indian town site (31DR1) with abundant 17th-century European trade goods and some objects of 16th-century type (Nuremberg token, snaphaunce parts, rapier hilt, aiglets), mostly from 17th-century contexts; evidence of Native re-working of European metal and glass; a strong documentary reason (the "CROATOAN" carving) to expect at least a visit. What is claimed but unpublished: a pre-shell-midden pit with hammerscale (2024), radiocarbon dates "from the 1580s" on animal teeth (4 dates by June 2026), a musket ball in a deer jaw, isotopes, a "blacksmith shop" (Dawson's word). What would be needed to credit it: a section drawing and context register showing the hammerscale-bearing deposit sealed by dated Native midden; the forge features (hearth, anvil base, slag, charcoal) Leeson and Ewen ask for; the laboratory numbers, calibrations and reservoir corrections for the radiocarbon dates (marine/estuarine diet can shift dates on deer/pig teeth less than on fish, but the sample identity matters); and, above all, a report. The alternative explanations still open are: (a) 1585-86 activity (Grenville's fleet and Lane's men were at Croatoan in 1585; Dawson himself allows "it is possible the musket ball comes from the 1585 crowd"); (b) 1590s-1600s English or other European visits/wrecks; (c) heirloom curation and 17th-century trade (Horton's own 2020 caveat); (d) Native smithing with acquired iron in the 17th century — hammerscale shows hot forging happened, not who did it, although the 16th-century date, if sustained, would make (d) unlikely. Verdict: **the strongest-sounding claim of 2025-26 is also the least documented; until published it cannot be weighed against the Bertie evidence.**

### 5.2 Where was "Croatoan"? FCF's mainland argument (2024)

Eric Klingelhofer, "Croatan: The Untold Story" (FCF history page, signed "Eric Klingelhofer, April 2024"): tracing map names, he argues "that a broad territory was attributed in the historical period to the remnant Croatoans, and that the likely location for their core habitation and Dasemunkepeuc itself lay northwest of Roanoke in the vicinity of modern Mashoes. Because no member of the Croatoan tribe had lived at their former location since the 1500s, the term Hatteras, on modern-day Bodie Island, was soon applied to the empty island to the south. It also supports the theory that members of the Coree tribe later came to occupy that island." He also says Roanoke Island's village "was abandoned in 1586. One year later, after a small war band from Damunkepeuc killed the settler George Howe, the Roanokes also left Dasemunkepeuc. Almost immediately, Croatoan families from modern-day Hatteras ca[me ...]" and ends: "[Manteo's] Algonquians would have known that the Lost Colony had moved to the fertile lands along the Chowan River and the protection of the Chowanoke tribe." (all VERIFIED [FCF_history_croatan_untold_story.txt]). FCF, "Roanoke and Croatan Tribal Movement" (17 Oct 2024): "the name 'Croatan' appears on numerous maps of Carolina: the 1671 First Lords Proprietors Map and Moxon's map and on Speed's 1676 map as all the mainland east of Alligator River. ... on the Thames School cartographers' 1684 map, on Maule's 1718 MS survey of Roanoke, and on the 1733 Moseley map, the Croatan area is confined on the mainland northwest of [Roanoke]" and "two coalesced tribes, Lumbee and Coharie, today claim their distant Elizabethan descent" (VERIFIED [FCF_news_roanoke_croatan_tribal_movement.txt]). This is a documentary/cartographic argument by the Bertie-County excavators, aimed at weakening the Hatteras case; it does not change what is in the ground at 31DR1, which all parties accept as the Croatoan/Hatteras Indian town of the 1500s-1700s (Phelps's identification, VERIFIED [CoastalReview_2024_10_maps.txt]).

### 5.3 The Hatteras Indians and modern descendant claims

Phelps identified 31DR1 as "the Croatan capital that stretched a half-mile from Cape Creek to Buxton village" (VERIFIED [CoastalReview_2024_10_maps.txt]). Dawson "can trace [his family's] roots on Hatteras back to the 1600's when a Dutchman named Thomas Mueller shipwrecked on the island, was rescued by the Croatoan Indians, and later married a Croatoan woman named Rea" (VERIFIED [IslandFreePress_CAS_new_discovery.txt], CAS's own biography). Lawson's 1709 report of Hatteras Indians with grey eyes who said their ancestors "could talk in a book" is documentary (see the documents dossier). ### 5.4 Lumbee / "Croatan Indians of Robeson County" (McMillan 1885-1888)

- Hamilton McMillan (state legislator) sponsored the act of 10 Feb 1885 recognising the "Indians of Robeson County" as "Croatan Indians" and published his theory in 1885 and as *Sir Walter Raleigh's Lost Colony. An Historical Sketch of the Attempts of Sir Walter Raleigh to Establish a Colony in Virginia, with the Traditions of an Indian Tribe in North Carolina ...* (1888) (title VERIFIED from the archive.org record [McMillan1888_fulltext.txt; mcmillan_sir_walter_raleighs_lost_colony_1888_djvu.txt]). NCpedia: "In 1885, the three hundredth anniversary year of the departure of the Ralph Lane colony from Roanoke Island, McMillan published a pamphlet on the Lost Colony that presented his personal conclusions linking the Croatan Indians and the English colonies to the Robeson County Indians." (VERIFIED [NCpedia_McMillan.txt]); "One scholar has identified no fewer than seven theories about the origins of the Lumbees" (VERIFIED [NCpedia_Lumbee.txt]). McMillan's evidence was surnames, dialect and oral tradition — no archaeology.
- Malinda Maynor Lowery (Lumbee historian), "Wingina, Wanchese and Manteo: A Lumbee Perspective on the Lost Colony" (Coastwatch, NC Sea Grant, 2019): "most of the similar names are found as much in the English population as in the Lumbee population. Further, we have no evidence that American Indians regarded English surnames as more special than any other kind of name; it is difficult to imagine that a group of Indians who took in foreign refugees would have changed their naming practices to accommodate these newcomers and then maintained them for over 200 years." and "there are many more plausible reasons for Lumbees' similarity to Europeans other than their possible connection to the Lost Colony." (VERIFIED [Coastwatch_Lumbee_perspective.txt]).
- Robert K. Thomas, "A Report of Research on Lumbee Origins" (1976; transcribed at nativeheritageproject.com, 17 July 2012): on McMillan's tradition, "Now this is very slim evidence for evidence of a Lost Colony connection"; and "the evidence I have just cited leads me to believe that the Lost Colony was massacred, according to testimony of Indians in Virginia." (VERIFIED [NativeHeritage_Thomas_report_part1.txt]).
- FCF (2024) notes that the Sampson County Coharie were also recognised as "Croatan" and that George Butler's 1916 booklet examined those traditions (VERIFIED [FCF_news_roanoke_croatan_tribal_movement.txt]).
- **Archaeological content: none.** No excavated site in Robeson or Sampson County has produced 16th-century English material in any source consulted.

### 5.6 The Chesapeake: Great Neck (44VB7) and Quinn's thesis

- Quinn's thesis (documentary; see documents dossier): most colonists moved north to the Chesapeake Bay area (the Chesepians near the Lynnhaven/Elizabeth rivers) and were killed on Powhatan's orders c. 1607 (UNVERIFIED here; standard reference D. B. Quinn, *Set Fair for Roanoke*, 1985). Strachey's report of the "slaughter at Roanoke" and Powhatan's prophecy are in [VirginiaPlaces_Chesapeake.txt] (VERIFIED as quoted there).
- The archaeological candidate for Chesepioc is Great Neck, Virginia Beach (44VB7). Va. Pilot, 17 Mar 1996 (VERIFIED [VaPilot_1996_GreatNeck.txt]): "When archaeologists were excavating at Great Neck in the 1980s, they discovered the remains of a group of longhouses encircled by a palisade, the only such settlement belonging to the Chesapeake Indians that has ever been found." (E. Randolph Turner, VDHR); "We found longhouses with oval ends". "Bones of 64 people were excavated by the archeologists" and were later reburied by the Nansemond (VERIFIED [VirginiaPlaces_Chesapeake.txt]).
- The report: Mary Ellen N. Hodges, *Native American Settlement at Great Neck: Report on VDHR Archaeological Investigations of Woodland Components at Site 44VB7, Virginia Beach, Virginia, 1981-1987*, Research Report Series No. 9, Virginia Department of Historic Resources, Richmond, 1998 (full citation VERIFIED via Gallivan 2011 [JAR_Gallivan_2011_Chesapeake.txt]; full text VERIFIED [Hodges1998_GreatNeck_44VB7_fulltext.txt]). Gallivan: "Near the mouth of the James, excavations at the Great Neck site identified a 16th-century palisaded settlement with an unusually large longhouse structure and burials with associated copper grave goods (Hodges 1998). The settlement, likely a political center tied to the Chesapeake Indians, contained both Roanoke and Townsend ceramics." (VERIFIED, Martin D. Gallivan, "The Archaeology of Native Societies in the Chesapeake: New Investigations and Interpretations", *Journal of Archaeological Research* 19 (2011), DOI 10.1007/s10814-010-9048-4; volume/pages UNVERIFIED beyond the DOI).
- **Hodges on European material — the key passage** (VERIFIED [Hodges1998_GreatNeck_44VB7_fulltext.txt], lines c. 7318-7332): "No evidence has been obtained from analysis of the artifacts recovered from Lot 16 to support the hypothesis that the latest Native American occupation on the property dates from after the period of sustained European contact in Virginia (see results of radiocarbon dating of Feature 29A and analysis of pipe bowl residue and copper). Nor were any European or American-made artifacts manufactured prior to ca. 1850 recovered from either surface, plowzone, or intact archaeological contexts on the site." On the copper (lines c. 7388-7400): X-ray diffraction of "triangular ornaments from Feature 18B and the bead located at the neck of Burial 25A ... Although not conclusive, the results of the analysis suggest the copper was originally obtained from geologic deposits in North America, since copper items of 16th-century European manufacture would be expected to contain a higher proportion of impurities (Stephen Clements, personal communication 1982)." And on the tobacco residue (lines c. 7464-7474): "The results of the analysis provide no supporting evidence for the hypothesis that the palisaded settlement on Lot 16 was occupied after European contact with the native populations of Eastern North America."
- **Verdict:** at the one excavated Chesapeake Indian town, the excavators found no 16th-century European object and no evidence of post-contact occupation; the copper is probably native. No other Chesapeake-area site with 16th-century English material was located in my searches (Encyclopedia Virginia's "Chesapeake Indians" entry was fetched but blocked/empty [EncyclopediaVirginia_Chesapeake_Indians.txt]). Quinn's thesis is therefore archaeologically untested rather than refuted: the relevant villages (on the Lynnhaven and Elizabeth rivers) are largely under modern Virginia Beach/Norfolk.

### 6.2 Which finds are diagnostic of 1587-1600 English *residence* rather than trade or transit?

Applying the usual criteria (sealed context; English-built structure; English burial rite; English foodways; objects that are not trade goods and not curated; tight dating):

- **Sealed context with tight date:** none published. Bertie: plough zone throughout. Hatteras: the pre-midden hammerscale pit is claimed to be sealed and dated, but nothing has been published; all earlier Hatteras finds are from 17th-century contexts by the excavators' own admission. Roanoke Island: the Harriot Woods/Elizabethan Gardens horizon is sealed but yields 1584-86 material.
- **English-built structures:** none anywhere. FCF: "We have no features of European-built structures. We have not found a fort." Ewen's minimum ("some structures, some evidence of European burials") is unmet at every site.
- **Burials:** no European-rite burial of the period has been found in North Carolina; Hatteras Native burials have not been reported as showing European ancestry; Great Neck burials are Native with native copper.
- **English foodways/midden:** none. No cattle/pig/sheep bone, no wheat, no English-style hearth or oven reported at Bertie or Hatteras (Coastal Review 2016 noted deer, fish, turtle, corn and peas at Cape Creek; Dawson said in June 2026 that pig remains are "next" for dating — an as-yet-unreported claim).
- **Non-trade, non-curated objects:** the best candidates are (i) hammerscale (a waste product nobody trades — but it proves iron-working, not who did it or exactly when, until the context is published); (ii) Border ware and baluster-jar kitchen ceramics (FCF: not routinely traded before the mid-1700s — a fair point, but they are equally the kit of an English boat party in 1586 or 1608); (iii) the slate tablet and cloth seal (personal/commercial items rather than trade goods, but single objects, unstratified or in later contexts).
- **Dating to 1587-1600 specifically:** no artefact class in play can be dated more closely than roughly 1580-1630 (Noël Hume: "I couldn't date artifacts between 1590 and 1630"); radiocarbon on short-lived samples can in principle (the 1580s falls before the c. 1640-1700 plateau, so a 1580s date is distinguishable from a 1650s date — but a calibrated 2-sigma range will still typically span several decades, and the four Hatteras dates have not been published with their ranges).

**Conclusion.** Nothing yet published is diagnostic of 1587-1600 English residence. Bertie shows English domestic pottery of the right general period without any of the residence signatures; Hatteras shows Croatoan-English interaction over a century, with a residence-type signature (smithing) claimed but undocumented. The one interpretive point that all sides share — that some colonists very probably went to Croatoan for at least a time, and that a Chowan relocation was planned — comes from the documents, not the spade.

### 6.3 What an archaeologist would need to see

1. At least one English-built structure of 1580s type — a post-in-ground or mud-and-stud house, a palisade line with an entrance, a well, a bread oven — with 16th-century material in its construction/fill and no 17th-century material.
2. A Christian-rite burial (extended, supine, east-west, possibly coffined) of the period, ideally with isotopes/aDNA showing English origin; or, conversely, a Native burial of c. 1600-1650 with a European-origin individual or a first-generation admixed individual.
3. A midden or pit group with English foodways (domestic animal bone, imported grain, English cooking vessels together) in a sealed deposit radiocarbon-dated on short-lived material to the late 16th century, published with lab numbers and calibration curves.
4. For the hammerscale: a hearth/anvil base, slag, charcoal, and the section showing the pit beneath undisturbed Native midden; the same for the "blacksmith shop".
5. Quantified assemblages (sherd counts, MNV, pipe-bore histograms, bead counts) for Sites X and Y and for 31DR1, so that the trade-vs-residence argument can be made by others.
6. Full reports available for scrutiny — the single most important missing item on both sides (FCF's Site X/Y reports; Phelps's and Horton's Cape Creek excavations).
