# Dossier D — The Dare Stones, with a separate verdict on the FIRST stone (the "Chowan River stone", November 1937)

Downloads are in `sources/` (file names given in the reference list). 

**Marking conventions.** VERIFIED = I read the quoted words in the cited source (or in a reprint/OCR of it, which is then stated). UNVERIFIED = taken from a secondary summary or from memory; not read in the original. Where a quotation comes from a fan-site transcription rather than a scan (the Sparkes 1941 article and Robert White's 1991 chapter), it is marked VERIFIED-REPRINT and the caveat applies that transcription errors are possible.

**Bottom line (details and the reasoning in §7):** the 47 later stones are forgeries beyond reasonable doubt (Eberhardt and associates; documented by Sparkes 1941, the Emory geologist Lester's 1939 report, the anachronistic vocabulary, and the May 1941 "We Dare Anything" episode). The first stone is a separate case: nothing physically or documentarily ties it to Eberhardt, its language is far more competent than his, and the 2016 slice shows the incised letters are darkened like the exterior rather than bright like the fresh interior. Against it stand the finder's unverifiable identity and evasive conduct, his own destruction of the groove evidence (steel brush and indelible pencil), the 1937 publicity context, the unfound "rock with names", a handful of spellings with no period precedent, the non-standard three-initial signature and the unattested abbreviation "Via", and (if the secondary reports are right — UNVERIFIED, see §3.2) Morison's own later disavowal. My estimate: roughly 75–85 % probability that the first stone is a 20th-century fabrication, which is to say that its authenticity is unlikely but not excluded; a properly controlled tool-mark and weathering study of the un-cleaned incisions Loubser identified in 2011 is the test most likely to settle it.

## 0. What the "Dare Stones" are

Forty-eight (by Sparkes's count forty-nine, because no. 46 came in two pieces) inscribed stones delivered to Haywood J. Pearce Jr. and his father between November 1937 and January 1941, now at Brenau University, Gainesville, Georgia. Stone no. 1 (21 lb 4 oz of vein quartz, said to have been found near the Chowan River in Chowan County, NC) is the only one not connected to the Georgia stonecutter William "Bill" Eberhardt and his associates; nos. 2–48 (soapstone and other rock, found in South Carolina and Georgia in 1939–41) purport to continue Eleanor Dare's story to a death near the Chattahoochee in 1599. Sources: Sparkes 1941 (VERIFIED-REPRINT: "There are forty-eight stones, to date... found, beginning in September, 1937, in North Carolina, South Carolina and Georgia, the latest recovered in January of this year"; and "There are really forty-nine stones, as two pieces of No. 46 were delivered separately. Seventeen months after Hammond appeared with the first, Eberhart appeared. The score thereafter: Eberhart, 42; Bruce, 2 (?); Turner, 2; Eberhart and Turner, 1; Mrs. Jett, 1."); NCpedia 2019 (VERIFIED: "a series of forty-eight rocks"; "Bill Eberhardt provided the Pearces with 42 stones, all later deemed forgeries, for which he was paid a total of about $2,000").

## 1. The FIRST stone: text, physical description, condition, present location

### 1.1 Transcription — Pearce 1938 (the primary published reading)

Source: Haywood J. Pearce, Jr., "New Light on the Roanoke Colony: A Preliminary Examination of a Stone Found in Chowan County, North Carolina", *Journal of Southern History* 4:2 (May 1938), 148–163; read in the Internet Archive OCR of the whole issue (`sources/pearce1938_jsh.txt`). VERIFIED (OCR; the line numbers are Pearce's, "introduced for convenience"). Note on the JSTOR stable ID: 2191862 is also cited for it; NCpedia and Wikipedia give 2192000 / doi 10.2307/2192000. JSTOR was unreachable through the proxy, so I could not confirm which is right; the DOI form is the one two independent secondary sources use.

Pearce, p. 149: "On the smoother side of the stone is carved a crude Latin cross. Beneath the cross are inscribed the lines:"

```
Ananias Dare &
Virginia went hence
Unto Heaven 1591
```
"Near the bottom, on the same side, are inscribed the lines:"
```
Anye Englishman shew
John White Govr Via
```
Pearce: "The cross and the legend immediately below it might indicate that the stone was originally designed as a grave marker, an impression that does not comport with the bottom lines on the face of the stone, nor with the seventeen lines on the reverse side." (VERIFIED)

"On the rougher side are inscribed the following seventeen lines, rendered here, so far as possible, exactly as they appear on the stone:" (VERIFIED)
```
 1  Father soone After yov
 2  goe for Englande wee cam
 3  hither/ onlie misarie & Warre—
 4  tow yeere/ Above halfe DeaDe ere tow
 5  yeere more from sickenes beine fovre & twentie/
 6  salvage with message of shipp unto us/smal
 7  space of time they affrite of revenge rann
 8  al awaye/wee bleeve yt nott-yov/soone after
 9  ye salvages faine spirts angrie/suddiane
10  murther al save seaven/mine childe—
11  ananias to slaine wth mvch misarie—/
12  bvrie al neere fovre myles easte this river
13  vppon smal hil/names writ al ther
14  onrocke/putt this ther alsoe/salvage
15  shew this vnto yov & hither wee
16  promise yov to give greate
17  plentie presents
    EWD
```
Pearce's footnote 3 on the reading (VERIFIED): "All o's and a's (with the exception of the capital A's in lines 1 and 4) are represented by the same sign—an irregular, roughly circular depression. All o's and a's, therefore (with the exception of the two capitals), are conjectural. The bars represent a conjectural division into sentences. It is believed that there is a badly defaced ampersand at the end of line 10. The Elizabethan long s, used throughout the stone, is not rendered in the text as reproduced here." And: "The smooth side of the stone presented little difficulty, being fairly legible to the naked eye. The reverse or back side, however, could only be read, and that tentatively, with the aid of binocular microscope, magnifying glass, and stereoptican slides, greatly enlarged. The stone has been under study and investigation since November 9, 1937."

Two things to notice in Pearce's own rendering: (i) he prints "Unto Heaven" on the face and "unto us" in line 6 with u, but "vnto yov", "yov", "vppon", "bvrie", "mvch", "fovre", "pvtt" with v elsewhere — i.e. his transcription itself mixes the conventions; (ii) line 6 has "message" with double s, and line 9 has "suddiane" (not "suddaine").

Reading of the variants: the popular texts (NCpedia, Lawler, WaPo, History Blog) silently modernise u/v and regularise "suddiane" to "Suddaine", "spirts" to "Spirits", "Ananias" to "Ananais" (or vice versa), "Ther" to "There/Their". The two 1938–41 readings by people who handled the stone (Pearce; Sparkes reproducing Pearce's manuscript) agree on "suddiane", "spirts", "beine", "misarie", "affrite", "bleeve", "yov", "nott". Pearce's "message"/"unto us" versus Sparkes's "mesage"/"vnto vs" in line 6 is the one substantive discrepancy between the two primary readings; since Pearce says all o's and a's are conjectural, and the letters are small and crowded on the rough face, some of these differences are differences of interpretation, not of the stone. No one has published a modern epigraphic re-transcription with photographs at letter scale; that is itself a gap.

What I could see myself on the Brenau photographs reproduced by NCpedia (`sources/dare_stone1_front_ncpedia_brenau.jpg`, `..._back_...jpg`, VERIFIED by inspection): the face reads, in mixed capital and minuscule forms, "AnaniasDArE &" (the "&" is a tall, Y-like sign), "VIRGINIA WENT hEnCE", "VNTO hEAVEN 1591" (V, not U, and Arabic numerals), then lower "ANYE ENGLISHMAN ShEW", "IohN WhITE GOVR VIA" (I for J; a lowercase-form h throughout). The grooves are filled with a white substance (the flour/chalk used for photography). The back is a dense block of small mixed-case letters with "EWD" alone on the last line; at the reproduction size I could not read it letter by letter. Wikipedia's all-capitals rendering ("VNTO HEAVEN", "MVRTHER") is therefore an editorial normalisation, not what is on the stone: the stone mixes capital and small letterforms.

### 1.3 Physical description

Pearce 1938, p. 148 (VERIFIED, OCR; fractions garbled in OCR and reconstructed in brackets): "The stone is rough vein quartz, stained a light rusty brown. The exact weight of the stone is 21 pounds, 4 ounces; the area of the face is 104.78 square inches; the maximum length is [13⅝] inches; the maximum width [9⅝] inches; the maximum thickness [2¼] inches. The stone has a specific gravity of 2.63, and its hardness is reckoned at 7 plus in Moh's scale." Measurements "recorded by Professor J. G. Lester of the Geology Department, and Professor J. H. Purks of the Physics Department, Emory University." (Mental Floss 2023 gives "roughly 14 inches long, 10 inches wide, and 2.5 inches thick" — consistent.)

Lester's petrographic note, in Pearce's n. 2 (VERIFIED): "Megascopically, the rock is a fine to medium grained, holo-crystalline quartz of light color, which has been stained superficially to a light brown color. Microscopically, the rock shows an even texture of anhedrol quartz, which commonly exhibits undulatory extinction due to stains developed during crystallization. Limonite occurs as a stain around and between many of the anhedra. This is perhaps the cause of the light brown color of the rock's surface. The rock is a fragment of quartz which commonly occurs as veins and lenses traversing the rocks of the Appalachian plateau." And: "There are no exposures of rock of any kind in the region, nor are there any exposures of rock encountered within a distance of several miles of the spot. It is thought that the discovered rock was transported from some other place to the point of discovery." (Lester on the find-site, Pearce n. 4.)

Lester on the grooves (Pearce n. 2, VERIFIED): "(1) They are definitely rounded on the lower surface with a very uniform sloping side wall. (2) The channels show gaping on both walls and bottoms. (3) Extremities of the letters are never vertical except where they are intersected by fractures. Rather they seem to be feathered out."

Tool question, Pearce p. 163 (VERIFIED): "Efforts are being made to produce inscriptions on similar stone by various methods. These methods have included sandblasting, drills, and acids. None has so far been successful. Men experienced in stone-cutting who have been consulted are not unanimous, but the prevailing opinion is that the stone could have been chiseled with tools or instruments which it is reasonable to suppose were possessed by the Roanoke colonists." Sparkes 1941 (VERIFIED-REPRINT) reports the reverse: Lester "had taken Hammond's stone to a stone-carving plant at Marietta, Georgia. There older workmen said they could reproduce it with hand tools; but younger men said they would prefer the sandblasting method. Lester himself had carved an inscription on quartz, using the sandblasting method." When Sparkes read Pearce his own claim that duplication "couldn't be done", Pearce "conceded: 'Well, that part's not accurate.'"

Later descriptions: NatGeo 2018 calls it "the quartzite stone" (VERIFIED) — a looser term than Lester's "vein quartz"; the 2016 slice showed a "bright white" interior (§4). The History programme of 2015 reported "no evidence of drilling in the first Dare Stone" and that it "was carved more carefully and with an iron chisel" (Colavito 2015, VERIFIED, reporting the programme). Wolter in 2013 found "the same type of quartzite in Virginia" (Colavito 2013, VERIFIED, reporting).

### 1.4 State of the letters — the contamination problem

- Hammond himself: "he 'cleaned it' with a steel brush, used an indelible lead pencil to intensify the lettering. Whatever his motives, he had frustrated future efforts to determine how long the stone could have been in the swamp." (Sparkes 1941, VERIFIED-REPRINT.)
- Emory used flour/chalk in the grooves for photography (NCpedia: "using flour to make the markings more visible", VERIFIED; the Brenau photo captions of the 2015 filming show flour being applied again, VERIFIED).
- Loubser 2011 (Brenau Window, Spring 2011, VERIFIED): "over the years the Dare Stones' carvings have been enhanced with a chalk-like substance and graphite to make them more photogenic, then scoured, cleaned and pressure washed, which renders such precise testing in those particular spots a little iffy. But Loubser's close examination, he says, turned up 'a few incisions on the sides and within irregularities on the stone's yellow brown surfaces [that] appear to have escaped highlighting and cleaning.'"
- La Vere 2010 (as quoted by Powell 2010, VERIFIED): "It still looks like a chisel did it. The problem was that it was 'corrupted' from the time it was found — gone over with a wire brush, pencils and nail. New techniques would entail destroying parts of the stone, and Brenau ... doesn't seem willing to do that."
- 2016: a slice was cut from one end/bottom (§4), so the "no destructive testing" position has since been relaxed.

### 1.5 Where it is now

Brenau University, Gainesville, Georgia. Brenau's own page (2015/2019 capture, VERIFIED): "This stone, regarded by most authorities as the only Dare Stone, surfaced in 1937"; contact "ewd@brenau.edu"; a viewing/photography policy exists (URL now dead; Wikipedia cites "Policy regarding the Dare Stones", 3 July 2019). Brenau Window 2007 (VERIFIED): the first stone and one other were "in the special collections section of the Trustee Library", the rest in "the basement boiler room under the stage of the university amphitheater". After the 2015 documentary President Schrader "moved the stone to his office, citing security concerns" (Wikipedia citing La Vere 2010/2nd ed., p. 254 — UNVERIFIED as to the primary source). Brenau Window 2011 (VERIFIED): the stones were "on display in Brenau's Trustee Library" when Loubser examined them.

## 2. The find story of the first stone, and the search for Hammond

### 2.1 As published in 1938 (Pearce)

Pearce 1938, p. 150 (VERIFIED): "The stone was brought to Emory University in November, 1937, by the finder, whose name must be withheld for the present. While engaged in a motor tour in coastal North Carolina he chanced to walk some distance from the highway and stumbled upon the stone. He sought in vain for some weeks to decipher the inscription himself, and then came to Emory University to seek aid for that purpose. The finder remained seven or eight days at the University working with members of the faculty engaged in efforts to read the inscription. During this period he accompanied four of the persons co-operating in the investigation to coastal North Carolina and pointed out the approximate spot where he stumbled upon the stone. His address is now known to those who have the stone in custody." Footnote 4: "For sufficient reasons the exact location of the spot where the stone was reported found cannot now be given"; Lester: "The site as pointed out by the finder of the stone was the east bank of the Chowan River in North Carolina. At this locality the bank is a part of a drowned swamp ... The floor of the swamp is above the level of the river, which empties into the Albemarle Sound at no great distance away. The soil of the swamp is a thick black alluvium about four to five feet thick".

The Emory team named by Pearce (n. 3, VERIFIED): Lester (geology), Purks (physics), T. H. English and J. M. Steedman (English), J. D. McCord (staff), Pearce (history). Emory announced the find on 14 November 1937 (Wikipedia citing La Vere, UNVERIFIED); Pearce "permitted publication of the translation, January 31, 1938" (Sparkes, VERIFIED-REPRINT).

### 2.2 As reported in 1941 (Sparkes, from Pearce's manuscript and his own inquiries)

Sparkes 1941 (all VERIFIED-REPRINT):
- "In mid-September, 1937, L. E. Hammond, a small produce dealer of Alameda, California, was touring in North Carolina. He wanted hickory nuts. On a causeway by a swamp about three miles from Edenton, North Carolina, he stopped his car. This swamp, until the causeway was built shortly before, had been as inaccessible as any jungle. About a quarter of a mile in the woods, on the east bank of the Chowan, the tourist stumbled over a rock slab. ... In November, 1937, he came into Atlanta, in search of a translator. Chance brought him to Emory University."
- "Two days later, with Hammond and faculty colleagues, Pearce went to North Carolina, but rains had obliterated all trace of where the stone had lain."
- Emory's president: "Dr. Harvey Warren Cox, the president, said then of the stone: 'It's a fraud. Why should I let you spend the university's money just to prove it's a fraud?'" "At Emory, staff and faculty had been 'suspicious' of Hammond from the beginning."
- The site visit: "university officials arranged for Legare Davis, president of the Atlanta Better Business Bureau, to go along. The university was represented by Pearce, Dean Harris Purks, Prof. James Lester, geologist, and J. C. McCord ... They traveled almost six hundred miles before they learned where Hammond was taking them. On the causeway near Edenton the man brought out a crude map scrawled on a piece of paper bag. He said it was about a quarter of a mile away. But after wandering a mile and a half in the swamp the professors lost patience. Finally Hammond saw a barge grounded on the bank of the Chowan. A sand shoal near by, he said, was where he had washed the rock."
- "Hammond had said he was traveling in a car with his wife. None ever saw Hammond's car. None ever saw his wife. George Mews, treasurer of Emory, tried to shadow the man one night, but was eluded. Another university man tried to get Hammond's fingerprints on a drinking glass. He failed."
- "Because of Hammond's furtiveness before he vanished, the Pinkerton agency was hired to trace his past. They were unable to find anyone who had ever known him. Later the man corresponded with Dean Purks, giving as his address P. O. Box 212, Alameda, California. Then came a proposal from Hammond to exhibit the stone at twenty-five cents a head. That was when Emory washed its hands of the affair, directing Hammond to come and get his stone. Thereafter Professor Pearce carried it to Brenau College. Months later a commercial credit agency sent a representative to see Herbert M. Dove, Oakland, California, whom Hammond finally had named to Pearce as a reference. Dove was found in a little jewelry store. He said he only knew Hammond to be married, about forty; that he had some kind of a pension; that his first name was Louis. The Pearces still knew nothing about a single day in the life of Hammond before he appeared at Emory, but they bought his stone, paying him $1000. Brenau College funds were sent to an Oakland bank. The payee on the bill of exchange was L. E. Hammond P. O. Box 212, Alameda."
- Hammond's return: Turner told Sparkes that "Once a man came and offered him $30,000 to find a rock with 'Yahoo' on it. Said his name was 'L. E. Martin.' That was Hammond, finder of the first stone. He had returned to Georgia and told Pearce, 'Those [later] rocks ain't so'; offered to prove it by getting a stone bearing any word Pearce asked for. Unfortunately for Hammond, he didn't look as if he had $30,000. At the time, Pearce was with Hammond by day, with Eberhart by night, or vice versa."
- The Manteo episode of summer 1937: Anthony Buttitta (the play's press agent): "That man who came there in 1937 during the first season and tried to sell a fake stone just before one turned up at Emory". Senator Bradford Fearing: an unnamed promoter of "the Coastal Highway, Maine to Miami" had years earlier "proposed a scheme for creating a bogus stone relic of the Lost Colony to get newspaper headlines ... 'We'll cut down an old oak, carve CROATOAN on it ...' He said he could get men who could prepare the inscription, carve the rock, do the whole job." That man "left Norfolk several years ago, deeply in debt. But the night Paul Green's play opened he was in the audience, boldly cranking a fullsized motion-picture camera ... About that time a stranger appeared and offered to sell a stone relic of Virginia Dare. He carried it in a suitcase. Several could identify him. Were these incidents connected with the stone of the 'tourist,' Hammond? ... When Professor Pearce mailed his article, December 13, 1940, he had been aware, certainly since October, that a stone had been offered for sale in Manteo. I got his word for it that he never personally troubled to investigate." (Fullam 2021 identifies this promoter as "'Captain' Jack DeLysle" — see §4; a search-engine snippet of an HMDB marker lists a "Jack DeLysle from Norfolk, Virginia" among the eight men who chose the Waterside Theatre site in May 1931 — UNVERIFIED, page blocked.)
- Sparkes's own summary of the tests on the first stone: "It was a piece of rough veined quartz weighing twenty-one pounds four ounces. The inscriptions could reasonably have been made with tools possessed by the colonists. The language was competently declared to be Elizabethan in character, spelling and idiom. Botanists said leaf mold in the grooves had been there 'a long time.' The Smithsonian Institution said the rock showed 'no evidence of fraud.' That was all they could honestly say." (This paragraph is Sparkes condensing Pearce's manuscript.)

### 2.3 Hammond's own written description of the site (via Robert White 1991 and the Lost Colony Center)

The Lost Colony Center paper (Willard/McMullan, c. 2015, VERIFIED, citing White 1991 p. 261 for the clues): "Hammond wrote that he found the stone three or four miles north of Edenton, about a half mile from the road, by a small bridge over water. He said he picked up the stone and carried it a short distance to the Chowan River to wash it. He described the place as marshy with a small island, and he could see a sunken barge nearby in the river." Their list of published clues: "1. ... approximately four miles north of Edenton, NC. 2. Hammond had just crossed a small bridge over a creek when he pulled over to gather hickory nuts and stretch his legs. 3. ... a swampy place. 4. ... walked a short distance to the river ... 5. ... he recognized a. a small sand spit where he washed the stone ... b. a sunken barge ... 6. Photographs of the location were taken, and soil boring samples were obtained. 7. Hammond was traveling on Rt. 17 when the discovery was made." They identify "the Cowpen Neck Road bridge over Rocky Hock Creek" and admit: "That he was on Highway 17 when he found the stone cannot be reconciled with the other reported evidence".

### 2.5 The search for the burial site and the "rock with names"

Sparkes (VERIFIED-REPRINT): after publication, "Capt. J. P. Wiggins, a former mayor of Edenton ... remembered seeing, upon a knoll, a moss covered stone ... Professor Pearce went to Edenton on February 5, 1938, and with Wiggins searched in the swamp. The second day they found the rock. An inch deep with moss, it weighed about two hundred pounds. Moss was carefully removed but no carving was found." Excavations followed in June and August 1938 ("Negro helpers labored five days without uncovering anything significant. Old residents told the Pearces that in their youth they had seen the mast of a ship above the swamp trees") and a fifth trip in March 1939, "a further waste of time. The Pearces had spent about $500 of their own money ... Now they offered a $500 reward to anyone finding the stone and bringing authenticating evidence." Two North Carolinians (T. E. Chappell of Tyner; Tom Shallington of Tyrrell County) claimed finds that were never examined (Wikipedia citing La Vere pp. 53–59, UNVERIFIED). The "rock with names" was instead "found" by Eberhardt in South Carolina in 1939 (§3). Modern searches: Willard/McMullan's Rocky Hock Creek site (2015): "Attempts to identify 16th-century artifacts at the test site have thus far been unsuccessful. Many English artifacts have been found, but they are from a later date." (LCC paper, VERIFIED; Daily Advance 2015, VERIFIED; Colavito 2015: "they admit that the pottery wasn't old enough".) No grave, no named rock, no Elizabethan object has ever been found at any proposed find-spot.

### 2.6 Attempts to trace Hammond after 1941

- Sparkes "continued to investigate Louis E. Hammond, with little success" (Wikipedia citing Lawler 2018 pp. 251–252 and La Vere p. 174 — UNVERIFIED).
- 2016: a researcher named Joe McDonald announced in the History Blog comments "a comprehensive search for heirs and relatives of Louis E. Hammond" (VERIFIED as a blog comment; no published result found).
- Genealogical leads by blog commenters (both UNVERIFIED and mutually incompatible): (a) a "Lewis Albert Hammond" b. 1882 Ludington, Michigan, Army 1902–08, married in Santa Clara 1909 as "Lewis Elbert Hammond", living in Eureka CA in 1930–42, d. Memphis 1956 (NC Miscellany 2009 comment by "Bob Foster"); (b) a Louis E. Hammond b. 1890/91 Wisconsin, later of Long Beach CA (History Blog 2016 comment). Neither has been connected to Alameda P.O. ## 3. The later stones (1939–41), the Brenau committee of October 1940, and Sparkes's exposé

### 3.1 Sequence of "finds" (Sparkes 1941, VERIFIED-REPRINT, unless noted)

- May 1939 ("seventeen months after the tourist Hammond's appearance"): "William Eberhart, a carpenter and stonemason in the Atlanta area, brought them a rock. He said he had found it about twelve miles south of Greenville, South Carolina, near the Saluda River and the town of Pelzer. He had heard they were offering money for old stones bearing inscriptions." Its date 1589 did not fit; "They told Eberhart the stone they sought was in North Carolina; his was probably a Spanish grave marker." He returned with two more dated 1589, then "A week later Eberhart was back and he had a fourth stone. On the back of this were names ... The date, 1591, was readable. The first had said seventeen persons had been massacred, their names carved on a rock. Professor Pearce counted. There were seventeen names." Text: "Heyr laeth Ananias & Virginia Father Salvage mvrther Al save seaven names written heyr mai God hab mercye Eleanor Dare 1591"; edge: "Father wee goe sw"; fifteen surnames on the back. Wikipedia (citing La Vere pp. 65–68, UNVERIFIED): the fit came after "the Pearces showed him Hammond's stone, and explained what writing they expected to find".
- Thirteen South Carolina stones in all (nos. 2–14); the Pearces bought the hill for $800; the $500 reward was converted into "$100 cash and a half interest in the hill"; on 17 September 1940 "Eberhart surrendered his interest in the sixteen acres, and the Pearces gave him $1400 of Brenau College funds."
- Stone 15: I. A. Turner of Atlanta, "late August, 1939", said to have been found in March 1939 in Hall County, Georgia; text includes "Wee pvtt moche clew bye wage" (Sparkes's reading) — the stone on which Sparkes found the acrostic "FAKE".
- Nine Habersham County stones (16–24) from Eberhardt, August 1939, including "Father wee ben heyr 5 yeeres in primaeval splendovr Eleanor Dare 1592" and "Father day by day some amang vs endeavovr tow Reconnoittre For signe of yov".
- Fulton County stones (25–31, then 32–45) in August–September 1940, most "within four miles of his bed"; no. 36 from William Bruce; no. 37 Eberhardt-and-Turner; no. 46 the Jett "mill stone" in two halves; no. 47 the cave-ledge inscription "Eleanor Dare heyr sithence 1593"; no. 48 (December 1940) "John White manye prisoner fourtie mylles nw. Griffen Jones & Agnes Dare 1603" (Wikipedia citing La Vere, UNVERIFIED for no. 48's text).
- Money (Sparkes): "The revealed investment of Brenau funds in this enterprise is $1000 to Hammond; $1600 and more to Eberhart; $50 to Bruce, $25 to Turner; $800 for the hill." Pearce had first told him "about $500". NCpedia: about $2,000 to Eberhardt in all.

### 3.2 The Brenau conference, 19–20 October 1940, and the committee's statement

- Attendance and outcome as Sparkes reported it (VERIFIED-REPRINT): "Last fall thirty-four scholars, headed by Dr. Samuel E. Morison, of Harvard, president of the American Antiquarian Society, journeyed to Brenau and after two days' study pronounced that 'the preponderance of evidence points to the authenticity of the stones.'" A five-man committee headed by Morison was "authorized by the thirty-four who met at Brenau to issue a statement and study further the problems posed by the stones" (Sparkes quoting an unnamed committee member: "This matter will be determined finally ... only on the basis of internal evidence, something in the inscriptions, or the stones themselves.").
- The qualifications: the LCC paper (VERIFIED) says the committee "made specific requests for a number of additional studies"; Wikipedia (citing La Vere p. 118): the statement "supported the authenticity of the Dare Stones, but cautioned that the investigation was still ongoing" (UNVERIFIED as to the wording). I did not find the full text of the statement anywhere online; only the "preponderance of evidence points to the authenticity of the stones" clause is quoted (by Sparkes 1941, NatGeo 2018, Brown's Guide, History Blog). The date "October 21, 1940" for the report appears in Sparkes ("writing De Mille October 25, 1940 four days after the conference had reported").
- Morison's private letter to the Post (Sparkes, VERIFIED-REPRINT): "I personally went to Gainesville to investigate the Eleanor Dare stones and believe them to be genuine, as did the other professors present. A geologist pronounced the inscriptions to be over 40 years old (they can't tell whether they are 41 or 401), and some of them have been knocking around farms of perfectly honest Georgians for the last fifty years, but were only produced when the story got around." And later to Sparkes: "I believe them [the stones] to be O.K. The chief obstacles are three words: trale, primeval, reconnoitre."
- Morison's later disavowal, in *The European Discovery of America: The Northern Voyages* (1971), p. 684 (as quoted by Wikipedia; UNVERIFIED — the book is in copyright and its archive.org copies are lending-only): "The committee, as politely as possible, declared the stones to be fake, largely on the use of two words, 'trail' and 'reconnoitre' which were not in the English language for a century or more after 1590." Robert White 1991 reads the same passage the other way: "Thirty years later, Samuel Morison recalled vividly that the stones were impressive because the inscriptions and backgrounds were equally weathered." (VERIFIED-REPRINT of White's ch. 3.) Both cannot be a fair summary; the 1971 text should be read in full.
- The Smithsonian: Sparkes has Pearce's manuscript saying "The Smithsonian Institution said the rock showed 'no evidence of fraud.'" The later "Smithsonian-commissioned team of historians" phrasing (Brenau Window 2007; History Blog 2016; WaPo 2018) conflates this with the 1940 conference; I found no primary evidence that the Smithsonian commissioned the Morison group.

### 3.3 What Sparkes established — separated by stone

Boyden Sparkes, "Writ on Rocke: Has America's First Murder Mystery Been Solved?", *Saturday Evening Post*, 26 April 1941 (pp. 9–11, 118, 120–122; Wikipedia adds 124–126, 128). Read in the Angelfire reprint (VERIFIED-REPRINT). The archive.org scan of the issue is listed but "not available".

About the later stones (Eberhardt, Turner, Bruce, Jett):
1. Provenance/association: "Eberhart acknowledged that for more than ten years he had known Turner and Bruce. They can get together any half hour." Turner "met Eberhart about 1930"; Jett was Turner's boyhood neighbour; "except for the two stones remembered by a man named Tom Jett ... no living soul is known who ever saw or heard of such stones before they were produced by Hammond, Eberhart and Turner, and Bruce." Eberhardt "always found his stones when nobody was looking"; "finally he was making all his finds within four miles of his bed!" The hill in South Carolina was a cultivated cotton patch whose family "Never saw anything like 'em."
2. Eberhardt's background: three years' schooling; hauler of stones; jail for drunken driving; the "product" he peddled in South Carolina "was actually moonshine whisky"; an Atlanta antique dealer had paid him "$271.50 for an assortment of 'Indian relics'" that "State-employed geologists ... believed ... to have been recently chiseled" and which the dealer "thinks ... were treated with a blowtorch after carving."
3. Fresh cutting: Lester's report of 26 July 1939 on Stone No. 25: the grooves of "sithence 1593" were "the freshest in the entire stone"; "The lower letters ... give the impression of having been cut within the past few days or weeks when compared with the letters on the upper part"; letters "poorly incised where a lichenous stain showed at places where grooves would have been"; "I am forced to believe less in the authenticity of this stone than in any ... It makes me believe it has been doctored". Lester: "You could scratch them with your fingernail. You could do the job, using the head of a ten penny nail as a cutting tool." Bruce's stone "was 'clean' when delivered; somebody had scraped the grooves of the inscriptions, as had been done with all the stones."
4. Vocabulary: "primeval" (OED earliest 1653), "reconnoitre" (1707), "trale" for trail (in 1590 "used to denote the scent of a quarry rather than a pathway"); confirmed by Morris Tilley (Michigan, Early Modern English Dictionary files). Tannenbaum: "Isn't it extraordinary to find 'primeval' and 'reconnoitre' when they do not appear in Shakespeare ...?"
5. Consistency of spelling across "the 712 words on the stones": "laeth" 9 times, "Eleanor" 26, "Salvage" 18, "hither" 5, "Englande" 2, etc. Tannenbaum: "No Elizabethan was ever so consistent in spelling."
6. Letterforms: Tannenbaum on "photographs of the stones": "There isn't a Gothic letter here. And this settles the whole matter! ... Every letter on these stones is a Roman letter." (Sparkes applies this to all the stones; White 1991 replies that White's 1590 narrative describes "faire Romane letters" and "fayre Capitall letters" on the Roanoke trees — VERIFIED in Pearce 1938's quotation of Hakluyt.)
7. The acrostic: on the back of Turner's Stone No. 15 Sparkes saw "Emory", "Atlanta, Ge", "shed", "Pearce", and, held sideways, "F A K E". ("Is it an acrostic? I don't know. But I am sure Eleanor Dare had nothing to do with it.")
8. The Jett stone (No. 46): identified through jail bars; the two halves "fit as neatly as a freshly broken teacup" after decades apart; the "8's" that turned out to be g's.
9. Motive/promotion: Pearce had written to Cecil B. De Mille on 25 October 1940; Brenau staged Maude Fiske LaFleur's pageant *This Heritage* in summer 1940 "rivaling the play at Roanoke"; Georgia's World's Fair exhibit showed replicas; Pearce had lectured "about fifty times".

About the FIRST stone, specifically, Sparkes established only:
- (i) that Hammond was untraceable (Pinkertons; no car, no wife seen; the P.O. box; Dove); (ii) that Hammond had scrubbed the grooves and pencilled the letters; (iii) that a stranger had tried to sell "a stone relic of Virginia Dare" at Manteo in summer 1937 and that a promoter had earlier proposed manufacturing exactly such a relic; (iv) that Hammond later reappeared as "L. E. Martin" trying to bribe Turner into producing a "Yahoo" stone (which cuts both ways: it shows Hammond believed the later stones were procurable to order, and it shows him behaving like a confidence man); (v) that the "couldn't be duplicated" claim was untrue (Marietta workmen; Lester's sandblasting). Sparkes explicitly notes the logical link Pearce made — the first stone's "seven of twenty-four" produced Eberhardt's seventeen names — and says "It seemed to me it should be equally true that if the first was a fraud, all were fraudulent. My hasty conclusion got me nowhere with a professor I consulted." The linguistic and geological findings (items 3–8 above) concern Eberhardt's and Turner's stones, not Hammond's; none of Tannenbaum's specific anachronisms occurs on the first stone; the spelling-consistency count is over all 712 words. Tannenbaum's "Roman letters" objection is the one criticism that applies to the first stone too (its letters are Roman/mixed-case, not secretary hand).

We Dare Anything."; per Brown's Guide/Jim Miles quoting Pearce's 1941 press statement (VERIFIED as reported): "Pearce and Dare Historical Hoax. ## 5. The arguments, both sides

### 5.1 Critical arguments against the first stone, with attributions

**(a) Language — anachronisms and oddities**

1. *Pearce's own admissions (Pearce 1938, VERIFIED).* Five forms for which he "found no precedent": misarie, beine, affrite, bleeve, spirts. "A woman's signature with three initials, in the period, presents more difficulty ... the writer has not found an instance where three occurred." His defence of the Latin cross and Arabic numerals cites Dingley's *History of Marble* — a compilation "made in the reign of Charles II" (n. 41), i.e. evidence from the 1670s–80s offered for 1591. (The numerals point itself is not controversial: Hakluyt's printed text of White has "An. 1587", and Champion in 2018 saw nothing anachronistic in numerals or Roman lettering — Wikipedia's summary of Lawler, UNVERIFIED wording.)
2. *Tannenbaum 1941 (Sparkes, VERIFIED-REPRINT):* "There isn't a Gothic letter here ... Every letter on these stones is a Roman letter." This is the one 1941 linguistic objection that reaches the first stone (its letters are Roman capitals with some minuscule forms, see §1.2). It is a weak objection for a carved stone: John White himself reported "faire Romane letters C R O" and "fayre Capitall letters" reading CROATOAN cut by the colonists on trees (Hakluyt, VERIFIED in `hakluyt_v8.txt`), and inscriptions in stone were habitually cut in capitals. Wilson and Champion (2018) in effect reject it.
3. *Spelling consistency (Tannenbaum via Sparkes, VERIFIED-REPRINT):* "No Elizabethan was ever so consistent in spelling." Tannenbaum's count is over the 712 words of all the stones. Within the first stone alone the sample is small, but it is also internally consistent: yov ×4, wee ×3, al ×4, salvage(s) ×3, ther ×2, smal ×2, fovre ×2, misarie ×2, yeere ×2, tow ×2 — no word is spelled two ways. Period writers commonly vary spelling even within a page; this is a mild, not a decisive, indicator.
4. *Specific forms (my corpus check, §6):* "yov" (final v) contradicts the period convention by which v is written initially and u medially/finally regardless of sound — in Arber's u/v-preserving Smith: vs 1,117, vnto 115, vp 255, vpon 528 (initial v) against haue 1,478, loue 81, giue 156, you 955, yov 0. "Mine childe": in c. 3.7 MB of period text the determiner "mine" never precedes a consonant-initial noun (Hakluyt vol. 8: "mine" + vowel/h 20, "my" + consonant 223; Smith: 16 vs 862; the apparent counter-examples are the noun "mine" or the absolute pronoun). "Via" for Virginia: Champion "couldn't locate another instance from the era" (VERIFIED as quoted by Mental Floss); as an English preposition "via" is, from memory, not recorded before the late 18th century (UNVERIFIED — OED not consulted). "EWD": Pearce 1938, Wolfe 2018 ("not a standard way to sign one's name in that era", VERIFIED) and MacCulloch 2018. "Affrite", "spirts", "beine", "bleeve", "misarie", "suddiane", "sickenes", "anye", "mesage/message with -ge": unattested in the corpora (which does not prove non-existence; EEBO was not available to me).
5. *Register (my own judgement, so marked).* The text's "Elizabethan" quality is produced almost entirely by (i) added final -e (Englande, Deade, greate, anye, awaye, alsoe, misarie), (ii) doubled final consonants (nott, putt, rann, shipp, smal/hil with single letters going the other way), and (iii) u/v and i/j swaps — the three devices a 20th-century imitator reaches for. Genuine period variation is not of this uniform kind; MacCulloch's "Dick Van Dyke" verdict is a judgement on exactly this register. Against this: several stone words are precisely the forms found in the sources (salvage, tow, cam, deade, nott, writ, alsoe, seaven, myles, murther, plentie, shew, twentie, halfe, slaine, burie, neere, soone, yeere, "goe for England", "went hence to heauen") — see §6 — which shows that whoever wrote it had read period texts closely, whether in 1591 or in 1937.
6. *Content stays inside the printed record (my own observation).* Every checkable statement on the stone — removal ~50 miles inland to the Chowan, a ship that came and went, Indian fear of revenge, priests inciting a massacre, seven survivors, burial "on a small hill" — is contained in Hakluyt (White 1587/1590), Strachey (Major ed. 1849), Smith (Arber 1884), the Gates instructions (Kingsbury 1933) and Weeks 1891, all in print before 1937; Pearce's seven "checks" (§5.2) are checks against precisely these books. Nothing on the stone is checkable against anything *outside* them (Pearce's own list of "questions not cleared up": the survivors' names, why CROATOAN was carved, "Why did not Eleanor Dare state her exact whereabouts"). A hoax is expected to stay inside the record; a genuine message would be expected, at least somewhere, to leave it.

**(b) Location — "60 miles west of Roanoke Island", the Chowan**

- For: Pearce 1938 checks (4)–(6): White's "prepared to remove from Roanoak 50 miles into the maine"; Lane's 1586 praise of "the Chaonists ... more faithfull in their promises"; Strachey and the Gates instructions placing survivors up the Chowan ("where the River Choanowki falleth into the sea of Rawnocke"). NatGeo 2018: "about 50 miles inland from Roanoke Island, matching White's passing mention" (VERIFIED). Modern archaeology (Site X / Site Y on Salmon Creek, Bertie County, 2012–) has since put at least some colonists on the western Albemarle, which La Vere and Schrader treat as corroboration (Powell 2016; WaPo 2018, VERIFIED).
- Against: (i) the same three texts were available to, and are the obvious reading of, anyone planning a "Lost Colony" relic in 1937; a hoaxer who read Hakluyt would put the stone exactly there. (ii) Lester: "There are no exposures of rock of any kind in the region, nor ... within a distance of several miles of the spot. It is thought that the discovered rock was transported from some other place" (Pearce n. 4, VERIFIED) — the vein quartz came from the Piedmont/Appalachians, i.e. it was carried to the coastal plain by somebody. (iii) The find-spot was never established: "rains had obliterated all trace"; Hammond produced "a crude map scrawled on a piece of paper bag", said the spot was "a quarter of a mile away", and after "wandering a mile and a half in the swamp the professors lost patience" (Sparkes, VERIFIED-REPRINT); his written description places him on Route 17, which the Lost Colony Center admits "cannot be reconciled with the other reported evidence" (VERIFIED). (iv) Nothing 16th-century has been found at any candidate spot (2015 test pits: later English ceramics only). (v) The distances quoted vary from 50 to 80 miles depending on the author (§2.4).

**(c) Timing — the 350th anniversary summer**

- NCpedia (VERIFIED): "in January [1937], North Carolina playwright Paul Green had been commissioned to dramatize the story"; "the state, and town of Manteo were preparing for a visit from then President Franklin Roosevelt on August 18 to mark the anniversary. On the same day, the U.S. Postal Service also released a 5-cent Virginia Dare commemorative stamp." Wikipedia (Roanoke Colony article, VERIFIED as Wikipedia text): Roosevelt "attended the play on August 18, 1937". NCpedia (VERIFIED): "Green's play opened on July 4, and Roosevelt made his historic visit a little more than a month later. And in November that year, the Chowan River Stone appeared."
- The stone "surfaced" within weeks of this publicity: "mid-September, 1937" (Pearce's MS via Sparkes) or "August 1937" (La Vere per Wikipedia); it reached Emory in early November. Lawler (VERIFIED as quoted by Mental Floss): "None of the Emory staff recorded whether Hammond said he went to the play or knew about the president's visit to celebrate Virginia Dare's birthday, though it was national news at the time."
- Sparkes (VERIFIED-REPRINT): in the same first season "a stranger appeared and offered to sell a stone relic of Virginia Dare. He carried it in a suitcase"; and a Coastal Highway promoter had years earlier "proposed a scheme for creating a bogus stone relic of the Lost Colony to get newspaper headlines ... He said he could get men who could prepare the inscription, carve the rock, do the whole job." Fullam 2021 names this promoter "'Captain' Jack DeLysle" and treats him as a perpetrator (ToC VERIFIED; argument not read). Whether the suitcase stone and Hammond's stone are the same object is unproven; Pearce "never personally troubled to investigate" (Sparkes).
- Fair caveat: an anniversary also motivates genuine searching — Hammond said he was out gathering hickory nuts, not hunting relics, and a stone lying in a swamp is as likely to be noticed in 1937 as in any other year. Timing is suggestive, not probative.

**(d) The finder's story**

Sparkes 1941 (VERIFIED-REPRINT) supplies all of it: Emory staff "suspicious ... from the beginning"; President Cox: "It's a fraud"; "None ever saw Hammond's car. None ever saw his wife"; an Emory treasurer "tried to shadow the man one night, but was eluded"; a fingerprint attempt failed; the Pinkertons "were unable to find anyone who had ever known him"; his only reference, Dove, "said he only knew Hammond to be married, about forty; that he had some kind of a pension"; Hammond proposed exhibiting the stone "at twenty-five cents a head"; he sold it for $1,000 to a P.O. box; he reappeared as "L. E. Martin" offering Turner $30,000 "to find a rock with 'Yahoo' on it", and told Pearce the later "rocks ain't so" while offering "to prove it by getting a stone bearing any word Pearce asked for". He had scrubbed the grooves with a steel brush and pencilled the letters. None of this proves he carved the stone; all of it is conduct one expects from a confidence man and not from a tourist with hickory nuts. Eighty-nine years on, no researcher has produced a documented life for "L. E. Hammond" (§2.6); Fullam 2021 claims to, and his Appendix A is the place to look.

**(e) Physical evidence**

- Against (or, more exactly, absent): the grooves were contaminated at the outset (steel brush, indelible pencil), then repeatedly floured, chalked, "scoured, cleaned and pressure washed" (Loubser 2011, VERIFIED); Stephenson 1983 "could devise no ... examination that would reveal anything useful" (UNVERIFIED wording); the "couldn't be duplicated" claim was false — Marietta workmen "said they could reproduce it with hand tools", Lester "himself had carved an inscription on quartz, using the sandblasting method", and Pearce conceded "Well, that part's not accurate" (Sparkes, VERIFIED-REPRINT). Sparkes's New York stonecutter on ageing: "it would be easy enough to 'age' the surface — tumble it in a barrel. Acids. Wrap the stone with wet sacking sprinkled with iron filings. Any number of ways." Note that the one mineralogical observation in Pearce's paper is a "limonite ... stain around and between many of the anhedra" — the iron-oxide staining that the wet-sacking-and-filings method would produce, and that also forms naturally; nobody has tested which. No laboratory report on the 2016 slice has been published; the interior/exterior colour comparison is qualitative and was made by the stone's owner for television (§4.2).
- For: Lester's 1937–38 observations on groove profile (rounded, uniformly sloping walls, gaping, feathered ends — i.e. no fresh conchoidal fracture); botanists' "leaf mold ... 'a long time'" and the Smithsonian's "no evidence of fraud" (both as summarised by Sparkes from Pearce's MS — the reports themselves are not published); the 1940 chemical weathering test "favorable" (reported for the collection as a whole); Wolter 2013 (weathering in grooves, no controls); History 2015 ("no evidence of drilling", "iron chisel"); the 2016 slice: interior "bright white", exterior and carvings "much darker", with Champion's judgement that "it takes a great deal of time for that whiteness to fade" and that chemical masking "particularly in the 1930s — would have been difficult" (NatGeo, VERIFIED). This last is the strongest single pro-authenticity datum, and it is also the one most exposed to the contamination history (iron-stained water, pencil graphite and decades of handling all darken quartz surfaces) and to the absence of any measurement.

**(f) The later fakes**

- Sparkes's inference (VERIFIED-REPRINT): "if the first was a fraud, all were fraudulent" — and, conversely, the exposure of the 47 poisons the first. The Brenau committee's 1940 statement was about "the stones" collectively; the collective judgement collapsed in 1941 and took the first stone's reputation with it.
- The independence argument (Southerland 1977; Brenau's page "regarded by most authorities as the only Dare Stone"; La Vere; Schrader; Lawler): different rock (vein quartz vs. soft soapstone/schist "you could scratch ... with your fingernail"), different geography (Chowan vs. Georgia/South Carolina), better cutting ("carved more carefully and with an iron chisel"), no anachronistic vocabulary (primeval, reconnoitre, trale are all on Eberhardt/Turner stones), and no proven link between Hammond and Eberhardt's circle.
- What does connect them: the first stone wrote the script for the rest ("seven of twenty-four" → seventeen names; "names writ al ther on rocke" → the roster stone; "salvage shew this unto yov ... greate plentie presents" → the reward expectation), the Pearces paid for stones from 1938 on, and Hammond himself came back to Georgia as "L. E. Martin" to interact with the Georgia finders. The extortion stone of 1941 ("Pearce and Dare Historical Hoaxes. We Dare Anything.") shows that by then the Pearces were a known mark; the first stone is what made them one.

### 5.2 Arguments for authenticity, stated fairly

- *Pearce 1938 (VERIFIED):* the message "checks" in seven particulars with White, Lane, Strachey and Smith (§5.1(b) and the JSH text); "the prevailing opinion is that the stone could have been chiseled with tools ... possessed by the Roanoke colonists"; the language presents no "serious difficulty", period precedents existing "for all cases except five"; Lester's groove description; the Elizabethan long s and the brevigraphs (wth, yt) are period manuscript habits.
- *Morison 1940 (letters quoted by Sparkes, VERIFIED-REPRINT):* "I personally went to Gainesville to investigate the Eleanor Dare stones and believe them to be genuine, as did the other professors present. A geologist pronounced the inscriptions to be over 40 years old (they can't tell whether they are 41 or 401)"; and the committee's "the preponderance of evidence points to the authenticity of the stones" (see §3.2 for the qualifications).
- *Botany and the Smithsonian (Pearce MS via Sparkes):* leaf mould in the grooves "a long time"; "no evidence of fraud".
- *Southerland 1977/1987 (Brenau Window 2007, VERIFIED):* "all but that first stone probably are fakes", later "50-50".
- *Robert W. White 1991 (VERIFIED-REPRINT of ch. 3):* the Post's exposé was commercially motivated ("acting on behalf of the Roanoke tourist attraction"); Tannenbaum's Roman-letter point is answered by White's own "faire Romane letters".
- *La Vere 2009/2010 (Powell, VERIFIED):* "a closer look shows it might well be what it purports to be ... It tells a credible story that coincides with the sources"; an Elizabethan scholar "came away amazed how it all fit Elizabethan English"; a small-time produce dealer is an implausible sole author of the text.
- *Wolter 2013; History 2015 (Colavito, VERIFIED as reported):* weathering inside the grooves; no drilling; iron chisel.
- *Schrader 2016–18 (WSJ, NatGeo, WaPo, VERIFIED):* the slice — bright white interior against dark exterior and carvings, "a good choice for a Roanoke colonist but a poor one for a modern forger"; not ship's ballast; a possible southern-Virginia source; Champion's concurrence on colour and on the difficulty of chemical masking in the 1930s.
- *Epigraphers 2018 (NatGeo, VERIFIED):* Wolfe: "There is nothing that jumps out as a forgery"; Wilson: "There's nothing in the inscription that couldn't be of its purported date"; Champion: consistent with the church inscriptions he studies. Quarmby 2015: the superscript-e "ye" distinction is one "only a 'magnificent' hoaxer would know" (Colavito's reply: reprints of the 1890s–1920s routinely reproduce it).
- *Structural:* the first stone is the only one with a credible geology, a coastal-plain find-site consistent with the sources, and no anachronistic word; the fakes of 1939–41 were made to *follow* it, which is what one would expect whether it was genuine or merely earlier.

### 5.3 What the two sides are really disagreeing about

The pro side rests on (i) consistency with the sources, (ii) the absence of a demonstrated anachronism on this stone, and (iii) one qualitative colour observation. The con side rests on (i) the impossibility of the finder, (ii) the timing and the documented intent to fake exactly such a relic that summer, (iii) the register of the language and a handful of un-Elizabethan forms (yov, mine childe, Via, EWD), and (iv) the fact that nothing on the stone leaves the printed record. Neither side has a controlled physical test. That asymmetry matters: the pro side's evidence is of a kind a competent hoaxer would produce deliberately (consistency with Hakluyt and Strachey is the hoaxer's brief, not a by-product), whereas the con side's evidence is of a kind a genuine artefact would not produce accidentally (a genuine stone does not need an untraceable finder or a promoter proposing to fake one).

**Summary of the 23 words in question:** common — salvage, warre, murther, seaven, myles, plentie, shew, hence, hither, message (Pearce's reading), suddaine (the popular reading; the stone has *suddiane*); attested but rarer — sickenes (via *sickenesse*), awaye, faine, alsoe, Englande; unattested — bleeve, misarie, beine, mesage, spirts, anye; neutral — Govr; contrary to convention — yov. Outside that list, "mine childe", "Via", "EWD", "affrite", "easte" and "to slaine" are the forms that most look like modern imitation, while "salvage", "goe for England", "yt", "went hence ... heaven", "faine" (= feign) and "seaven/myles/plentie/shew" are exactly what a reader of Hakluyt, Strachey and Smith would reproduce.

**Linguistic verdict.** The vocabulary is period-plausible and in several places source-exact, which is consistent either with a genuine text or with an author who had read Hakluyt, Strachey and Smith with care — and those were the very books Pearce used to "check" it. The unattested forms cluster in the add-an-e / drop-a-letter type. Two usages — "yov" and "mine childe" — run against habits a 1590s writer followed without thinking and a 1930s imitator would not know; "Via" and "EWD" have no period parallel. Nothing here is a *proven* anachronism of the "primeval/reconnoitre" kind that sank the later stones; the linguistic case against the first stone is a matter of register and convention, and it is a case, not a proof. The proper test (an EEBO-TCP search for yov, "mine" + consonant, anye, misarie, affrite, spirts, beine, suddiane, Via) has never been published and should be run.

### 7.3 Assessment of the first stone

**Probability that the first (Chowan River) stone is a 20th-century fabrication: about 80 % (my range 75–85 %).** I would not go higher because no physical or linguistic anachronism has ever been demonstrated on this stone, the language is in places source-exact rather than merely plausible, and one physical observation (the 2016 colour contrast) points, weakly, the other way. I would not go lower for the reasons below.

*What carries the most weight, in order:*

1. **The finder.** A man with no traceable past, no visible car or wife, a post-office box, a jeweller for a reference, who scrubbed and pencilled the inscription before showing it, who proposed to exhibit it for money, who sold it for $1,000, and who came back under a false name offering $30,000 for a stone with "Yahoo" on it. A genuine artefact can have a bad finder, but this finder behaved throughout like the author of a swindle, and no one in 89 years has produced a document that makes him a real Alameda produce dealer.
2. **The season and the plan.** The stone appeared within weeks of the 350th-anniversary play, the presidential visit and the stamp; in that same season a stranger was hawking "a stone relic of Virginia Dare" from a suitcase at Manteo; and a promoter with a documented scheme "for creating a bogus stone relic of the Lost Colony" was in the audience on opening night. A relic that a known person had proposed to fake, appearing at the moment it would be most valuable, is exactly what that plan predicts.
3. **The text stays inside the printed record.** Everything checkable on the stone is in Hakluyt, Strachey, Smith and the Gates instructions — Pearce's "seven checks" are checks against those books — and nothing checkable lies outside them. Add the register (uniform add-an-e spelling), "yov", "mine childe", "Via" and "EWD", and the message reads like a careful reader of the sources writing the letter Eleanor Dare *ought* to have written.
4. **The physical evidence is unusable rather than favourable.** The grooves were contaminated on day one and repeatedly since; the "couldn't be duplicated" claim was false; no report exists for the 2016 test; every pro-authenticity physical statement (leaf mould, "no evidence of fraud", "41 or 401", dark grooves) is qualitative and uncontrolled. The best that can be said physically is that nothing has been shown to be *wrong*.
5. **On the other side**, the strongest points are the absence of any demonstrated anachronism (a real distinction from the 47 fakes) and the colour contrast of the 2016 slice with Champion's judgement that chemical masking would have been hard in the 1930s. These are real, but the first is what a competent hoaxer produces on purpose and the second has never been measured on a surface that was not scrubbed with a steel brush, pencilled, floured and pressure-washed.

*What would settle it:*

- **Tool-mark and residue analysis of the uncleaned incisions** that Loubser identified in 2011: SEM imaging of groove-floor morphology (chisel percussion vs. abrasive/sandblast texture), and SEM-EDS or LA-ICP-MS for metal traces (a modern alloy signature — Cr, Ni, Mn, V — or carborundum/abrasive residue would end the question; plain iron proves little either way).
- **A quantified weathering comparison**: rind thickness or surface chemistry measured on (a) an uncleaned groove floor, (b) the natural exterior, (c) the 2016 saw cut, with control fragments of vein quartz from the Chowan floodplain and from the Piedmont, so that "dark" becomes a number with an age range. This is the only test that could positively *support* authenticity.
- **An EEBO-TCP corpus test** of yov, "mine" + consonant, anye, misarie, affrite, spirts, beine, suddiane, Via, and of a woman's three-initial signature, published with its method.
- **Archival**: the Emory "Dare stones collection, 1937–1987" (Hammond's correspondence, the Pinkerton and credit-agency reports), Fullam's Appendix A, and California records for L. E. Hammond and Herbert M. Dove. Finding Hammond would do more than any of the above; finding that he did not exist would do almost as much.
- Geochemical provenance (Darby's proposal) would tell us where the quartz came from, which matters less than it seems: a colonist and a hoaxer would both have had to carry it to the coastal plain.

If forced to choose one test, the tool-mark/residue analysis of the uncleaned incisions is the one most likely to return a decisive answer, because a positive result (modern alloy or abrasive) is unanswerable, whereas every other test yields a probability.
