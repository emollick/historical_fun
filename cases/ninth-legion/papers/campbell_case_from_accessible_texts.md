## (b) Campbell's arguments, source by source

### B.1 *Ancient Warfare* 4.5 (2010) 48–53 [FULL] — "The fate of the Ninth. The curious disappearance of Legio VIIII Hispana"

Standfirst (p. 48): "But was it really destroyed in Britain during the reign of Hadrian? Or have we fallen for a myth that should have been laid to rest fifty years ago?"

**p. 48 — The York slab (RIB 665).** Found 7 October 1854; "the last testimony of the presence of the Ninth Legion in Britain. Dated to AD 108"; it "probably celebrated the construction of the gateway, built by the emperor per legionem VIIII Hispanam".

**p. 49 — Horsley, Borghesi, Mommsen.** Horsley (1732) "could find no trace of it. Last mentioned in AD 82 by the historian Tacitus (in Agricola 26.1)... 'it might possibly be broke', he wrote..., 'or incorporated with the legio sexta victrix'". Campbell: "Unfortunately, there was no record of a legion having been 'broke', as happened, for example, in AD 161, when the Parthians, 'completely surrounding an entire Roman legion stationed under Severianus at Elegeia, a place in Armenia, shot it down and annihilated it with its officers' (Dio, Roman History 71.2.1). Horsley did not consider the obvious solution, that the Ninth Legion had been withdrawn from Britain and transferred elsewhere." Borghesi, from the career of L. Burbuleius Ligarianus (CIL X 6006 = ILS 1066, cos. 135, "probably served with the Ninth Hispana towards the end of Trajan's reign, perhaps around AD 115") and the legion's absence from the list of legions (CIL VI 3492 = ILS 2288, "shortly before AD 165"), "proposed that the Ninth Legion had been overwhelmed in a rebellion and had been replaced by the Sixth, a solution that seemed perfectly acceptable to his nineteenth century contemporaries." Mommsen (Römische Geschichte Book 8, 1885) is quoted: "Under Hadrian, there was a terrible catastrophe here, apparently an attack on the fortress at Eburacum and the annihilation of the legion stationed there, the very same Ninth that had fought so unluckily in the Boudican revolt." After RIB 665, Mommsen "announced that the disaster had occurred 'undoubtedly soon after AD 108', adding that 'this was probably not caused by an enemy invasion, but rather by a revolt of the northern allied peoples, particularly the Brigantes'." Also p. 49: the Pontius Laelianus inscription (CIL VI 1497+1549 = ILS 1094+1100), tribune of VI Victrix "cum qua ex Germania in Brittaniam transivit", dated to "the early AD 120s"; "It seems more than coincidental that a new governor, Aulus Platorius Nepos, had arrived in Britain from Lower Germany during the summer of AD 122, so he perhaps brought Pontius Laelianus and the Sixth Legion with him."

**p. 50 — "A British war?"** Mommsen's two literary passages: HA Hadr. 5.2 ("the Britons could not be kept under Roman control") and Fronto, *On the Parthian War* 2 ("Under the rule of your grandfather Hadrian, what a number of soldiers were slain by the Jews, what a number by the Britons"). Campbell: "Hadrian's Jewish war was a major event, proved by archaeology and coin studies. The supposed British war, on the other hand, is more ephemeral." Weber (1907) "decided upon a revolt which had been crushed by AD 119." The coins: "there is only circumstantial evidence for a British war. Some of Hadrian's coins carry the figure of Britannia..., and these have been taken to imply warfare in Britain; specifically warfare during the years AD 117-119. But their evidence is problematic. Major changes in Hadrian's coinage occurred only in AD 119 and 128, but coin expert Harold Mattingly believed that he could differentiate certain coins within this period on stylistic grounds" — Mattingly's Britannia coins "celebrate the restoration of peace in the North after the revolt under Trajan, in which the ninth legion was destroyed". Campbell: "if Mattingly was wrong in his stylistic analysis, the coins could then fall some years later (though still pre-AD 128), and might celebrate a different event in Britain. For example, the emperor's own visit (normally dated to AD 122, but perhaps later)... Yet, to this day, many scholars still assume that Hadrian's coinage proves that a war was won in Britain in AD 119." Ritterling (RE 12, 1925) is quoted at length, ending: "Whether VIIII Hispana had already met its end, or only several years later, around AD 125, remains unknown." Campbell: "As Ritterling astutely realised, there was no guarantee that the transfer of the Sixth Victrix Legion was occasioned by the destruction of the Ninth Hispana Legion. This had simply been an assumption first proposed by Borghesi and followed by Mommsen. But if the Ninth Legion was still in the province, fully operational, then clearly Hadrian had intended temporarily to increase the provincial army to four legions." On T. Pontius Sabinus' *expeditio Brittanica* (ILS 2726): "it should be clear by now that there was never any direct linkage between the disappearance of the Ninth Legion and Hadrian's 'British campaign'. Nor, indeed, was there any compelling evidence to date the campaign to AD 119. This particular link was based on Mattingly's subjective assessment of the coins. So the arrival of these massive reinforcements could equally have been linked with Hadrian's decision to build his Wall in the years following AD 122." CAH 11 (Weber 1936) quoted; Campbell leaves the "British war" to "its own Ancient Warfare debate".

**pp. 50–51 — "Legionary tribunes" (Ritterling's prosopography).** "Ritterling harboured doubts about an early destruction of the Ninth Legion. His reasoning was based on prosopography. He saw that the careers of certain officers seemed to have peaked too late for their service in the Ninth Legion to be restricted to the Trajanic period. The legion had surely survived into the early years of Hadrian's reign at least."
- **L. Aemilius Karus** (CIL VI 1333 = ILS 1077): "This former tribune of the Ninth Legion was governing the praetorian province of Arabia in AD 142, the task of a man in his mid- to late-thirties (...; a recently discovered diploma confirms the date of his Arabian governorship)." Syme's identification with the "Lucius Aemilius" consul of 144; "Ritterling suggested a date 'only after AD 120' for Karus' service in the Ninth Legion, for it ought to have been at roughly the same time as Laelianus' service in the Sixth Legion."
- **L. Novius Crispinus Martialis Saturninus**, consul 150 after commanding III Augusta: "As he was probably born in around AD 105, he was thus a little younger than Aemilius Karus and Pontius Laelianus. Ritterling realised that Crispinus' service in the Ninth Legion 'could not reasonably have fallen before AD 123'. Destruction in a British war of AD 119 was out of the question. Ritterling's only mistake lay in not stressing this logical conclusion. Instead, he simply advised that 'we should reckon on the possibility that a second British revolt broke out towards the middle or in the second half of the AD 120s, to which the legion fell victim.'" Parker (1928) "wrote, with misplaced confidence: 'the Roman legions were unable at first to cope successfully with the [British] revolt, and IX Hispana was destroyed not later than AD 122.'"

**p. 51 — "An ignominious defeat?"** "A British war was universally believed to have occurred in AD 119, despite both the absence of direct evidence and the fragility of the circumstantial evidence. And the Ninth Legion was universally believed to have been destroyed in that war, despite Ritterling's warning." Eric Birley (1948) "proposed two possible scenarios. Either the Ninth Legion had been transferred from Britain under Trajan in connection with the Parthian war... Or else the Sixth Legion was brought over to Britain, not to replace the Ninth Legion, but to supplement the garrison during the building of Hadrian's Wall. (In this case, the Ninth Legion might have been transferred from Britain at a later date, perhaps in connection with Hadrian's Jewish War, which certainly saw other legionary transfers.)" Richmond (Roman Britain 1955) quoted ("That the legion was cashiered, there is no doubt... But the unit was not annihilated. Some of its officers at least survived..."); Campbell: "The extent to which theory had become fact in his account is astonishing. And thus, the carefully weighed caution of Ritterling and the perceptive theories of Birley were swept aside by the pre-eminent Roman military scholar of the day." "Richmond's fantastical theory". Sutcliff's foreword quoted; her source "was probably the Cambridge Ancient History".

**pp. 51–52 — Nijmegen.** 1959: the Hunerberg fortress excavation "unearthed a roofing tile which bore the ownership stamp of the Ninth Legion. This was not an isolated find. Earlier excavations at the legionary pottery and tile-works, located at De Holdeurn less than 5 km from the fortress, had turned up the stamped rim of a mortarium... stamped LGVIIIIHIS, clearly an abbreviation for l(e)g(io) VIIII His(pana)." X Gemina left for the Dacian wars ("mentioned as still operating in Lower Germany in ca. AD 101/102"). "The next most common tile-stamp from Nijmegen reads VEX BRIT... Most scholars now follow Bogaers in assuming that, during the early years of the second century, the vacant Hunerberg fortress was occupied by mixed troops detached from the garrison of Britain. However, Bogaers realised that such a vexillation would be unlikely to have stamped their products with the name of the Ninth Legion. His solution was to suggest that the Ninth Legion took up residence in the Hunerberg fortress after the vexillation had returned to Britain, and continued the production of ceramics at the Holdeurn tilery. Unfortunately, no firm date could be applied to these events, except that they were broadly 'early second century'." Brian Hartley: York "under reduced occupancy in the early second century, whereas stamped tiles of the Ninth Legion were being produced at Scalesceugh, around 5 km south of Carlisle." The spelling argument: "Intriguingly, stamped tiles of the Ninth legion discovered in York were invariably marked LEG IX HISP, whereas those from Carlisle and Scalesceugh (and indeed from Nijmegen) were marked LEG VIIII HISP. It seemed possible to Bogaers that the legion had brought its distinctive Scalesceugh tile-stamps over to the Holdeurn tilery near Nijmegen, to continue production there." The Ewijk pendant: "found some 10 km west of the Nijmegen camp, near the site of a Roman villa; its inscription, LEG IX HISP, suggests that its owner had learned the 'York' version of the legion's name, rather than the 'Carlisle/Nijmegen' version."

**p. 52 — The Aachen altar (AE 1968, 323).** "Further evidence supported the case that the Ninth Legion was quartered in Lower Germany. An altar to Apollo, discovered near the Roman spa of Aquae Granni (Aachen, Germany), was set up by Lucius Licinius Macer [sic — the article prints 'Licinius'; the man is L. Latinius Macer], primus pilus ('chief centurion') of the Ninth Legion, who had been promoted to the highly prestigious position of praefectus castrorum ('prefect of the camp', AE 1968, 323). Eric Birley believed that 'the praefectus castrorum could not have been serving with a mere vexillation', but in theory he could have been commanding one. Of course, only the presence of the aquilifer would be a cast-iron guarantee that the entire legion was there, but it would have been odd for the primus pilus to receive a promotion while absent in charge of a vexillation."

**p. 52 — Florentinus and Numisius Iunior.** "Lucius Aninius Sextius Florentinus was known to have moved from the post of legatus legionis VIIII Hispanae... to the proconsulship of Gallia Narbonensis, and finally to the governorship of Arabia, but the dating had always been uncertain. Then, in the 1960s, a newly discovered papyrus finally showed that his governorship fell around the year AD 127. Such a man ought to have held his legionary command no more than five years earlier." "And the German classicist Werner Eck lent his considerable authority to the dating of another legionary tribune, Quintus Numisius Iunior, to around AD 140. The discovery that this man was consul in February AD 161 should have closed the issue, once and for all. It is unthinkable that his glowing career (CIL XI 5670) could have begun as long ago as AD 119, for this would mean that he only achieved the consulship at the age of sixty!" "And so, a new chapter in the Ninth Legion's history was taking shape during the 1970s. It seemed that, if the legion was already forming a stop-gap in Lower Germany, it might be called upon to fulfil other duties further along the frontier. Trajan's Parthian War had caused some dislocation of units, and Hadrian's Jewish War would do the same."

**p. 53 — Asclepiades; Watson; "Fresh doubts"; conclusion.** "So it is interesting to note that a previous soldier of the Ninth Legion, Aelius Asclepiades, hailed from the east, in present-day Turkey (CIL X 1769). Other explanations are possible, but both Bogaers and Birley thought it significant that the man bore Hadrian's family name, Aelius, which he might have taken on enlistment to the legion in his native Cilicia." Watson (1969) quoted: "the loss of IX Hispana ... probably during the Jewish War of AD 132-5 ... or even in Armenia in 161." Then: "Nevertheless, archaeology is an inexact science. Most theories can be objected to, at some level... For example, the destruction of the Ninth Legion in a British war at the beginning of Hadrian's reign is a very weak theory. There is no direct evidence for any of the required elements. And yet, the idea has exerted such a hold on the popular imagination that it is difficult to dispel the myth. The logical alternative, that the legion continued to exist at least until AD 140, in order to accommodate the tribunate of Numisius Iunior, has even been questioned. Professor Lawrence Keppie has suggested that the consul of AD 161 was actually the son of the tribune of the Ninth Legion. He has also doubted that Aelius Asclepiades was recruited to the legion while in his native Cilicia, preferring to see him transferred from the Italian fleet at Misenum. Clearly this is a debate which is destined to rumble on. Whether the Ninth Legion met its end in Hadrian's Jewish War, or with the ill-fated Severianus at Elegeia in 161, or somewhere entirely different, we can only hope that further epigraphic discoveries will bring clarity." Further reading: Haalebos 2000 ("has references to the earlier works of Birley, Bogaers, Eck, and Keppie").

**Stated position in 2010:** destruction in Britain early in Hadrian's reign = "a very weak theory"; the legion was at Nijmegen (whole legion, following Bogaers/Birley, "early second century") and survived at least into the 120s (Karus, Crispinus, Florentinus) and probably to c. 140 (Numisius); end in the East — Jewish war or Elegeia — with **no preference expressed** between them, and "somewhere entirely different" left open. He does not, in 2010, argue that a loss in Britain in the mid/late 120s is impossible; he reports Ritterling's own allowance for "a second British revolt... in the second half of the AD 120s" and answers it only with the positive evidence for the legion's presence abroad and the later officers.

### B.2 The book (2018 / Kindle 2020) — what can be said without the text

1. **Author's own characterisations.** Blog "The Lost Legion" (15 Jul 2019): "The destruction of the Roman Ninth Legion at the hands of the wild Caledonian tribes north of Hadrian's Wall is one such idea... I wrote The Fate of the Ninth in order to try and explain where this idea came from. And also why, in my opinion, it's 'a bad idea'. I've also suggested a different scenario to explain the curious disappearance of the Ninth Legion." Blog "A View of Ancient History" (17 Aug 2019): "If you read my book, you will find a closely argued, meticulously documented study of how and why scholars and researchers ever assumed that the Ninth Legion had been destroyed in Britain, with an accurate and reliable account of why this was — and remains — an unlikely conclusion to draw from the available evidence." Blog "A tribune of the Triumphant Ninth" (28 Jul 2019): "My book The Fate of the Ninth describes the journey of discovery, from the eighteenth century to the present, that finally revealed the likely explanation for the curious disappearance of Rome's Ninth Legion." Books page: "Like a detective, Campbell cuts through the layers of myth and assembles the facts, piecing together the unfolding solution to the age-old mystery of the lost legion." Note the tension between "the likely explanation"/"unfolding solution" (author) and "there is no fixed conclusion" (Goldsworthy, below).
2. **Book's Introduction (verbatim, via Goodreads reviewer):** "The 'Fate of the Ninth' is a story told through Roman inscriptions [i.e., epigraphy] … Epigraphy is the study of ancient inscribed texts, chiefly, but not exclusively, those carved onto stone … The Roman army were amongst the most prolific generators of inscriptions, from the tombstones that listed a man's military career to the building inscriptions that recorded the erection of some archaeologically identifiable structure. Roman epitaphs, dedications, honorary texts, and administrative decrees abound. As an added bonus, military building inscriptions generally invoked the reigning emperor... This is important because the erection of the inscription can often be closely dated by the combination of titles or offices held by the emperor during a particular period of time." Also quoted from the book: "[the] science of prosopography, which is the study of persons and their careers from the evidence of inscriptions", and the Mommsen passage (same translation as 2010).
3. **Reviewers' summaries of the conclusion.** Spooner (Classics for All): "presents all the evidence (mainly epigraphic, and with the minimum of speculation), explains it, and presents us with the most likely conclusion about what became of the Ninth. But if you think that this book is the last word on the fate of the Ninth, think again. And this is C.'s main point. The book is most importantly a history of epigraphical and prosopographical scholarship... Nevertheless, the conclusion, as far as it goes, is state of the art, even if not accepted by all. The legion is represented on tile and mortarium stamps after it was supposedly wiped out; their commanders had lengthy careers thenceforth; the legion appears in later eastern campaigns. As ever, it seems far easier to show what didn't happen than what did." Goldsworthy (endorsement): "Legio VIIII Hispana (who usually chose to spell their title that way, rather than using the more familiar IX as numeral)... Campbell traces the way the matter has been studied and how the idea of a legion lost in the mists of northernmost Britain was created and gathered a momentum of its own... This is not an author parading a pet theory and forcing the evidence to fit... I do not think it is giving the game away to state that there is no fixed conclusion - no solving every mystery - and instead we are taken as far as the evidence will go." A.R. Birley (endorsement): the book "traverses the Roman Empire searching for the 'lost' Ninth legion: after Lincoln, York and Carlisle, to Nijmegen in Holland, Aachen in Germany, the Bay of Naples, Rome itself and other parts of Italy, Petra in Jordan, Timgad in Algeria, and elsewhere... former officers of the legion - Burbuleius Ligarianus, Novius Crispinus, Aemilius Karus, Sextius Florentinus, and new discovery Quintus Numisius Junior - are all carefully discussed here." Goodreads readers: "In Duncan's work a case is made that the Legion most likely last[ed] past 118-120 AD and left Britain to disappear in the East, possibly Israel or in Armenia... The case is based off the presence of IX Legion stamped tiles in Njimegen Holland years after the Legions' supposed destruction. There are also records of IX Legion soldiers in the East too"; "he decimates the theory, but his evidence on what really happened to Britain's lost legion is inconclusive"; "I was disappointed that the author did not end the book with a satisfying conclusion. ### B.5 Topic index — Campbell's positions with sources

- **Nijmegen tiles/mortarium/pendant:** whole legion at the Hunerberg after the *vexillatio Britannica* left, "broadly 'early second century'" (2010, 52), following Bogaers 1967 and Haalebos; the single 1959 tile is "securely stratified" in Period III/destruction layer of an officer's house, implying the Ninth re-roofed X Gemina's buildings (RAT 2011); production moved from Scalesceugh to Holdeurn (2010; RAT 2011); "Why manufacture roofing tiles unless you're planning to be there for a while?!" (RAT 2010); the book (ch. 11, tile on p. 122) apparently dates the presence c. 120s (Wikipedia paraphrases: "from c. AD 120", "between 121 and 130"). He no longer relies on the VIIII/IX spelling (blog 2025). He has never engaged Haalebos's 104–120 redating in the obtained texts — the 2010 article cites Haalebos 2000 only as further reading; Hodgson's use of it is answered (2026) only with the Cappadocia remark.
- **Aachen altar (AE 1968, 323):** primus pilus promoted to praefectus castrorum: "it would have been odd for the primus pilus to receive a promotion while absent in charge of a vexillation" (2010, 52), while conceding "only the presence of the aquilifer would be a cast-iron guarantee that the entire legion was there". The 2010 article misnames him "Lucius Licinius Macer".
- **Florentinus:** governor of Arabia c. 127 (papyrus), so legate of VIIII "no more than five years earlier" (2010, 52), i.e. c. 122 or later.
- **Karus:** governor of Arabia 142 (confirmed by diploma), cos. 144 (Syme); tribunate "only after AD 120" (Ritterling) (2010, 51); Birley's date 122 is a terminus post quem (RAT 2010); spelled Karus on ILS 1077 (blog 2023).
- **Crispinus:** cos. 150, born c. 105; tribunate "could not reasonably have fallen before AD 123" (Ritterling), so "Destruction in a British war of AD 119 was out of the question" (2010, 51).
- **Numisius Iunior:** tribune c. 140 (Eck), consul Feb. 161; "unthinkable" that his career began in 119 (2010, 52); Keppie's father/son solution reported without rebuttal (2010, 53); "if there had been no pressure to remove the legion ca. AD 120, we would most logically have seen it surviving up to ca. AD 140" (RAT 2010).
- **Asclepiades (CIL X 1769):** Cilician with Hadrian's nomen — "Other explanations are possible" (2010, 53); Keppie's Misenum-fleet alternative called "a plausible get-out"; the praenomen argument and everything else about the lost stone "is conjecture" (RAT 2011).
- **Keppie:** treated with respect ("a lifetime's study of the legions", RAT 2018); his two objections (Numisius father/son; Asclepiades via the fleet) are reported as live and unresolved (2010, 53; RAT 2011). Campbell nowhere claims to have refuted them.
- **Hodgson:** pre-2021 Hodgson endorsed the book; Hodgson 2021 (per Ross) calls it "a recent fine scholarly monograph"; Campbell's only reply (RAT, 20 Feb 2026) is the "unknown unknowns"/Cappadocia rejoinder to the no-vacant-eastern-base argument.
- **Bar Kokhba vs Elegeia:** 2010 — explicitly undecided ("Whether... in Hadrian's Jewish War, or with the ill-fated Severianus at Elegeia in 161, or somewhere entirely different"). Book — Elegeia examined pro and contra at pp. 134–39 (Juntunen); Goldsworthy: "no fixed conclusion"; Spooner: "the most likely conclusion"; readers: "possibly Israel or in Armenia". 2023 blog — "nobody in the last 40 years has suggested" annihilation by the Jews, "whereas Cassius Dio records the destruction of a legion by the Parthians", which reads as a preference for Elegeia over Bar Kokhba, but he states no probability. **No numerical or verbal probability between the options was found in any obtained text.**
- **Could the legion have been lost in Britain in the 120s?** Campbell's obtained texts never argue that a loss in Britain in the mid/late 120s is impossible. His case is: (i) the "British war of 119" and the link between VI Victrix's arrival and the Ninth's disappearance rest on Borghesi's assumption and Mattingly's coin dating, for which "there is no direct evidence for any of the required elements" (2010, 50, 53); (ii) the officers' careers push the legion's existence to at least c. 122–3 (Florentinus, Karus, Crispinus) and probably c. 140 (Numisius) (2010, 51–2); (iii) the Nijmegen stamps, the Holdeurn mortarium, the Ewijk pendant and the Aachen prefect place the legion, not a vexillation, in Lower Germany in the early second century (2010, 52; RAT 2011), so that "evidence of the legion's departure from Britain does at least exist, whereas there is no evidence that it remained" (blog 2023); (iv) Richmond's cashiering story is "fantastical", with "no evidence" — and Reid (2023) "has failed to explain why Richmond's offhand comment... does not constitute a credible theory" (his words: the burden is on the proponent); (v) "one departure of the Ninth Legion from Britain is quite enough" (Occam, RAT 2011). He records without answering Ritterling's allowance for "a second British revolt... towards the middle or in the second half of the AD 120s" (2010, 51).
