The State is supposed to possess two

For all present purposes these dr.dgers
might as well be hauled up high and dry or
lying at the bottom of the bay. If they are
at work anywhere it is certainly not where
they are needed.
Only two days ago the Mail steamer City
of New York stuck in the mud at the wharf
and had to wait nearly six hours for the
tide to float her off.
Yesterday, while tbe tide had still two
hours to ebb, pretty nearly every ship with
a draught exceeding twelve feet was hard
aground.
It is no sort of exaggeration to say tbat
from Lombard street to the Oceanic wharf
no large ship can be docked except at cer
tain hours of the day when the tide is hlt,h
enough to permit it. One or two of the
wharves are not so bad as the others, but
those that are bad are very bad.
Not only this, but the deposits of sewage
matter from the great sewers under Wash
ington and Broadway wharves send up at
low tide reeking stenches, which nre not
only a menace to passers-by but a danger to
health. Even the chief wharfinger's office
Itself is not a pleasant place to be in at low
tide.
There is work for three or four dredgers
for several weeks right now. In the basin
where the red-stack towboats are moored
there were not more than ten feet yester
day, whereas there should be a minimum
depth of twenty feet at least all aloug the
city front wharves.
One cause of the rapid filling up of the
space between the wharves i. that the
sewers, instead of being carried out to the
end of the wharves so as to discharge the
contents fully into the tideway, are merely
brought out a few feet beyond the seawall.
The piles of the wharves effectually cut off
ths tide current, and all the alluvium in the
bay waters, together wi;h the sewage de
posits, inevitably falls to the bottom be
tween the wharves, producing mud banks
with a rapidity which could hardly be
credited.
It is only a few months since the two
dredgers were at work all along the portion
referred to, but the work will have to be
done over again, and that soon.
Below the Pacific Mail dock and in the
vicinity of Fourth-street long bridge the till
ing up of the bay has gone on even more
rapidly. There are many banks of mud In
the neighborhood of the bridge, with only a
fathom or so of water over them, where
there was a depth of fifteen to twenty feet
only four years ago.

ishment for Their Fine
Work.
Society in far away Alaska is very much
wrought up over an incident that recently
occurred at Juneau.
Judge Iloytisan honorable Commissioner
of Alaska Territory, whose duties are
equivalent to a Justice of the Peace here
abouts, but from a social point of view his
position permits him to rank as high as our
Lieutenant-Governor, and thedignity of his
standing in the community requires a due
observance of social etiquette, which
usually takes the shape of semi-occasional
card parties with abundant refreshments.
Tne Judge's turn for a "blowout" Lav
ing come recently invitations were duly
forwarded to the eligible with the excep
tion of two of Juneau's elite, tho exceptions
being in the case of Frank F. Meyers,
editor of the Mining Record, and Charles E.
Coon, Juneau's baud. orne druggist
These gentlemen were not accustomed to
social ostracism and were becomingly indig
nant at their ill-treatment. There was no
excuse in their estimation for their degra
dation, even If they were at "outs" with the
Judge, and they decided that the shabby
course pursued by the latter should be re
sented. But how the insult was to be
avenged proved to be a poser to the
neglected couple. Finally, however, a
solution of the problem was arrived at, the
druggist agreeing td -uipplv a sufficient
quantity of croton oil to "do up" a regiment
of their enemies while the editor guaran
teed to furnish enough watermelons to hide
ail the drugs in the store. Having loaded
up tbe melons to their liking, an old Si wash
was paid to deliver the goods at the Judge's
residence on the evening of the card party,
which he did.
But alas, for the plans of the conspira
tors, the merrymakers had brought more
good things to the party than they could
eat, the night was too cold anyway for
watermelons, besides there were not enough
plates to go around, so the luscious fruit
was permitted to lie untasted while the
festivities proceeded without a hitch.
But in the morning the Judge's good wife,
teeing she had on hand more melons than
could be eaten before they spoiled, sent a
couple of them to a neighboring friend, a
lady who could not attend the party owing
to an ailing baby.
The latter appreciating the gift cut one
opeu and partook but little thereof, giving
the baby a taste also.
Soon lhey were taken sick, and a doctor
called In, who, after an investigation, found
the cause of the trouble to rest with the
doctored melons.
An analvs showed that the lives of the
mother and babe had been saved by their
partaking of the melon so sparingly.
The next thing was to ascertain who the
eullty parties were, which was not difficult
alter the Siwash who took the melons to
the house had been found. This individual,
although bound to secrecy, fully confessed,
and implicated Meyers and Coon as the
offenders, and the two were immediately
placed under arrest.
The populace was highly incensed, and it
was evident had either of the victims died
there would have been a lynching party as
a sequel, but cooler heads prevailed and the
two were examined before a United States
Commissioner, who held them for trial In
13000 hail at the next term of court to be
held in Sitka some time this month.
The authorities increased the bail of the
men from £3000 to $5000. and as the latter
sum could not be raised before 'he steamer
sailed they were taken on the Mexico to
Sitka, where they now languish, meditating
over the social amenities and vicissitudes of
the polar regions.
The Mining Record, referring to the mat
ter, styles the occurrence as a practical
joke, stupid and vicious, hut with no real
intent to do harm. It asks an abeyance of
public opinion till after the trial.
One reason for the high state of feeling is
owing to tbe various cliques among the
people up there, consisting mainly of the
missionary element, Oregon official*, and
the miners and business men.
The objection to the Oregonians Is that
they are too numerous, receiving nearly all
the fat places. They do not like the Ore
gon laws, classing them as antiquated and
obstructive.
The Oregonians and missionaries clash
because each desires to run things to suit
themselves.
Am Meyers and Coon have mixed up con
siderably in the affairs of these cliques they
ami t know just where and how hard they
will be dropped.
