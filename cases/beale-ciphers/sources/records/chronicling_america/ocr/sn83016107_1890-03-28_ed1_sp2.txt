Canners Protest Against the Proposed
Tin-Plate Increase.
Last Saturday Messrs. J. W. Numsen, A.
K. Shriver and R.T. Smith, representing the
canners of Maryland, appeared before the
ways and means committee of Congress and
protested against the proposed increase of
duty on tin-plate. They were informed that
there was but a slight prospect of the bill
being changed in that respect.
Mr. Shriver said he represented the Can
ned Goods Exchange of Baltimore and about
400 canning establishments in Maryland. He
proceeded to show that several thousand
persons are employed in that industry, and
that 150,000,000 cans of frnit, vegetables and
oysters are pat up annually in various parts
of the State. He produced figures to show
that the canning industry amounts to about
$20,000,000 per annum, in which $12,000,000
worth of tin is used. Under the present rate
of duty Maryland canning establishments pay
about SBOO,OOO yearly into the treasury of
the United States in taxes on tin. If the pro
posed increase is sustained that amount will
be swelled to about $1,500,000.
Mr. Bayne, of the committee, said that in
the course of a year or two tin-plate would be
manufactured in this country in a great
abundance for domestic consumption and at
price that would compare favorably with the
present price paid for imported tin-plate. He
added that thirty thousand men would be
given employment and the country would
save the $20,000,000 now sent out of the
country to pay for English tin.
If Mr. Bayne’s prediction should come
true, of which there are serious doubts, who
would profit by the amount saved Not the
canners nor the consumers of canned goods.
The entire sum would go into the hands of
the gentlemen who propose to experiment in
the manufacture of tin-plate at the ex
pense of other people. Let them first estab
lish their works and demonstrate that tin
plate can be produced as cheaply here as
abroad. Then they will have some grounds
upon which to ask for protection. They do
not promise to give canners cheaper tin, but
only say that their prices will “compare fa
vorably” with the present prices. The
proposed increase is nothing more
or less than an extortion imposed upon
one of the most important industries of Mary
land, tending still farther to depress it and
lessen its profits. It affects, also, millions of
consumers, who would be required to pay
more for their canned goods in order that one
or two firms may be able to carry on their
business at a profit. This is but a sample of
the workings of the Republican tariff bill
now before Congress.
The Philadelphia Tima says:
If tin-plate cannot be manufactured in this
country with a tax of Si% per cent, on con
sumers, it should not be manufactured.at ail. In
point of fact it could be made here now at a fair
profit, but that industry has been delayed sole
ly to compel such increased taxes upon the peo
ple as would establish a complete tin-plate mo
nopoly and the whole battle now is for mono
poly and greed against the hundreds of thou
sands of farmers who grow vegetables and
fruits for canning and are general consumers of
tin and the nearly 800,000 of American working
men now employed in the tin industries.
The Resources of Maryland.
One of the most interesting and valuable
documents lately issued in this Slate is that
of J. Thomas Scharf, LL. D., Commissioner
of the Land Office.
In the Land Office are preserved important
records, documents, maps aud other valuable
papers collected by the Province and State
since 1680, including extracts from deeds, fur
nished by clerks ot court in each county, to
which reference could be made if the original
records were destroyed.
Dr. Scharf has established, in the Land
Office, a Slate Museum, in which there is al
ready a collection oi over 500 stuffed and
mounted Maryland birds and animals, speci
mens of our building stone, mar], sands,
clays, cereals, geological specimens, &c.,
which illustrate the valuable minerals and
ores of the State. There is also a collection
of Indian antiquities.
Dr. Scharf has likewise done good service to
the State by publishing interesting papers on
the soil, climate and other farming advant
ages of Maryland. The pamphlet containing
this information has been largely circulated.
Ad appendix to the report contains a series
of articles descriptive of the resources of the
different counties. Of Harford the report
says
“The soil varies from light loam to heavy clay,
and is easily improved and very productive.
The land is for the most part arable and undu
lating and highly improved. For farming pur
poses the price varies from ten to a hundred
dollars per acre. The chief products are corn,
wheat, oats, bay, tomatoes and small fruits and
vegetables. Stock raising and grazing and the
making and sale of butter and milk are grow
ing industries. Since the opening of the Mary
land Central Railroad, the development of the
milk trade has been very rapid. Over this road
are nowshipped 1,500 gallons of milk daily. Most
of the farmers ar Industrious and thrifty. As a
result they have improved their stock of horses,
cattle, sheep and swine by crossing with the
best strains of each, and now use labor-saving
machinery of the most approved patterns.
