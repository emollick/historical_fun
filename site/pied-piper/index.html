The Pied Piper of Hamelin

Did Hamelin really lose 130 of its young people on 26 June 1284, and if it did, what became of them?

What happened. Hamelin (in German, Hameln) is a small town on the river Weser in northern Germany, some 40 kilometres south-west of Hanover. The story it has told since the fifteenth century: on 26 June 1284, the feast day of Saints John and Paul, a handsome, well-dressed stranger with a silver pipe came in through the gate on the river side, played through the streets, and led 130 of the town's children out through the gate on the far side, towards the hill outside the walls where the town carried out its executions. The children vanished; their mothers went from town to town and found nothing; and the town, the story says, counted its years from that day. The rats, the unpaid fee and the mountain that opened to take the children in first appear in books of the 1550s and 1560s; the lame boy left behind first appears in an English book of 1605, from which the poet Robert Browning took his poem of 1842.

Why it is a mystery. Nothing written at the time survives: the earliest account is a short Latin note added about 1430–50, some 150 years after the event, to a manuscript now in the town library of Lüneburg, a city about 150 kilometres to the north-east (the Lüneburg note), and its writer says he copied it from "an old book"; the house inscriptions that give the date and the number begin in 1525, and the first printed accounts date from 1556. Historians therefore argue about whether anything happened at all, and if it did, what: Hans Dobbertin, a local historian who collected the sources in 1970, and Jürgen Udolph, a German specialist in place names, hold that the "children" were young people recruited for the German settlement of the lands east of the river Elbe, which was actively under way in the 1280s, a view shared by the Museum Hameln, the town's museum, by Norbert Humburg, editor of the 1985 volume of the Hamelin conference on the legend, and by Wolfgang Mieder, author of the English handbook on the legend of 2007, while the medieval historian Bernd Ulrich Hucker objected in 1985 that a lawful emigration should have left traces. Others read the story as a religious procession of the young that came to grief (the Britannica encyclopaedia of 1911; two later writers, G. Spanuth in 1958 and Rumpf in 1985); as an accident on the hills outside the town (the folklorist Waltraud Woeller in 1957–61, the local historian Gernot Hüsam since 1990); as a levy of young men for a local war (Fein, a garrison preacher, in 1749; Thomas Küntzel, writing in the Hamelin museum society's yearbook in 2010, who pointed to the siege in 1284 of the castles of the counts of Everstein, Hamelin's former lords, near the town); or as a crime, and some folklorists (Willy Krogmann in 1934, in part; Rostek-Lühmann in 1995) doubt that any single event lies behind the tale. The version most often repeated in English, that Udolph proved where the children went by feeding Hamelin's surnames into a computer, rests on a press report of 1998; Udolph published no such study.

How it was judged. The evidence is every surviving witness to the story, meaning each document that records it, read in order of date and, wherever possible, from the originals: the Lüneburg note from the facsimile, the images of the manuscript that the Herzog August Library at Wolfenbüttel has put online; the house inscriptions from Christine Wulf's standard edition of 1989 (DI 28, in the Deutsche Inschriften, the standard German edition of medieval and early modern inscriptions); the sixteenth-century printed accounts from digitised copies; and the medieval charters of Hamelin and of Pomerania, the duchy on the Baltic coast about 470 kilometres to the north-east, from the printed charter books, the volumes that collect such medieval documents. The method is to list what the tradition, the story as the town handed it down, claims and when each claim is first attested, then to score seven explanations against those claims and against the documentary record of the 1280s, and to attach to each explanation a probability expressing an estimate of its likelihood; two documents not previously used for the legend came out of the charter books, a bishop's letter of 1502 that fixes the place the inscriptions name and a Pomeranian charter of 8 July 1284 that puts Count Nicolaus of Spiegelberg, of the family of minor nobles who from 1281 held Coppenbrügge, the next lordship east of Hamelin, at the Pomeranian duke's court twelve days after the date of the exodus, and neither document shows what happened in 1284, since the Spiegelbergs show that a channel to the east existed and no evidence shows that a Hamelin group used it. The result is a ranking of the seven explanations by estimated likelihood, and it does not establish which of them is correct: a real loss of young people probably lies behind the tradition (80 in 100, with the caution that no surviving witness is older than about 1430); a recruited departure for the new villages east of the Elbe, the colonial east, is the best single explanation at 42 in 100, which ranks it first, with the other six together carrying 58; and the sources cannot say where the group went. A charter, a locator's contract (a locator was a contractor who recruited settlers for a territorial lord) or a town book of about 1284–1295 naming settlers "de Hamelen" (of Hamelin) in a new village in the east would push emigration above 70; a fourteenth-century Hamelin document mentioning the exodus would settle whether it happened.

Verdict

Hamelin probably did lose a group of its young people in or close to 1284 (80 in 100); of seven explanations for how, a recruited departure for the new villages of the colonial east ranks first at 42 in 100, and where they went cannot be recovered from the sources.

80 in 100 that a loss was real; emigration first of seven at 42 in 100 (about 26 to about 46 as the first figure moves between 50 and 90)
Leading candidate

The 42 for emigration is a plurality among seven explanations rather than a majority, and no source describes a recruitment, a route or a destination; the shares are one reader's judgements, conditional on the hypotheses listed and the weights chosen. The date, the number and the sequence come from witnesses written 150 years and more after the event.

The story as it was first written down, about 150 years after the fact: on the feast day of Saints John and Paul in 1284 a handsome, well-dressed young man with a silver pipe came into Hamelin by the Weser gate, the gate on the river side; all the pueri, the note's Latin word for young people, who heard him, 130 in number, followed him out through the east gate "as if towards" the place called Calvary (the name of the hill of the Crucifixion, which the note itself explains here means the town's beheading place), and were never found; their mothers "ran from town to town and found nothing at all". Every clause of that rests on one note, written about 1430–50 into the Lüneburg manuscript by someone who says he found it "in an old book" and names one eyewitness, the mother of a dean of St Boniface's, Hamelin's principal church, who left a will in 1378. Later witnesses repeat parts of it. The year, the feast day and the number recur in a lost rhymed prayer of unknown date, once in a service book of the same church, and on house inscriptions from 1525 onward; the Calvary in the same prayer, in the book on demons of 1566 by Johann Weyer, a physician, and on inscriptions from 1572; a piper (or a magician) leading the children out of a gate in every witness from 1556 on. The entry by the Weser gate, the exit by the east gate, the silver pipe and the mothers running from town to town have no witness except the Lüneburg note. The calendar date "26 June" is a sixteenth-century conversion of the feast day, first on an inscription on a house on the market square, Markt 1 (1525?, text via Samuel Erich, rector of Hamelin's Latin school, whose book of 1654 preserves several lost texts); and "Monday", the weekday sometimes attached to it, is a computation from it. Pueri, the note's Latin word, and kinder in Middle Low German, the language of medieval northern Germany, cover unmarried young people, servants and crew as well as small children. So the verdict ranks explanations of a tradition; no documented account of the day exists to reconstruct.

Emigration ranks first because it is the one mechanism that needs no special assumption: a locator, a contractor recruiting settlers for a territorial lord, advertised in market towns with music and fine clothes, gathered young unmarried people and led them east in early summer, and a party of 130 would fill one or two new villages. A channel is documented. Count Nicolaus of Spiegelberg is recorded at the Pomeranian duke's court as the duke's kinsman between November 1282 and 8 July 1284, twelve days after the tradition's date (Pommersches Urkundenbuch II no. 1308, the Pomeranian charter book). But no document names a single settler from Hamelin anywhere, the place-name evidence dates nothing, and the story that Jürgen Udolph fed the town's surnames into a computer comes from a 1998 press report rather than from his one scholarly article of 1997. The strongest objection is Bernd Ulrich Hucker's of 1985: given the density of trade and family contact between the Weser and the Baltic through the Hanseatic towns, "von einem spurlosen Verschwinden der Hamelner hätte keine Rede sein können", there could have been no question of the Hamelners vanishing without trace. Dobbertin and Udolph reply that one disaster on the road or at sea would be enough to turn a departure into a catastrophe in the town's memory. That is possible but unproven, and the whole explanation depends on it. It is what keeps the share at 42 rather than 70, and nearly a fifth of the probability stays with the possibility that the tradition records no single event at all.

One judgement carries the ranking, the 80 for a real loss. Holding the event explanations in the same proportion to each other, at 50 "no single event" rises to about 50 and emigration falls to about 26, still first among the event explanations; at 90, emigration rises to about 46. Left off the list because they fail the year every witness agrees on, or the settlement record: the children's crusade of 1212, the battle of Sedemünder near Hamelin (1259/60), the Black Death (1347 onward) and the Transylvanian colony, the German settlement of Transylvania.

What was done

Every surviving witness to the story was read in order of date and, where possible, from the originals: the Lüneburg note, added about 1430–50 to a manuscript in the town library of Lüneburg, from the facsimile; the house inscriptions from Christine Wulf's edition of 1989 (DI 28); the sixteenth-century printed accounts from digitised copies; and the medieval charters of Hamelin and of Pomerania, the duchy on the Baltic coast, from the printed charter books. Not available were Hans Dobbertin's own collection of the sources and several later studies, and the Brandenburg land register of 1375 could be had only as a scan too poor to search for names.

The method was to list what the tradition, the town's own story, claims and when each claim is first attested, then to score seven explanations against those claims and against the documentary record of the 1280s, and to attach to each a probability expressing an estimate of its likelihood. The only computation is calendar and distance checks: the weekday of the feast day in 1284 (a Monday) and the distances from Hamelin to Stettin, the Pomeranian duke's seat, and elsewhere. No statistical method was used; the probabilities are one reader's judgement of the tradition's features and add no evidence of their own.

What is new here

  • None of the source corrections is new evidence in the sense of an unpublished document; each is a new use or a re-reading of something printed or digitised: Fink's Urkundenbuch of 1903 (the Hamelin charter book, the collection of the town's medieval charters), Prümers's Pommersches Urkundenbuch of 1885 (the Pomeranian one), the Wolfenbüttel facsimile of the Lüneburg manuscript, Udolph's article of 1997.
  • New use of a printed source. The Minden charter of 6 July 1502 (Urkundenbuch des Stiftes und der Stadt Hameln II, no. 663) documents a Calvary, a devotional Way of the Cross with stations, "before the east gate … to the boundary of Klein-Afferde, commonly 'beneden den Koppen'". It fixes what the inscriptions of 1525–1602 meant by "Koppen", agrees with Samuel Erich's location of 1654, and rules out Kopahn (Dobbertin) and Coppenbrügge or the Ith ridge, thirteen kilometres east of Hamelin (Waltraud Woeller, Gernot Hüsam), as readings of the phrase. It cannot establish anything about 1284: neither that the group went that way, nor that a Calvary existed there in 1284, nor a destination. It is not cited in Udolph 1997, in Alfred Cammann's summary of Dobbertin's theory of 1984, in Wulf's commentary in DI 28 or in the theory literature that could be read; Hüsam's 1993 paper on "Calvaria" and volume I of the Hamelin Urkundenbuch (Meinardus 1882) could not be obtained, so the claim of novelty is qualified.
  • Checked against the original. Pommersches Urkundenbuch II no. 1308 shows Count Nicolaus of Spiegelberg with his brothers at Stettin on 8 July 1284, twelve days after the tradition's date; the "† 1283" of the Wikipedia genealogy, repeated in recent summaries, is wrong (Dobbertin had the right date, as Cammann's summary of him in 1984 shows). A mounted count could ride the 470 kilometres from Hamelin to Stettin in twelve days; a column of 130 on foot, at 25 kilometres a day, could not. This corrects a date and shows that a channel existed; it does not show that any transfer of people from Hamelin took place.
  • Checked against the original. The Lüneburg note (Ratsbücherei Lüneburg, the town library, Ms. Theol. 2° 25, fol. 268r), re-read from the facsimile: the text circulating on Wikipedia has no basis for its heading "Fabula exodi Hamelnsis" (a cataloguer's title) or its "fere" ("about", before the number), the scribe wrote "valvam" over a cancelled "portam" (both Latin words for a gate), and the note contains no Latin verses. The eyewitness chain runs through Dean Johannes de Lüde (will 1378), the dean whose mother the note names as having seen the children go; it is the only link the sources offer to a fourteenth-century record.
  • Correction. The "surnames fed into a computer" story (1998 press) has no counterpart in Udolph 1997, which examines place names and has only a short section on medieval bynames (the added names that preceded hereditary surnames); several of the place names that Udolph traced from the Hamelin region to the east are attested in the east before 1284 (Hindenburg near Prenzlau in 1269, Depenau in 1250), so the names show that a settlement corridor existed; they do not show that the group of 1284 travelled it.

Already established by others. Christine Wulf's edition of the inscriptions (DI 28, 1989) had already shown that the date "of about 1300" that most retellings give to a stained-glass window in Hamelin is Dobbertin's and undocumented, that the oldest inscription is 1525, and that the "1384: 100 years since our children left" record, often repeated, cannot be traced to any scholarly or early-modern source. Willy Krogmann showed in 1934 that the rats are a south German wandering motif, a story element that travels from place to place, which fused with the Hamelin story in the 1560s. Dobbertin, Udolph, Norbert Humburg, Wolfgang Mieder and the Museum Hameln already rank emigration first; the report agrees, at lower confidence. Bernd Ulrich Hucker's objection of 1985 (a lawful emigration should have left traces) stands as the counter-case, and the accident, procession and levy readings have advocates of their own.

Where this verdict stands among historians

Agrees withDobbertin, Udolph, Humburg, Mieder and the Museum Hameln in ranking emigration first, at lower confidence and without choosing a destination

What historians have said. Since the 1950s a substantial line of opinion (Hans Dobbertin from 1955, Jürgen Udolph 1997, Norbert Humburg, Wolfgang Mieder 2007, and the Museum Hameln today) has read the tradition as a recruited emigration to the colonial east; Dobbertin argued for Pomerania and Prussia, Udolph for the Prignitz and the Uckermark, two districts of Brandenburg, all of them lands east of the Elbe. Bernd Ulrich Hucker objected in 1985 that a lawful emigration should have left traces. The accident reading (Waltraud Woeller 1957/61, Gernot Hüsam 1990–2024), the procession reading (Britannica 1911, G. Spanuth 1958, Rumpf 1985) and the levy reading (Fein 1749, Thomas Küntzel 2010) have advocates of their own, and Willy Krogmann (1934, partly) and Rostek-Lühmann (1995) doubt that any single event lies behind the legend. The popular version adds a computer analysis of surnames said to prove the destination, which comes from a 1998 press report.

The report keeps emigration in first place, at 42 in 100 rather than as a settled result, refuses to choose a destination, and removes the surname evidence from the case. It gives the possibility that the tradition records no single event more weight than the specialists do, 18 in 100. The source corrections change dates and readings in the literature and leave the ranking unchanged.

What would overturn it

  • A charter, a locator's contract or a town book of about 1284–1295 naming settlers "de Hamelen" (of Hamelin) in a new village in Pomerania, the Neumark (Brandenburg's lands east of the river Oder), Prussia or Brandenburg would, on these weights, push emigration above 70 and fix the destination. The Pomeranian charter book's volumes for 1284–1300 contain no such entry; the charter books of Prussia, Brandenburg and Mecklenburg and the earliest burgher books, the registers of townsmen, of Riga, Danzig, Elbing, Stettin and Prenzlau, five towns in the east, have not been searched systematically.
  • A fourteenth-century Hamelin document mentioning the exodus, in the records of St Boniface's, the collegiate church (one run by a body of canons) that was Hamelin's principal church, or in the "old book" the Lüneburg writer used, would settle whether it happened; a full search of the Hamelin deposit in the Hauptstaatsarchiv Hannover, the Lower Saxony state archive in Hanover, finding nothing would push "no single event" up.
  • A mass grave of the 1280s at the Koppen (the Afferde hills) or on the Ith would revive the accident theories; nothing of the kind has appeared in seven centuries of quarrying and building there.
  • Hartmann's 1941 genealogy of the Spiegelberg counts, which could not be obtained, may record Nicolaus after July 1284; if he reappears in Saxony in 1285, the reading in which he himself led the group to Pomerania fails, though the channel remains possible.
