Where was Brunanburh?

Where did Æthelstan, the first king to rule the whole of England, defeat the Viking king of Dublin, the Scots and the Britons of Strathclyde in 937?

What happened. In 937 Anlaf Guthfrithson, the Viking king of Dublin, sailed to join Constantine II, king of Alba (the Gaelic kingdom that became Scotland), and the king of the Britons of Strathclyde, the kingdom on the Clyde, against Æthelstan. The three armies met Æthelstan and his brother Edmund at a place the English called Brunanburh and were beaten: the Norse survivors, Anlaf's Vikings, sailed home to Dublin, Constantine fled north, and Constantine's son, five kings and seven earls were among the dead. A poem in Old English, the English of the period, copied into the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, the year-by-year record of events kept in English monasteries, names the field and the water over which the Norse fled, Dingesmere. The Annals of Ulster, a year-by-year record kept in Irish monasteries, call it "a great, lamentable and horrible battle" in which several thousand Norsemen fell.

Why it is a mystery. No source before the twelfth century says where the battle was, and no place is called Brunanburh today; between about 1095 and 1140 John of Worcester, a monk writing a Latin chronicle of England, wrote that Anlaf's fleet entered the Humber, the great estuary on England's east coast, and every later medieval writer who names a place copies him. More than forty sites have been proposed; four cases are still argued in print: Bromborough, a village on the Wirral, the peninsula between the Dee and Mersey estuaries opposite Liverpool, whose medieval spellings descend from Brunanburh, argued by Paul Cavill, a place-name scholar at the University of Nottingham, and his Nottingham colleagues Stephen Harding, a scientist who writes on the Wirral's Viking past, and Judith Jesch, professor of Viking studies, whose case Cavill and Harding's book Finding the Battle of Brunanburh (University of Chester Press, December 2025) argues at length, together with the place-name scholar Richard Coates and Clare Downham, a historian of Viking-age Britain and Ireland at the University of Liverpool, and cautiously endorsed by Sarah Foot, Æthelstan's biographer, by Alex Woolf, a historian of early Scotland who names no site, and by the Cambridge Old English Reader, a standard university textbook; the Humber and south Yorkshire, argued by Michael Wood, the historian and broadcaster, who puts the field south of York, first at Brinsworth near Rotherham (1980) and later in the Went valley near Doncaster (2013); Burnswark, a flat-topped hill in Dumfriesshire with an Iron Age fort on its summit, nearest of all to Dublin and Strathclyde, argued by Kevin Halloran, an independent historian; and Lanchester, a Roman fort in County Durham above a river called the Browney, argued by Andrew Breeze, a scholar of the Celtic languages at the University of Navarra. Michael Deakin, an independent researcher, denies that Bromborough's name descends from Brunanburh and argues for a fifth site, Bourn in Cambridgeshire, the only new rival since 2011, and the Lancashire coast is a further region of proposals rather than a single site: Amounderness, the district between the rivers Lune and Ribble, put forward by Tim Clarkson, a historian of Strathclyde, as a possible battle zone, the Fylde around Blackpool, Wigan and Burnley. Since 2019 a community group, Wirral Archaeology, has been recovering metal finds on Bebington Heath near Bromborough, while Clarkson and Thomas Williams, a historian who assessed the archaeological claims, hold that the original name may have been lost and the finds do not prove a battle, so that no identification is secure; Historic England, the public body that keeps the official register of battlefields, does not list Brunanburh, because the register requires a battlefield to be securely located.

How it was judged. All 22 medieval texts were read in their original languages (Old English, Latin, Irish, Welsh and Old Norse, the language of the Vikings), and none before John of Worcester locates the battle, while his sentence is a stock formula that he used for other fleet arrivals. Each candidate's name was tested against Old English grammar: Brunanburh is "Brūna's stronghold", formed from a personal name of the class that took the possessive ending -an, and only Bromborough's recorded spellings descend from it regularly. Routes and travel times were computed for every army to every site on one basis, and the judgements were combined in an explicit evidence model, in which each candidate starts with a prior probability, its chance before any evidence is weighed, and each line of evidence multiplies it up or down by a stated factor with a stated margin of doubt, and the calculation is re-run 20,000 times with every factor varied within its margin. The result is a ranking with a stated uncertainty and does not identify the site: Bromborough at roughly 60 percent on the weights chosen (the model's own point figure is 64 percent, rounded down), about one chance in five that the battle was fought at a place nobody has proposed, and no named rival above 5 percent at the point estimate (the figure with every factor at the middle of its range); a demonstration that Bromborough's name derives from brūn, "brown", rather than from the personal name Brūna, an independent witness to a Humber landing from before the Norman Conquest of 1066, or a securely located set of tenth-century battlefield finds elsewhere would overturn it.

Verdict

Bromborough, on the Wirral, at roughly 60% on the evidence weights chosen, ranks first among several sites that remain possible.

The case for Bromborough depends on the derivation of its name: it is the only surviving English place-name that descends regularly from Old English Brūnan burh (Paul Cavill's argument, disputed by Deakin), and the personal name Brūna is rare, four place-names in Keith Briggs's 2021 index of personal names in English place-names, three of them on the Wirral. The other evidence is consistent with the Wirral but does not by itself require it: the Norse fled "on Dingesmere" to Dublin, a name that Cavill, Harding and Jesch read in 2004 as the mere, or wetland, of the Thing, the assembly of the Norse settlers, whose site survives in the name of Thingwall, a Wirral village six kilometres from Bromborough; Dublin to the Dee is about a day's sail in a fair wind, while the Humber can only be reached by sailing round Britain, 700 to 800 nautical miles by the routes computed here; and the poem describes the English as defending their own land, which Cheshire was in 937 and Annandale, the Dumfriesshire valley in which Burnswark stands, was not. Alistair Campbell, the philologist who edited the poem, objected in 1938 that a place bearing the name need not be the battle site; that is why the model gives an unidentified site a large share, and it does not favour any named rival whose own name fails the test.

No source locates the battle; the Humber claim rests on one twelfth-century sentence in John of Worcester; the diagnostic Wirral finds (the few that can be dated to the early Middle Ages) are almost all without recorded findspots (the places where each object was found); and Historic England has not registered the battlefield. Some place not yet proposed gets about 20 percent (9 to 35 across the re-runs). No named rival is above 5 percent at the point estimate: the Lancashire coast, the region of proposals from Amounderness to Burnley, 4 percent; the Went valley (Wood 2013) 3.5; other Humber-bank sites 3; Burnswark 2; Brinsworth (Wood 1980) 2; Lanchester (Breeze) 1; southern and inland sites 0.2. In the 20,000 re-runs, in which every evidence factor is picked at random within its stated range (the Monte-Carlo draws), the highest any named rival reaches is about 10 percent (the Lancashire coast at its 95th percentile, the level it exceeds in only one re-run in twenty), and Bromborough ranks first in every one of them. In the joint worst case, with every one of Bromborough's factors at its low end and every rival's at its high end, the Lancashire coast leads at 24 percent with Bromborough at 2.5; the joint best case gives Bromborough 98.

The strongest case against the Wirral is Michael Wood's reading of the Humber sentence: that John of Worcester was transmitting a lost York annal of about the year 1000. If such an annal existed the sentence would be strong evidence, but no independent witness to it has been found, and the Durham tradition (the History of the Kings, a compilation made at Durham from lost tenth-century northern annals, and the history of the church of Durham that Symeon of Durham wrote between 1104 and 1109) gives 615 ships and the name Wendune, "holy hill", and does not mention the Humber. The sentence is built from John's stock formula for fleet arrivals, the same frame he uses for a Danish invasion of 1013 and for the Norwegian landing in the Humber in 1066, in which only the river-name varies, and it departs from the poem by sending both kings to ships where the poem sends Constantine north by land. Dropping the Humber tradition altogether, the only item that favours the Yorkshire sites, raises Bromborough to 72 percent.

What was done

All 22 medieval texts that mention the battle were read in their original languages, Old English, Latin, Irish, Welsh and Old Norse: from the Old English poem entered in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle under 937 to John of Worcester, a monk writing a Latin chronicle of England between about 1095 and 1140, and each was asked whether it is an independent witness or a copy. The published cases for four sites were read: Bromborough on the Wirral, argued by Paul Cavill and Stephen Harding of the University of Nottingham; the Humber and south Yorkshire, by Michael Wood; Burnswark in Dumfriesshire, by Kevin Halloran; and Lanchester in County Durham, by Andrew Breeze.

Not everything could be read. The 2026 review of Cavill and Harding's book Finding the Battle of Brunanburh by Barbara Yorke, a historian of Anglo-Saxon England, Halloran's 2005 article, Wood's 2013 article, the 2022 and 2025 papers of Michael Deakin, an independent researcher who argues for Bourn in Cambridgeshire, Neil McGuigan's 2014 review in the Scottish Historical Review (McGuigan is a historian of northern Britain before 1300 at the University of St Andrews, and his review doubted the Wirral on strategic grounds) and the Cavill and Harding book itself were paywalled or unobtainable and are cited from abstracts, extracts and replies. John McNeal Dodgson's Cheshire volume, the county survey of place-names that records Bromborough's early spellings, was not consulted directly; no manuscript images were examined beyond published transcriptions, and no fieldwork or archive search was possible.

Each candidate's name was tested against the grammar of Old English, in which Brunanburh means Brūna's stronghold and the spelling of the first element tells what kind of name it was; only Bromborough's recorded spellings descend from it regularly. Routes and travel times were computed for every army to every site on one basis. The judgements were then combined in an evidence model: each candidate starts with a prior probability, its chance before any evidence is weighed, each line of evidence multiplies it up or down by a stated factor with a stated margin of doubt, and the calculation is re-run 20,000 times with every factor varied within its margin.

The result is a ranking with a stated uncertainty, and it does not identify the site. Bromborough is the leading candidate, at roughly 60 in 100 on the weights chosen; there is about one chance in five that the battle was fought somewhere nobody has proposed, and no named rival reaches 5 percent at the point estimate. Every percentage is conditional on the candidate list, the starting probabilities and the evidence factors, which are judgements, and the case turns on one fact, the derivation of Bromborough's name: take it away and Bromborough falls to 24 percent while an unidentified site leads at 45 percent.

What is new here

  • What appears to be the first explicit, reproducible probability model of the question: a prior for each candidate, evidence factors given as ranges, intervals from the 20,000 re-runs and leave-one-out sensitivity (dropping each line of evidence in turn to see what changes), in place of the qualitative "on balance" verdicts in print. It shows that the case turns on one fact about the name and quantifies how far: drop the place-name item and Bromborough falls to 24 percent, with "unidentified" at 45; drop the Humber tradition and Bromborough rises to 72.
  • Route and time computations for every candidate on a single, stated basis, where the figures in print are scattered: Dave Capener of Wirral Archaeology gives 120 miles a day for laden ships, a figure Cavill cites and calls "optimistic for a voyage around the north of Scotland in autumn", and Cavill's own figure is "1,200 miles round Scotland".
  • A source table checked against letter-for-letter transcriptions of the manuscripts rather than repeated from secondary works. These are checks against the editions rather than new findings: the superscript n in the Parker manuscript (Cambridge, Corpus Christi College MS 173, the oldest copy of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle), which shows the double-n spelling Brunnanburh to be a later hand's addition and the original reading to be Brunanburh, the weak form that the derivation needs; the Welsh entry "Bellum Brune", which stands in only the oldest of the three versions of the Annales Cambriae, the Welsh Annals; the actual wording of Æthelweard, an English nobleman who wrote a Latin chronicle around 980, whose form of the name is Brunandune, with "hill" as its second element, and who says the fight was still called "the great battle"; the first combination of the Humber with the 615 ships of the Durham tradition in the Chronicle of Melrose, a thirteenth-century chronicle from the Scottish border abbey; and the 934 date of the Beverley story, the tale told at Beverley Minster in Yorkshire that Æthelstan left his knife on the altar of St John of Beverley as a pledge before the campaign, which in its earliest form belongs to the Scottish war of 934, with no Anlaf and no Brunanburh, as the historian Alice Law noted in 1910.
  • An explicit share for "somewhere else", which earlier writers have mentioned only in passing and which, on these weights, is the second-ranked hypothesis.

Already established by others. No new text, inscription or find; everything here is new analysis of old evidence, and every argument about a candidate has an author in the published literature. The Bromborough derivation was argued in detail by John McNeal Dodgson, the surveyor of Cheshire's place-names, in 1957. The conclusion agrees with the position argued by Cavill, Harding, Jesch, Coates and Downham and cautiously endorsed by Foot, by Woolf, who finds that Bromborough has "much to commend it", and by the Cambridge Old English Reader. It does not establish a field consensus: the Battlefields Trust, the charity for battlefield preservation, calls the site "unlocated", Clarkson calls the identification "distinctly unproven", and Wood, Halloran, Breeze and Deakin argue for other sites.

Where this verdict stands among historians

Agrees withthe Wirral case argued by Cavill, Harding and their colleagues, as the likeliest site rather than a settled one

What historians have said. No formal consensus exists and no poll of specialists has been taken. Bromborough is argued by Cavill (2011 to 2025), Harding and Jesch (with Cavill, 2004), Coates (1998) and Downham (2021) and cautiously endorsed by Foot (2011), Woolf (2007) and the Cambridge Old English Reader (2015). Wood argued for Brinsworth in 1980 and for the Went valley in 2013; Halloran for Burnswark (2005, 2010); Breeze for Lanchester (2016, 2020); Deakin for Bourn in Cambridgeshire (2025); Clarkson (2014) and Williams (2021) hold the site unproven. To judge by reference works and reviews, opinion has moved towards the Wirral since 2011: the case from the name has been re-argued in peer-reviewed journals, where specialists vet an article before it is printed, and each rival answered in print, while Wood, Breeze and Halloran have published nothing new on the site since 2021 and Deakin's Cambridgeshire case is the only new rival; Clarkson warns that "widely accepted as Bromborough" risks becoming a "pseudo-fact".

The verdict sides with the Wirral, as does the case from the name re-argued in peer-reviewed journals since 2011, and it puts a number on how much rests on the single fact of the name. The University of Chester, whose press published Finding the Battle of Brunanburh, said in its press release of December 2025 that the book "paves the way for ending the debate", and the popular history website Medievalists.net said the question "may be solved"; both go further than Cavill and Harding's own "likeliest location". The archaeological claims of 2019–2021 have been scaled back to "consistent with" a camp, with the diagnostic pieces almost all without findspots.

What would overturn it

  • A sustained demonstration that Bromborough's early spellings derive from brūn, "brown", rather than from the personal name Brūna (Deakin's reading): the name factor would fall to about 1 and an unidentified site would lead the ranking.
  • An independent witness to a Humber landing from before the Norman Conquest: a northern annal behind John of Worcester, if one were found, combined with a genuine Brun- fort-name near the Humber, could tie or overtake the Wirral.
  • A securely located, dated tenth-century battle assemblage (a body of finds from one battle) anywhere else, or a charter or saint's-life text naming Brunanburh at another place. A gridded re-survey of Bebington Heath with recorded findspots and dated deposits, or isotope evidence that the iron objects found there came from Scotland, where Constantine's army came from, would harden the verdict instead.

Limits

  • Barbara Yorke's 2026 review of Finding the Battle of Brunanburh (Yorke is a historian of Anglo-Saxon England at the University of Winchester), Halloran's 2005 article, Wood's 2013 article, Deakin's 2022 and 2025 papers, a 2014 review by Neil McGuigan in the Scottish Historical Review (McGuigan is a historian of northern Britain before 1300 at the University of St Andrews, and his review doubted the Wirral on strategic grounds: a Scottish king would have marched into a small peninsula hundreds of miles from his own territory) and the Cavill and Harding book itself were paywalled or unobtainable and are cited from abstracts, extracts and replies.
  • Dodgson's Cheshire volume, the county survey that records Bromborough's early spellings, was not consulted directly. No manuscript images were examined beyond published transcriptions, and no fieldwork or archive search was possible.
  • The evidence factors, the likelihood ratios that say how much each piece of evidence favours each site, are judgements, and a specialist may reasonably set several of them differently; the code is provided so that they can. Nobody has yet re-run the model independently.
