# Brunanburh (937): the Humber-side / South Yorkshire case — dossier

Compiled for the Brunanburh location investigation. Scope: the case FOR a Humber/Yorkshire location (Michael Wood's Brinsworth and Went-valley versions, and the older Humber-dependent candidates) and the documented case AGAINST it.

## A. Michael Wood's arguments, in their successive versions

### A1. Wood 1980, "Brunanburh Revisited" (Saga-Book 20, 200–217) — the Brinsworth / White Hill / Tinsley version

**Method and premise.** Wood follows "Sir Allen Mawer's line of approach, namely that close attention to the politics of the North in the second quarter of the tenth century is a better guide than the guesswork employed by most historians of the battle in the last 150 years" (p. 200). He accepts that "over thirty sites have been put forward" (p. 200).

**Step 1 – the object of the invasion was Northumbria/York (pp. 201–2).** From the Latin panegyric quoted by William of Malmesbury (*GR* ii.135: *Scottorum rege volente, commodat assensum borealis terra serenum … cedunt indigenae, cedit plaga tota superbis*) Wood argues that *borealis/aquilonis (plaga, regnum, terra)* "for Northumbria is commonplace in early sources on the North", that the Northumbrians "submitted to the invaders … willingly, even gladly", and that the *Annals of Clonmacnoise* "state definitely that the allies gave battle with the support of Danes within England who can hardly be other than Northumbrian" (p. 201). Hence "York was undoubtedly at the centre of these events, as it was in all the wars between the Norse of York and their allies on the one hand and the southern English on the other in the period 927–54 … This region was the patrimony of the 'grandsons of Ívarr' and Anlaf Guthfrith's son had undertaken the expedition to regain it. This is the true background to *Brunanburh*" (p. 201). The 937 war "resembles that of 939 when Anlaf Guthfrith's son took a fleet to York right at the end of the year, received the Northumbrian submission and waited until the next campaigning season before striking into Mercia" (p. 201).

**Step 2 – the Humber landing (p. 202 and n. 11).** "Whether the allies came separately over the Irish Sea from Dublin and overland by the well-used early medieval routes through Cumbria and Bernicia, or whether they landed their combined fleet in the Humber, as an annal probably deriving from York around the year 1000 says, they united in southern Northumbria and received the allegiance of the Anglo-Scandinavian aristocracy of that region in York or nearby" (pp. 201–2). Note 11 (p. 213): "Florence of Worcester, *Chronicon ex Chronicis* (ed. B. Thorpe, 1848), I 132. This has been rejected by some modern historians, but with no good reason. Recent research has shown that other Norse-Irish invasions of Northumbria came down the east coast via the lowlands of Scotland, e.g. A. P. Smyth, *Scandinavian York and Dublin* I (1975), 63, 95-6, 107-8." Note 14 adds that the "Florence" annal "seems to contain a valuable survival in its assertion that Constantine had married a daughter to Anlaf son of Sihtric and was the instigator of the 937 coalition".

**Step 3 – chronology implies a late-autumn Northumbrian campaign (pp. 202–3).** The battle "was fought after September 24th, for it was originally dated under 938 in the Parker MS" (indictional-year argument, citing R. Vaughan, *EHR* 69 (1954), 60; Cavill 2022a n. 34 notes this is "not widely accepted now"); Anlaf was still fighting on Lough Ree "at the start of August"; Æthelstan "may have been on the south coast of England on August 21st, making contact with Louis d'Outremer"; Anlaf's "arrival in Dublin 'with a few' after the battle is recorded in the New Year of 938" (p. 202). Wood infers the allies "did not intend to march into southern England in 937, but to winter in Northumbria and strike south in the 938 campaigning season" (p. 202). Note 40 (p. 215): "The suggestion that the invaders embarked at a point completely different from their landing (Dodgson, 1953-7, 315 n. 48) cannot be accepted. If their fleet entered the Humber they can hardly have escaped from the Dee. … If the fleet landed in the Humber, as is most likely, then it will have returned via Scotland, and the notice of its arrival at Dublin early in 938 would support this."

**Step 4 – ravaging in the northern Five Boroughs; Æthelstan's delay; coins (pp. 203–4).** The panegyric's *agricolae* and *miseri* driven off the land "are clearly depicted as considering Athelstan their protector … The likeliest interpretation … is that part of the Five Boroughs is referred to here"; Wood conjectures "the damage … took place in the northern reaches of Danish Mercia" (p. 203). Æthelstan's delay may explain "the strange double obverses produced at this time in York and Nottingham … the work of moneyers who were unwilling to put the king's name on their coins while the issue was undecided" (p. 204, citing C. Blunt, *BNJ* 42 (1974), 92).

**Step 5 – the battle was in the border zone, on a Roman road, on a hill, around a burh (pp. 205–7).** "Late in the year the allies were established in southern Northumbria and ravaging, presumably southwards" (p. 205); the day-long pursuit (*ondlongne dæg*) "perhaps indicates that the battlefield was at least the best part of a day's ride from the ships" (p. 205). "The lost *burh* therefore lay in the border zone between the Northumbrians and the southern English" (p. 206). "The main route from York into the Five Boroughs was the Roman road from Tadcaster to Castleford and Doncaster, where it divides eastwards to Lincoln and westwards via the ford of the Don at Templeborough to Derby and the double fortress at Nottingham" (p. 206) — the axis of Tanshelf 947, Castleford 948 and the 942 Dore–Whitwell line; Æthelstan "probably also came north by Rykneild Street in June 934 when his Mercian rendezvous was Nottingham" (p. 206; cf. S 407, dated Nottingham 7 June 934: https://esawyer.lib.cam.ac.uk/charter/407.html). "Our sources tell us that it was on a prominent hill: this is given us by Simeon's *Wendun* or *Weondun*, and presumably Æthelweard's *Brunandun* is an authentic tenth-century variant. It was obviously on or close to a major road north and south. … It can be no coincidence that the fighting took place around a fortification. *Ymbe Brunanburh* suggests a place of some significance" (p. 206).

**Step 6 – the Don forts and the "Northumbrian limes" (pp. 207–10, Map 1 p. 208).** "It is now known that there was a line of forts along the southern frontier of the Northumbrians in the Viking age, straddling the Roman roads and controlling their crossings over the Don" — Conisbrough (*æt Cunugesburh* 1002–4), Mexborough (*Merces-burh*, "the fort on the frontier", "immediately to the north of the Don ford at Templeborough"), the Doncaster double ditches (p. 207); the Don "was the traditional line of the southern frontier of the Northumbrians" (n. 49, citing Smith, *PN WRY* VII 46 n. 2). "Between the upper Trent and the Aire, then, was a heavily fortified zone where the wars of the second quarter of the tenth century were waged" (p. 211).

**Step 7 – the site: Brynesford/Brinsworth on White Hill (pp. 211–12, Map 2).** "South of the confluence of the Don and the Rother there is a great hill which dominates the Don valley, the Northumbrian frontier and the ford which carries Rykneild Street over the Don. On this hill, White Hill, stood a Domesday settlement called *Brynesford*, 'Brȳni's ford'. In later medieval times this centred on a walled and ditched manor house with an open central enclosure which stood at the side of the Roman road where it crossed the top of the hill" (p. 211). Wood is explicit about the name: "The evidence of the place-name is not decisive: what the Norman scribe wrote down in the later eleventh century implies a *Brȳni*, not a *Bruna* or a *Brune* as in *Brunanburh*. This does not allow us to be definite about the name the site bore in 937 … It is the strategic significance of *Brynesford* which forces our attention upon it" (pp. 211–12). "Five miles to the south lay the forts on the Dore–Whitwell line where the English army stopped in 942. What more natural site than this no man's land for a tenth-century battle with the *Suðangli*?" (p. 212). Note 66 (p. 216): "Brinsworth, WR Yorks, Smith (1961), I 177-8 < OE *Brȳni* + *ford*. Thirteenth-century forms include *Brinys-*, *Brenes-*, *Brunis-ford* … The ford referred to is perhaps the main ford over the Don to the north. The Roman fort at Templeborough stood on low ground by the Don, but there is evidence for old earthworks at Brinsworth itself, presumably on or near the site of the Old Manor House."

**Step 8 – Weondun and the "holy hill" (pp. 212–13).** "Ekwall and Stenton took *Weondun* as the best form and assumed it to contain OE **wēon*, an oblique case of an adjective **wēoh*, 'holy'. *Æt Weondune* would then be comparable to Weedon in Buckinghamshire (*Weodun* 944-6) … None of these names has so far been found north of the Humber … This geographical distribution led Stenton to think that the allies had penetrated far into England … However some of these assumptions are now questionable. Margaret Gelling has shown that the case for an adjective **wēoh* … is not sufficiently strong to be regarded as established. With the alternative form of *Wendun* given by the *Historia Regum* we cannot even be sure which was the original form of the name. The Brinsworth site certainly has the characteristics of the known pagan sanctuaries … situated on prominent hills and often near Roman roads. White Hill at Brinsworth was all these things, and the Roman settlement at Canklow on its northern side produced a temple podium. But the early forms of the name (which are only late fifteenth century) point to an OE *wiht*, 'bend in a river', rather than *wīh*" (p. 212; n. 73: temple podium information from D. Greene, excavator, and Greene, "The Riknild Street. The Route across Brinsworth Common near Rotherham", *YAJ* 38 (1952–5), 112). "In conclusion, the most we can derive from Simeon of Durham is that the site of *Brunanburh*, whether it was called *Wendun* or *Weondun*, was on a prominent hill which must have been known to northern travellers" (p. 213). On the value of the Durham tradition: "*Wendun* seems more than a stab in the dark. By the same token the northern author of the annals incorporated in Simeon's *Historia Regum* could give more extremely circumstantial detail about Athelstan's campaign in Scotland, 'as far as Dunnottar and Wertermor'" (n. 46, p. 215).

**Step 9 – Egil's saga (n. 68, p. 216).** Wood does not rest on the saga but notes that "A. L. Binns … has demonstrated that north-eastern Northumbria was the site of a Latin Vinheath source … '*Vínheiðr* then could be a literal translation of the Latin *Wendun*'"; that in the *Hrokkinskinna* addition to *Heimskringla* ch. 84 "one scribe thought the *Vína* a tributary of the Humber" (*upp eptir ánni Vinu til Úsu*), "so *Vínheiðr* may have been thought of as in the region of the Don valley"; and "the striking correspondence between the Brinsworth site and the famous description of *Vínheiðr* in *Egils saga* … with its forts north and south of the field, its gentle slopes north and south, the steep slope to the river, and the narrow gap to the south where the river and the forest come close together (see map 2)". He acknowledges the "forcible objections" of Hollander (1933) and Campbell (1938, 68–80).

**On Bromborough (n. 4, p. 213).** "Bromborough in Cheshire, though unproven, still holds the field … As Dodgson shows, the place-names *Brunanburh* and Bromborough are identical. But *Brunan-* names with various second elements must have been common in OE, Campbell (1938), 63, and the Norse community in the Wirral cannot be shown to have any relevance to the campaign of 937. … the geographical and political facts given by our sources do not support the identification with Bromborough."

### A3. Wood 2013 (with 2017 and 2020 restatements) — the Went valley / Burghwallis / Went Hill / Barnsdale Bar version

**Citation.** Michael Wood, "Searching for Brunanburh: The Yorkshire Context of the 'Great War' of 937", *Yorkshire Archaeological Journal* 85:1 (2013), 138–59, doi:10.1179/0084427613Z.00000000021 (paywalled). Abstract (T&F page, [read]): "The site of the Battle of Brunanburh has long been controversial, but a consensus has grown over the last few years that it should be located in the Wirral. This article sets out to show that the events of the war of 937 must be understood in the context of Northumbrian history in the Viking age, and that the battle probably took place south of York in the main war zone of the second quarter of the tenth century. A new location is proposed near the River Went, whose name it is suggested is contained in an alternative Northumbrian name for the battle, Wendun." Keywords: Brunanburh, Æthelstan, Northumbria, Viking York, River Went. Acknowledgement: "My thanks to Clare Downham, Charles Insley and Matt Townend for their helpful comments, Alex Woolf for bracing discussion of some points, Eric Houlder for information about his excavation of the Roman crossing of the Went; and Judy Jesch and the contributors to the Brunanburh conference at Nottingham University in 2011". Restated in Michael Wood, "The Spelling of Brunanburh", *Notes and Queries* 64 (2017), 365–69 [Cavill 2022b n. 1 prints "365–96", evidently for 365–9; Cavill's own citations run 365–369]; in *The Times*, 21 Nov 2017 (Simon de Bruxelles, "By 'eck! England's destiny was decided at a Doncaster lay-by"; via Cavill 2022a n. 2); in the *Doncaster Free Press*, 21 Nov 2017 (https://www.doncasterfreepress.co.uk/news/was-doncaster-lay-by-site-of-a-bloody-battle-that-saved-england-from-the-vikings-1000-years-ago-51315 [read]); in a BBC website piece "Brunanburh: Where did the Battle that Saved England Take Place?" (via Cavill 2022a n. 3; URL not recovered); and in Wood's Society of Antiquaries of London lecture "The Battle of Brunanburh: New Light on the 'Great War' of the Tenth Century" (Nov 2020; YouTube https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-C_DjWU2HnA, listed by SAL at https://www.sal.org.uk/event/the-battle-of-brunanburh-new-light-on-the-great-war-of-the-tenth-century/ [pages not retrievable; cited via Cavill 2022a n. 4 and search listing]).

Because the 2013 article is paywalled, its argument is reconstructed below from Cavill 2022a's paraphrases and verbatim quotations (with Wood's page numbers as given by Cavill), the T&F abstract, the 2017 press coverage, and Wood's interview at https://www.historyanswers.co.uk/history-of-war/michael-wood-on-athelstans-great-war-to-unite-anglo-saxon-england/ [read].

**(i) York as the goal; the "Yorkshire context".** Constantine's and Anlaf's "goal at the very least was presumably … the restoration of the kingdom of the Northumbrians in York" (Wood 2013, 140, 142); "in this warfare [between the 920s and the 950s] the focus was the city of York" (151–2) [via Cavill 2022a nn. 5–6]. In the 2020 lecture Wood posits a "Submission of the Northumbrians at York" in early September 937 before the battle, reading Malmesbury's poem (*cedunt indigenae, cedit plaga tota superbis*) as "The natives submit, the whole province (plaga tota) gives in to the proud" with *plaga tota* = Northumbria (Wood 2013, 152–3) [via Cavill 2022a]. In interview: "If York is the goal of the invasion why should the battlefield be at Bromborough?" (historyanswers [read]).

**(ii) The Humber landing rests on a lost northern annal, not on John's invention.** "The tale of the Humber landing cannot be John's own invention. … [He] transmitted a set of tenth-century northern annals from York" (Wood 2013, 147–8 and n. 54), "a good source from pre-Conquest York" (149) [via Cavill 2022a n. 7]. Per Cavill 2022b n. 28, Wood ('Searching', 147–50) argues that "John of Worcester, Symeon of Durham, the Melrose Chronicle, Roger of Howden, Ailred of Beverley, Higden, Langtoft, Roger of Wendover and Robert of Gloucester, all drew independently on 'a set of tenth-century northern annals from York' now no longer extant". Wood also treats the *Historia Regum* annal for 937 as "a tenth-century annal from Chester-le-Street" and "near-contemporary" (141, 146, 147–8 n. 14, 149, 155 n. 89) [via Cavill 2022a n. 16]. He argues the Dublin–York sea route was familiar: "The Viking age sea route from Dublin to York is well attested: 'Dublin Stones' was long a riverside quay in York for unloading wares of ships from Ireland" (149 n. 58) [via Cavill 2022a n. 38]. On the voyage: "an easy sail in the summer"; twelve hundred miles "would take two weeks sailing at an easy pace, more if time is allowed for stopovers" (149); "[I]f the fleet sailed via the Western Isles round the north of Scotland, the journey of two or three weeks might suggest that Anlaf landed in Northumbria in September and that the battle could have been fought in late October, in November, or even early December" (146) [via Cavill 2022a nn. 36, 39–40].

**(iii) Departure also from the Humber.** "If the invaders landed in the Humber they cannot have left from the Dee. When Anlaf set off on the journey back to Dublin … he did so from the place where he had left his ships at the start of the campaign" (149–50, 154); "Guthfrithson escaped with his ships from the Humber northwards, perhaps wintering in Scotland, hence his return noted in spring 938" (144, 146, 149) [via Cavill 2022a nn. 9–10]. This is the 1980 n. 40 argument restated.

**(iv) Corroborating 'Yorkshire' evidence claimed.** (a) *Castleford's Chronicle* (Thomas of Castleford, 14th c.) is said to describe "the Scottish army marching into Yorkshire, the devastation of the land to the south, and Æthelstan's 'knights' fleeing the area down to the Trent" as "a hitherto unnoticed later source from south Yorkshire" echoing the 937 devastations (152 n. 73, 153–4) [via Cavill 2022a]. (b) Numismatic evidence "hints at the collapse of Æthelstan's authority in the East Midlands at some point late in the reign" (154). (c) "the battle is unlikely to have been in the heartland of English Mercia … otherwise how could the site of so momentous a battle have been so completely forgotten?" (143). (d) *Egils saga* is said to relate the battle to an area near York (158 n. 96; 152 n. 73, 153–4) [via Cavill 2022a nn. 12–14, 56]. (e) Late sources (one Irish, one English) say the invaders "were helped by compatriots within England who could only have been from Northumbria or the East Midlands" (Battlefields Trust "Location" page summarising Wood: https://www.battlefieldstrust.com/resource-centre/battlepageview.asp?pageid=891 [read]).

**(v) The name Wendun = "the dun by the Went" = Went Hill; Barnsdale Bar as alternative.** Wood: "the most obvious interpretation of the name Wendun would be a hill by a river with a first element 'Wen'. If this is so, then the evidence of name and topography fits only one river, the Went, between the Don and the Aire. … No pre-Conquest forms have survived, but the Went appears from the twelfth century onwards as Wenet (1154 × 89 until the thirteenth century), Wente (1190 and onwards till [the] sixteenth century) and Went from the thirteenth century … On this reading, Wendun would simply mean 'the dun by the Went' – 'Went Hill'" (Wood 2013, 156) [verbatim via Cavill 2022a]. "[T]he *Historia Regum*'s name for the battle site, Wendun, simply means the 'dun by the Went', i.e. Went Hill, one of the most prominent features on the Great North Road" (Wood 2017, 369 n. 26) [via Cavill 2022a]. And: "The name Wendun indicates that the *burh* was situated near a prominent hill or landmark massif. … On the northern side of the river, Went Hill has name forms going back to the twelfth century. Dominating the Roman and medieval crossings, this ridge is a major landmark to travellers coming from the north-east Midlands to the plain of York, rising precipitously to an escarpment 150 feet above the river in what Smith aptly calls a 'steep-sided lofty ridge'. In its shape and elevation it certainly meets the requirements for a late OE 'dun'. Went Hill is the obvious candidate for Wendun, but it is worth also drawing attention to another very prominent hill south of the Went in a vital strategic position astride the Roman road to York. This is the imposing rounded hill of Barnsdale Bar which rises steeply 150 feet above the important Roman site at Burghwallis, where the Great North Road is met by the Roman road from Templeborough. No early name is recorded for Barnsdale Bar, but it too matches all the requirements of an OE 'dun' and it is perhaps the key strategic position controlling the southern frontier of the Northumbrians and the route to York from the south" (Wood 2013, 155–6) [verbatim via Cavill 2022a]. This last sentence is Wood's claim that the Went/Barnsdale line was the southern frontier of Northumbria (in 1980 he had placed the frontier on the Don, five miles further south-west: 1980 pp. 207–11, n. 49).

**(vi) The "Roman Ridge" / Roman roads.** The site sits where the Roman Great North Road (Margary 28b, Doncaster–Castleford–Tadcaster) is "met by the Roman road from Templeborough" at Burghwallis, with the Went crossing at Wentbridge to the north; Wood credits Eric Houlder's excavation of the Roman crossing of the Went (acknowledgement, [read]). The escarpment north of the Went is the "Roman Ridge" of local usage (the search-engine digest of Wikishire renders Wood's description as "The Roman Ridge dominates Roman and medieval crossings and rises precipitously to an escarpment 150 feet above the river" — UNVERIFIED wording; the substance matches the Went Hill passage above).

**(vii) *Brunanburh* itself: from "Bruna's burh" to "the fort at the spring".** In 1980 Wood accepted *Brunanburh* = *Bruna*'s (or the river *Brune*'s) fort (p. 211). In 2013/2017 he argues that three of the four Chronicle manuscripts of the poem spell the name *Brunnanburh* with <nn> ("including the best tenth-century text, B"), that "This is not, however, simply a matter of a different spelling of the same name. The spellings represent different names" (Wood 2017, 367), that *Brunnanburh* "may have been the original form of the name" and *Brunanburh* "a West Saxon rendering of a northern place-name, which the southerners may have only heard spoken" (2017, 366–9), and that Symeon's *Æt Brunnanwerc uel Brunnanbyrig* comes from "an earlier northern source … very likely one in Old English … the place to which Symeon referred was evidently called not 'Bruna's fort', but 'the fort at the spring'" (2017, 367). "Following Campbell, Brunnanburh then could be the genitive singular of Old English **brunne* a form of *burne* 'stream or spring', but more likely influenced by Old Norse *brunnr* (dat *brunni*)" (2017, 369) [all verbatim via Cavill 2022b]. The spring is "a famous and copious spring, St Helens Well, later known as Robin Hood's Well", beside the Roman fort and Domesday *burh* at Burghwallis: "Was the Burghwallis fort in the Anglo-Saxon period 'the burh at the spring'?" (2017, 369 n. 26) [via Cavill 2022a n. 82; 2022b n. 87]. The *Doncaster Free Press* (2017 [read]) put it thus: "Altering the spelling to a double 'n' and Brunnanburh changes the Old English meaning from 'Bruna's fort' to 'the fort at the spring', which could refer to Robin Hood's Well." In the historyanswers interview [read]: "Three of the four Anglo-Saxon chronicle manuscripts spell it with two 'n's instead of one … Finding 'Wendun' and 'Brunannburh' together south of York warrants investigation." Adrian Grant (2019, p. 6 of v4 PDF, [read]) reports the press version as "In 2017 Wood revised his view, offering instead Skelbrooke, some 15 miles NE of Brinsworth … close to Burghwallis and … on the Roman road" (Skelbrooke is the parish containing Robin Hood's Well).

**(viii) Dingesmere.** Wood 2013 (143 n. 15, 144 n. 16, 146 n. 43) disputes the Cavill–Harding–Jesch reading of *Dingesmere* as "wetland of the þing" at Thingwall/the Dee estuary [via Cavill 2022a n. 11, who says "the evidence relating to the place-name elements in *Brunanburh* and *Dingesmere* is largely misunderstood by Wood"]. Wood's own alternative reading of *Dingesmere* is not recoverable from the sources consulted; in 1980 he offered none, arguing only that the poem "does not portray the return in general and poetic terms" of the embarkation point (n. 40). UNVERIFIED beyond this.

**(ix) Vínheiðr.** In 1980 (n. 68, above) Wood followed Binns in suggesting *Vínheiðr* "could be a literal translation of the Latin Wendun" and noted a Norse scribe's placing of the *Vína* between the Humber and the Ouse; in 2013 (158 n. 96) he again uses *Egils saga* for a York-area setting [via Cavill 2022a n. 56, who counters with Cavill, "Scandinavian *Vína* and English Battles", *Notes and Queries* 63 (2016), 1–5]. Cavill's Casebook (proof p. 320) notes that Townend holds "though the assimilation of Symeon's *Wen-* to *Vína* is problematic, it is not inconceivable". I did not find Wood explicitly equating *Vín-* with *Went* ("Wen-heath = Went-heath") in any verifiable passage — UNVERIFIED.

**(x) Wood's critique of the Wirral case (2013).** "Any attempt to defend [Bromborough] will rest entirely on uncertain and overstated onomastic evidence, unless a good explanation can be found as to how a land invasion of Northumbria from Scotland ended up in a climactic battle at the water's edge of the Mersey – a very difficult situation to envisage, as Plummer long ago pointed out" (Wood 2013, 154) [verbatim via Cavill 2022a]. He concedes the Bromborough derivation is "convincing as a place-name" (154) [via Cavill 2022b n. 4] but stresses that Bromborough "first appears as a place-name in the first half of the twelfth century" (149) [via Cavill 2022a n. 99]; in interview: "The place name isn't even called that until the 1130s-40s so there's a real issue about it … The sources don't even remotely suggest that it was on the shores of the Wirral and why should it have been?" (historyanswers [read]); in 2017: "He says Bromborough is not mentioned in the 1086 Domesday Book and doesn't appear until the 12th century" (Doncaster Free Press [read]). On the Humber: Wood's answer to the "1066 confusion" objection is the lost-York-annals argument at (ii) above, i.e. that John's Humber entry "cannot be John's own invention" (2013, 147) and that the several later witnesses are independent (147–50).

## B. The Humber evidence itself

### B1. The texts (Latin checked in the printed editions on archive.org)

### B2. Source relationships: one Worcester root, or a lost York/Northumbrian annal?

- **Cavill's stemma (2011 proof pp. 312–14; "As the evidence now stands, the source of the tradition that the invading forces came into the Humber is John of Worcester").** Derivatives of John: *Historia Regum* (c), Richard of Cirencester, *Eulogium*; derivatives of Symeon: Melrose, Langtoft; of Henry of Huntingdon: Bartholomew Cotton, Oxnead, Waverley; of Malmesbury: Higden, Book of Hyde. "There is no very strong reason to believe that these particular details were available in, and used from, other sources than Symeon and John in the later tradition." Cavill 2014 p. 97: "All the sources referring to the Humber entry derive from John, so the proposal that he had information from a lost early source has no independent corroboration." Cavill 2014 p. 96 cites Foot 2011 p. 173 on early antiquarian theories.
- **Wood's counter (1980 n. 11; 2013, 147–50)**: the Humber notice is "an annal probably deriving from York around the year 1000" (1980, p. 202); "The tale of the Humber landing cannot be John's own invention" (2013, 147); the later witnesses drew "independently" on "a set of tenth-century northern annals from York" (2013, 147–50, via Cavill 2022b n. 28). Grant 2019 (v4, p. 5 [read]) presses the same point from a Humber-side perspective: Howden "came from Howden – just 12 miles from Brough … It is beyond insulting to suggest that he 'got the river wrong'"; and "it is Simeon of the three who mentions Wendune. So clearly he had access to a source beyond these two."
- **Downham 2021** (Clare Downham, "A Wirral Location for the Battle of Brunanburh", *Transactions of the Historic Society of Lancashire and Cheshire* 170 (2021), 15–32, at 21–2) "argues that John did, in fact, make the story up, and that the numerous sources containing the story got it from John" and "has given good reasons for discounting Wood's argument" about independent witnesses [via Cavill 2022a n. 20 and 2022b n. 28; not read].
- Lendinara (in the *Casebook*, "Brunanburh in Later Histories and Romances", 206): "John of Worcester was simply transferring the motif of crossing the Humber (frequent in Wace and other writers) to the momentous battle of Brunanburh" [via Cavill 2022a n. 30].
- Livingston (*Casebook*; *Never Greater Slaughter* 2021) treats John, writing 200 years after the event, as unreliable on this point [via Battlefields Trust summary and Wikipedia; not read].

### B3. The "1066 borrowing" argument and the counter-arguments

- **The formula argument as actually documented.** Cavill 2011 (proof pp. 321–2 = published 337–9) sets John's 937 sentence beside his 1066 entry (Darlington & McGurk II 602–3; Thorpe I 226 [OCR read]): "*Ad quem comes Tostius … sua cum classe venit, et citato cursu ostium Humbre fluminis intraverunt, et sic adversus cursum Use fluminis navigantes, in loco quo Richale dicitur applicuerunt*", noting Darlington & McGurk's remark that "The approach through the Humber and the landing-place are not mentioned in ASC", and concludes: "In this case the Humber entry is accurate, but the almost formulaic addition of the details to the Chronicle record, closely parallel to those John records for Brunanburh, reveals a habit of mind; in the preceding passage John even confuses Harald Harfagri with Harald Hardrada, much as he confuses the two Anlafs." He adds that John, reporting the Northumbrians' choice of "Anlaf, *rex Nordmannorum*" in 941, "does not distinguish this Anlaf from Anlaf Guthfrithsson", so "It would be logical for John, with these associations in mind, to assume that if Constantine and Anlaf were northern confederates, then the likeliest waterway for their entry and escape would be the Humber." Cavill 2014 p. 97 repeats the parallel and notes "Woolf 2007, p. 171 also points out that John recorded another Humber entry three years later" (i.e. 1069). John's 1013 entry (Thorpe I, s.a. 1013 [OCR read]) has Swein "*East-Anglia circumnavigata, ostium Humbrae fluminis intravit*". Cavill 2022c (913–14, via 2022a n. 20) "shows how frequent the formula '*ostium* (mouth) of river name + verb of motion/entry' is in John's work for what invading fleets did" — "more than twenty examples before the Norman Conquest" (search digest of Cavill 2022c; wording UNVERIFIED).
- **Attribution to Halloran 2005.** The view that John's Humber entry may be a borrowing from 1066 has been attributed to Kevin Halloran, "The Brunanburh Campaign: A Reappraisal", *Scottish Historical Review* 84:2 (2005), 133–48 (doi:10.3366/shr.2005.84.2.133; paywalled). I could not access the article. Its abstract [read via EUP/search] says only that it "proposes a radically different interpretation of the campaign of 937", questions "the reliability of the variant form *Brunanburh* as a guide to the battle's location and concludes that the most likely site was Burnswark in Annandale"; the *Cambridge Antiquity* / Wikipedia summaries say Halloran rejects the Humber landing as "a completely unrealistic scenario imagined by the twelfth-century chronicler John of Worcester" and that "His analysis does not derive from 1066 confusion" (Wikipedia, [read]). The 1066 parallel is securely documented for Cavill (2011, 2014, 2022a, 2022c) and Woolf (2007, 171); its attribution to Halloran 2005 is UNVERIFIED.
- **Counter-arguments (the "details differ" point).** Wood 2013 (147–50): the Humber notice comes from a lost pre-Conquest York annal and John "transmitted" rather than invented it (above). Foot 2011: I could not verify her wording; the IHR review (https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/1151/ [read]) records only that she "cautiously supports an identification of Brunanburh with Bromborough". Grant 2019 (above). Sally England (2013/2020) "point[s] to at least nine different medieval sources referring to the Humber or Beverley", to which the Battlefields Trust replies that "such medieval references can be traced back to John of Worcester, who has been shown to elaborate on the sources he used" (BT "Location" page [read]). Livingston (per Cavill 2014 p. 98 n.): "Anlaf Guthfrithsson took his army from a campaign in central Ireland to the field at Brunanburh in less than two months", so no long voyage/delay can be accommodated.
- **Cavill's further point (2022c, 915–16, via 2022a n. 21):** John "does not claim that the fleeing Scandinavians returned to their ships" in the Humber; "he, and other writers, do not assert that the Humber was the site of the flight of the fugitives" (abstract [read]). Cavill 2011 (proof pp. 321–2) also notes that John "misreads or adjusts" the poem in making both Anlaf and Constantine flee *ad naves*, whereas the poem has Anlaf alone escape by ship and Constantine flee north (*fleam* = land flight).

### B4. Cavill's Casebook treatment of the Humber tradition (2011, §4 "John of Worcester and the Humber entry", proof pp. 321–2 = published 337–9)

Summarised: (1) the Humber statement is "peculiar to" John (Darlington & McGurk); (2) it is the introduction to an entry which is otherwise "a summary of the Old English poem"; (3) John misreads the poem's flight; (4) John conflates the two Anlafs (also Malmesbury *GR* ii.131.4) and associates Anlaf with Northumbria (941 entry); (5) "As the most significant northern English river and boundary, and as a place of entry for invading forces, the Humber figures numerous times in the Chronicle and other authorities" (n. 36 cites Higham, "Context", 144 n. 6 and Lendinara 206); (6) the 1066 parallel; (7) "No earlier or independent tradition mentions the Humber in relation to the Battle of Brunanburh … All these factors make it reasonable to doubt that John has the only accurate tradition about Brunanburh and that all the others omitted such a useful detail." In §5 (proof p. 323) Cavill notes that of the serious modern candidates "Burnswark (Dumfriesshire), Brinsworth (Yorkshire), Bromswold … and Bourne (Lincolnshire) … all but the first of these rely on John of Worcester's tradition of the Humber entry". Cavill 2014 (p. 97) adds two further arguments: "One might also ask why the poet of Brunanburh named the rather obscure Dingesmere as the route of escape for the Norsemen if he knew it was or was near the instantly and universally recognisable Humber", and the *Capture of the Five Boroughs* poem "mentions the Humber as the boundary between the Danes in east Mercia and the Northumbrians of York".

## C. Place-name arguments

### C1. Brinsworth
- Forms: Domesday *Brinesford/Brynesford*; 13th-c. *Brinys-*, *Brenes-*, *Brunis-ford*; EPNS: A. H. Smith, *Place-Names of the West Riding of Yorkshire* I (1961), 177–8: OE pers. name *Brȳni* + *ford*, "Brȳni's ford" (Wood 1980 n. 66 [read]; EPNS page not checked directly). Cavill 2011 (proof p. 324): "Brinsworth, first recorded as Brynesford, might be confused with Bruneford in William of Malmesbury (though Wood does not argue for this)"; the "persistent -s genitive in Brinsworth spellings" (n. 50, citing R. I. Page, "A Tale of Two Cities", *Peritia* 1 (1982), 344–5) contradicts the weak genitive *Brunan-* of the battle name. Page: "it is also clear that, whatever the name was in the tenth century, it is unlikely it was Brunanburh" (via Cavill 2011 proof p. 324). Wood himself concedes the Norman scribe's form "implies a Brȳni, not a Bruna or a Brune as in Brunanburh" (1980, p. 211). Cavill's conclusion on the White Hill/*Weondun* link: the temple-podium argument "suggests confusion, on the part of the tradition, of Old English *wiht* [river bend] in the name White Hill, with *wih* [holy]" — "for which there is no evidence at all" (proof p. 324 and n. 46), Wood having himself shown that the White Hill forms point to *wiht* (1980 p. 212). Cavill 2022a (§Conclusion): Wood "contradicts his own earlier work, where he identified Brinsworth with Brunanburh, and White Hill with Wendun". Grant 2019 (v4 p. 6): "taking 'Brin' for Brunan … does not work etymologically" (his own Brythonic etymology of Brinsworth is idiosyncratic and should not be relied on).

### C2. The Went, *Wendun/Weondun*, Went Hill, Barnsdale Bar, Burghwallis
- **The river-name Went**: forms *Wenet* (1154×89 to 13th c.), *Wente* (1190 onwards), *Went* (13th c. onwards) (Wood 2013, 156, via Cavill 2022a; from Smith, *PN WRY* VII and Watts, *Cambridge Dictionary of English Place-Names*, 663). Watts's etymology: "Pr[imitive] W[elsh] **wīnet* or **winēd*", "ultimately referring to pleasant characteristics" (Cavill 2022a, citing Watts 663). Went Bridge: *pontem de Wente* 1190, *ponte(m) de Wenet* 1190×1210, *Wentbrig(g)* 1302 onwards (Smith *PN WRY* II 51, via Cavill 2022a n. 96). Went Hill: *Weneteshil* 1180–1200, *Wenteshill* c.1200 (Smith *PN WRY* II 64, via Cavill 2022a n. 99).
- **Cavill's objections (2022a, §"Wendun and Went Hill")**: (1) "there is no evidence that Went Hill was ever known by the name *Wendun*"; (2) "the identification of the first element of *Wendun* with the name of the River Went is linguistically problematic": the early river-name is disyllabic *Wenet/Wente*, monosyllabic *Went* only from the 13th c., and "There is no sign of the characteristic disyllabic element in *Wendun*"; Went Hill's forms show a strong genitive *-es-* (*Weneteshil*), unlike *Wendun*; "There are literally hundreds of names with the element *dūn* but very few of them, perhaps as few as two, are combined with a river name: Brandon … Laindon"; Wood "entirely ignores the spelling *Weondun* in *Symeon of Durham, Libellus* which would make even less sense as a reflex of *Wenet* or *Wente*"; (3) topography: Went Hill "was not a *dūn*" — Gelling's *dūn* is "a low hill with a fairly level and fairly extensive summit … in open country", "characteristically not a 'prominent hill or landmark massif', not 'steep-sided', not 'an escarpment', not a 'lofty ridge'"; it was called a *hyll*; and Gelling holds *dūn* "may not have been much employed after A.D. 800" as a settlement-name element, so Wood's "late OE 'dun'" needs explanation. Cavill also notes the irony that Wood rejects Bromborough as first attested in the 12th c. yet relies on Went Hill, first attested "in the second half of the twelfth century" (n. 99). Barnsdale Bar has "No early name … recorded" (Wood's own admission, 2013, 156).
- **Burghwallis**: Domesday *Burg* etc. (reflex of OE *burh*), "with sporadic addition of the family name *Wallis* 'from the twelfth century and after'" (Smith *PN WRY* II 35–6, via Cavill 2022a). Cavill: "there is no evidence that Burghwallis was ever called anything like *Brun(n)anburh*"; over two dozen simplex *burh* names are attested by 1086; the spring's names (St Helen's Well, Robin Hood's Well) "derive from the Old English term *wella* 'spring'", not from any **brunna*; "It would be, and apparently was, redundant to add a generic water feature element to the name of a fortification. The water-source for a *burh* is generally unremarked" — no *burh* compounds with *ǣwylm, bekkr, brōc, burna, ēa, flēot, funta, kelda, lacu, mere, pōl, wæter, wella* are known, whereas *burh* readily compounds with specific river-names (Blythburgh, Kintbury, Tenbury …); so *Brun(n)anburh* "interpreted as 'fort at the spring' would be formally unexampled, hence intrinsically implausible … The only claim that Burghwallis has to being more plausible than Wood's earlier guess at the name *Brunanburh*, Brinsworth, is that it has one of the elements of the battle name (*burh*) instead of none" (2022a).

### C3. *Bruna*, *brunnr*, the <nn> spellings and Wood's "two places" hypothesis
- Cavill 2011 (§3.1, proof p. 315): the first element "might be thought to represent Old English *Bruna*, a weak masculine personal name; Old English *brune*, a weak feminine river-name meaning 'dark, brown, shining (one)'; or a metathesized version of the Old English weak masculine noun *burna*"; the <nn> forms occur "in distinctively northern sources, namely Symeon and Langtoft, and in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle MS C and in an addition/correction to … MS A"; "The Scandinavian noun *brunnr* … might have influenced the northern forms with -nn-"; but "the element *brunan-* is clearly declined weak", and *Bruningafeld* (12th-c. charters, Scottish tradition) "reinforce[s] the notion that the first element … is most likely to be a personal name *Bruna* or a river name *brune*" (§3.7).
- Cavill 2022b (full rebuttal of Wood 2017): the <nn> witnesses are C (certain), B (probable, damaged), and a later interlinear *n* in A ("'n' added in another hand", Bately); B and C descend from the West Saxon "Continuation 2", so the <nn> is "linguistically West Saxon", not northern; the northern-recension manuscripts D, E, F all have <n>; Symeon's *Etbrunnanwerc* lacks a dative *-e* and "reflect[s] the loss of inflections in late Old English and early Middle English"; "there is no case in the standard declension of singular Old Norse *brunnr* that would plausibly give a form *brunnan*"; OE *burna/*brun(n)e* means "stream, brook, river", not "spring" (DOE); the one OE common-noun example (*on brunnan/brunan*, Stoke by Ipswich, S 781) is a *burna* "stream"; *Bruna* is rare (PASE lists none; Briggs's index gives four *Bruna* place-names, three of them the Wirral cluster Bromborough–Brimstage–Brimston, plus Bromham, Beds.), so Campbell's "*Brunan-*, a common … element" is wrong; and *Bruna* may be a hypocorism (Brungar, Brunstan, Brunwine …) whose geminated form *Brunna* would explain the <nn>. Conclusion: "it is overwhelmingly likely that the *Brunanburh* and *Brunnanburh* spellings … are orthographical variants … So the answer to Wood's question, 'Was the Burghwallis fort in the Anglo-Saxon period "the burh at the spring"?' must be negative: it is vanishingly unlikely." Cavill also catches Wood calling Brinsley (Notts.) "a *Bruna* name" when EPNS gives *Brūn* with strong genitive (2022b n. 86).
- **What place-name evidence does Wood give for a "Bruna" in the Went country?** None that I could find: the 2013/2017 case abandons the personal name in favour of *brunnr* "spring" (Robin Hood's Well) — see A3(vii). Cavill 2022a: "there is no evidence that Burghwallis was ever called anything like *Brun(n)anburh*." UNVERIFIED whether Wood 2013 cites any local *Brun-* minor names near the Went.

### C4. The *dun* / *burh* / *werc* alternation and *Weondun* = "hill of the shrine"
- Æthelweard, *Chronicon* (ed. Campbell 1962, 54): *in loco Brunandune*; Symeon (a): *Weondune/Wendune*; Symeon (b): *Et Brunnanwerc uel Brunnanbyrig*; the Chronicle: *Brunanburh* (Cavill 2011 §2, proof pp. 313–14). Cavill: "The element *dun* appears in Æthelweard's … *Brunandun*, and in Symeon of Durham's own tradition (a), *We(o)ndun*. These traditions are independent" (§3.3), which "may be thought to reinforce the credibility of Æthelweard's report" (§1). *(ge)weorc* "has much in common with *burh*"; Dodgson: "the -werc(e) forms are no more than a paraphrase of Brunanburh" (*PN Ch* 4.238) (§3.5). Cavill 2014 pp. 100–1 adds Southwark/*Suðbyrig*, *Barnewerc*/Burton Lazars, *Aldwerch*/Burrow Fields (Margidunum) as burh~weorc doublets, and notes Symeon's singular *alio nomine* shows he took *Etbrunnanwerc* and *Brunnanbyrig* as one name (Halloran 2010 conceded the translation).
- *We(o)ndun*: Campbell (1938, 62 n. 2) proposed a scribal error for **Weordun* (river Wear); A. H. Smith (*EPNE* II 254) an oblique case of **wēoh* "holy"/heathen shrine; Gelling doubted the adjective, but "the *dun* element and feature provides us with a significant number of heathen names": Harrowden (×2), Harrowdown, Weedon (Berks.), Weedon Bec and Weedon Lois (Cavill 2011 §3.4, proof p. 317). Cavill adopts Smith's reading ("*Weondun* and *Wendun* would be '(place at) a holy hill'", 2022a, citing Smith *EPNE* II 254) and develops it in Cavill, "We(o)ndun, Symeon of Durham and the battle of Brunanburh", *Medium Ævum* 93:1 (2024), 46–69 (https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/44422949/ — not accessible; a search digest reports that he connects it with a lost *Welondrys* 1357, "scrubland of the shrine", OE *wēoh + land + hrīs*, near Bromborough — UNVERIFIED). The *wēoh* reading was already used FOR Brinsworth by the 1937 Spectator correspondent ("*Weondune* is 'the hill or temple of the heathen deity'", https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/1st-october-1937/20/ [read]) and, hedged, by Wood 1980 (§A1 step 8) and Wood 1999. Andrew Breeze instead reads *We(o)ndun* as a "wen-shaped hill" (Lanchester), which Cavill 2023 (*Neophilologus*, abstract [read]) calls phonologically implausible; Grant 2019 (v4, pp. 11–12) reads it as "Woden's Hill" at Elloughton — a private etymology. Halloran 2010 (via Cavill 2014 p. 99 n.) simply declares "Wendune rather than Weondune is the correct form and refers to the hill of Burnswark". Cavill's topographical point (2011 §3.3): *dūn* names are "hills of 200ft–500ft" with level summits, cluster in the south Midlands/Essex, are absent north of the Ribble and rare in the north-west — a constraint that cuts against Burnswark (920 ft) and, he later argues, against Went Hill, but which Brinsworth's White Hill (230 ft; Cavill 2011 proof p. 324) and the Went country arguably satisfy in elevation if not in form.

## D. Strategic arguments

### D1. FOR the Humber / Yorkshire
1. **Olaf's claim on York.** His father Guthfrith held York briefly in 927 before Æthelstan expelled him; Wood: the region "was the patrimony of the 'grandsons of Ívarr' and Anlaf Guthfrith's son had undertaken the expedition to regain it" (1980 p. 201); the 937 war "resembles that of 939 when Anlaf Guthfrith's son took a fleet to York" (1980 p. 201). Wood 2013: the goal was "the restoration of the kingdom of the Northumbrians in York" (140, 142). Grant 2019 (p. 6): "Olaf and Constantine were seasoned generals … their primary objective was to secure York."
2. **York Norse / Northumbrians / "Danes within England" as allies.** Malmesbury's panegyric (*cedunt indigenae …*), the *Annals of Clonmacnoise* ("Danes … within England"), Henry of Huntingdon, Bartholomew Cotton, Bower's *Scotichronicon* (*Northumbri … statim Constantino juncti*) — collected by Wood 1980 pp. 201, 203 and conceded by Cavill 2022a n. 65 ("Many sources suggest that the Anglo-Scandinavian areas supported the coalition, as Wood notes").
3. **The Roman-road war-zone south of York.** Tanshelf 947, Castleford 948, Dore–Whitwell 942, Æthelstan's 934 Nottingham muster (S 407) — all on the York–Doncaster–Rykneild Street axis (Wood 1980 p. 206). The Don/Went forts (1980 pp. 207–10).
4. **The 1066 analogy.** Hardrada and Tostig entered the Humber, sailed up the Ouse to Riccall and fought at Fulford for York (John of Worcester s.a. 1066 [OCR read]) — an exact template for a Norse bid for York by sea; the same route was used by Swein in 1013 (John s.a. 1013) and by the Danish fleet of 1069. Wood 1980 n. 11 stresses that "other Norse-Irish invasions of Northumbria came down the east coast via the lowlands of Scotland" (Smyth).
5. **The Durham tradition's independence and the two names.** *Wendune* and 615 ships in the *Historia Regum*/*Libellus* are independent of John (Cavill 2011 §1 agrees); Wood reads them as "a genuine tenth-century northern name for the battle site" (1980 p. 212) and "near-contemporary" (2013, 146).
6. **Late-season chronology** (Lough Ree in August; Æthelstan on the south coast on 21 August; battle after 24 September; Anlaf back in Dublin only in 938) fits a long voyage and an intended wintering in Northumbria (Wood 1980 pp. 202–3; 2013, 146).
7. **The forgotten site** argument: a Mercian battlefield would not have been forgotten; a Northumbrian frontier site could be (Wood 2013, 143).
8. **Egils saga** places Olaf's landing and the campaign in "Northumberland" and describes a heath with a river on one side and a wood on the other, a town north of the heath and Athelstan's tents "between wood and river" (Egla ch. 52, https://wikisaga.hi.is/index.php/Egla,_52 [read]) — matched by Wood to White Hill/Tinsley Wood (1980 n. 68). Wood's Brinsworth map (1980 Map 2) shows the river (Rother), the wood (Tinsley Wood) and the narrow gap south.
9. **Numismatic hint** of wavering allegiance at York and Nottingham (Blunt 1974, 92; Wood 1980 p. 204; 2013, 154).

### D2. AGAINST
1. **Sailing distance and logistics.** Cavill 2014 p. 97: "There is a profound improbability to the idea of an invading Viking force from the west (Dublin) sailing hundreds of miles around Scotland to the east to meet another force from the west, the warriors of Strathclyde … and the men of Alba." Cavill 2022a: "a 1,200-mile sailing trip around the north of Scotland for two or three weeks (or so) as autumn was wearing on"; "Men are a peculiarly awkward and hungry cargo … much of the coastline between the embarkation point and the Humber is inhospitable"; "From the Ribble or the Mersey, York and the proposed Yorkshire battle sites are no more than eighty miles away; the cross-Pennine routes were well-known"; "John of Worcester's sailing expedition would have been an utterly pointless and impractical venture". Livingston (via Cavill 2014 p. 98 n.): the timetable from Lough Ree (August) to the battle allows "less than two months", excluding a leisurely circumnavigation plus a delayed return. (A southern route round Cornwall is not seriously entertained by any writer consulted.)
2. **No source says York was taken, or even mentions York.** Cavill 2022a: "This opinion [York as focus] is shared by precisely none of the fifty-three medieval and early Modern sources given in the Livingston *Casebook*, not even John of Worcester. John of Worcester dumps Anlaf's fleet without ceremony in the Humber and without further detail … No source mentions York in relation to the Brunanburh campaign." Blunt 1974, 89: "It has been suggested that Anlaf recovered York for a short time in 937, but this has been disputed … Certainly there is no identifiable break in Athelstan's coinage there and no coins in the name of Anlaf that can be associated with such a capture" (via Cavill 2022a n. 55). Castleford's *Chronicle*, Wood's one Yorkshire witness, describes the 934 campaign of "Girge" and "makes no mention whatever of the *Brunanburh* campaign and no mention of Anlaf … Castleford's complete lack of interest in *Brunanburh* strongly suggests that the battle did not take place in south Yorkshire" (Cavill 2022a). Malmesbury's poem: *aquilonis* qualifies *fera barbaries* (the northern barbarian), and *borealis terra … Scottorum rege uolente* is Scotland, not Northumbria; "We are left, then, with nothing to suggest a formal submission of Northumbria" (Cavill 2022a).
3. **Even Olaf's 939 kingship at York is doubted.** Kevin Halloran, "Anlaf Guthfrithson at York: A Non-Existent Kingship?", *Northern History* 50:2 (2013), 180–85, doi:10.1179/0078172X13Z.00000000042: the proposition that Anlaf Guthfrithson took the York kingship after 939 "is unlikely", the record confusing him with Anlaf Cuaran (abstract [read via search]). If so, the 937/939 parallel loses force.
4. **The Chronicle poem's *Dingesmere* and Dublin.** The Norsemen "*on Dingesmere ofer deop wæter Difelin secan*" — a west-coast departure; Cavill: why name "the rather obscure Dingesmere … if he knew it was or was near the instantly and universally recognisable Humber" (2014 p. 97); Clarkson: the poem's return to Dublin "suggests a western approach makes more logistical sense than circling Scotland to reach the Humber" (https://senchus.wordpress.com/2014/12/09/brunanburh-in-937-bromborough-or-lanchester/ [read]). *Dingesmere* = "wetland of the þing" by Thingwall on the Dee (Cavill, Harding & Jesch, *JEPNS* 36 (2004), 25–38; Cavill 2007, 2014 pp. 104–6).
5. **Absence of the Humber from the Chronicle, Æthelweard, the Durham annals, Malmesbury, Huntingdon, the Irish, Welsh and Norse notices** (B1 §8) — "it must be doubted that if the Viking force did so, no source would mention such a significant detail until John of Worcester" (Cavill 2014 p. 97).
6. **A Dublin king's natural landfall was the Mersey/Dee, Ribble or Lune.** Cavill 2011 (proof p. 324): "Nicholas Higham and Sarah Foot have separately argued with just as much evidence and conviction for the strategic and political significance of west Mercia at this time" (Higham, "Context", and Foot, "Where English Becomes British", 2008); Cavill 2014 p. 99: "The goal of the coalition was Winchester, not York. Ravaging English Mercia west of the Pennines would be part of the strategy and indeed that is what William of Malmesbury also tells us: *multum in Angliam processerat*"; Cavill 2022a: "a rendezvous on the Wirral would be convenient for the Dublin, Cumbrian and Scots forces, open to good roads (Margary 6, 41, and 43) from Chester through English territory to the centre of West Saxon power at Winchester"; Carlisle–Chester is "about the same distance as Carlisle to Burghwallis"; Plummer (1892–9, II 139–40): "such a spot must be sought in the west of England, and … Fl. Wig.'s statement that Anlaf … entered the mouth of the Humber must be an error". Clarkson prefers a western landfall, possibly "North Lancashire" [read]. Even the Humber-partisan Grant (v4 p. 6) concedes that if landing in the west "Morecambe Bay/Lunesmouth or Preston/Ribblesmouth would have been far more intelligent landing place[s]" than the Wirral.
7. **Silence of the St Cuthbert community.** Constantine's army marching to York down Dere Street would pass "within half a dozen miles of the community's complex at Chester-le-Street", whose extensive records (*Historia de Sancto Cuthberto*, *Libellus*) "record nothing of the battle or those involved" (Cavill 2022a §"Scots and Cumbrians"). The *Libellus* attributes the victory to St Cuthbert's patronage but locates nothing.
8. **The retreat problem.** "None of the places suggested by Wood is within realistic reach of the Humber after a day's battle and the retreat which followed: Went Hill and nearby Burghwallis are around twenty miles from the Humber, Brinsworth and White Hill much more"; a retreat up Margary 28b/Dere Street "would have passed within ten miles of York", where a Northumbria that had (on Wood's own showing) submitted to Anlaf would have sheltered him — yet "Anlaf escaped by sea" (Cavill 2022a). Grant makes the same objection from the Humber side (v4 p. 6).
9. **The "English soil" argument.** The poem's *ealgian* ("defend their land, treasures and homes") and Huntingdon's *defenderent patrie* imply "*Brunanburh* was English, was part of the English king's patrimony in a way that the Solway region never was at this time, nor Northumbria, and even in a way that east Mercia was not when Edmund overran it in 942" (Cavill 2011 §5.1, proof p. 324) — cutting against a Northumbrian frontier site north of the Don/Went.
10. **Coalition composition.** The Scots and Cumbrians had no interest in "a Norse York- and Dublin-controlled Britain" (Cavill 2014 p. 99); Wood "largely ignores the Scottish and Cumbrian contingents … whose strategic goals … might have been rather different from the secure establishment of Anlaf as king in York" (Cavill 2022a).

## Balance: honest assessment

**Strongest points FOR a Humber/Yorkshire location**
1. It is the only *positive* statement of a landing-place in any medieval source (John of Worcester, before c. 1140), and the notice is not obviously derivable from the Chronicle poem that John otherwise summarises. Darlington & McGurk confirm the Humber detail is "peculiar to" John, and it was accepted by every northern compiler who touched it (Howden, Melrose, Wendover, Langtoft) and by Skene, Arnold (1885), Hodgkin and others down to Wood.
2. The Durham tradition (*Wendune/Weondune*, *Brunnanwerc*, 615 ships) is independent of John and shows that northern writers of the early 12th century had a *different* name for the battle. That is a genuine northern interest in the battle, whatever its location.
3. The political logic — the York–Dublin axis, the *Uí Ímair* claim to York, the 927 expulsion, the 934 campaign, the 939–40 sequel, the Northumbrian/Danelaw sympathy for the coalition attested in several late sources, the border wars of 942–948 along the York–Doncaster road, the 1013/1066/1069 precedents — is coherent and is the best argument the Yorkshire case has. It is a strategic-probability argument, not a documentary one.
4. Wood's 1980 topographical case for Brinsworth (Roman road, Don ford, Templeborough, frontier forts, hill with Roman temple, wood and river answering *Egils saga*) remains the most concrete "fit" any Yorkshire site offers to the *dun/burh/heath/wood/river* descriptors — but Wood himself conceded the name does not fit.

**Strongest points AGAINST**
1. The name. Bromborough is the only place in Britain whose recorded forms descend from *Brunanburh*; no Yorkshire candidate has any recorded form resembling *Brun(n)anburh* or *We(o)ndun*. Brinsworth is *Brȳni's ford* (strong genitive, wrong name, wrong generic); Burghwallis is simplex *Burg*; Went Hill is *Weneteshil* (a *hyll*, with a disyllabic strong-genitive river-name), not a *dūn*. The "fort at the spring" reading of *Brunnanburh* founders on Old Norse grammar, on the West Saxon origin of the <nn> manuscripts, and on the absence of any *burh* + generic-water-term compound anywhere in England (Cavill 2022a–b).
2. The single-source problem. Everything Humber-related traces to one sentence in John of Worcester, whose "*ostium* + river + *ingreditur*" is a demonstrable formula (1013, 1066, 1069 and more than twenty other river-mouth entries) and who mis-states the flight and conflates the two Anlafs in the same stretch of text. Wood's lost "tenth-century York annals" are a hypothesis without a witness; Downham and Cavill show the "independent" later witnesses all copy John (verified above for Melrose, Howden and Wendover: their Latin is John's).
3. Strategy cuts both ways, and more sharply against. A Dublin fleet had a two-to-five-day crossing to the Mersey/Ribble/Lune versus a 1,200-mile autumn circumnavigation; the Scots and Cumbrians had no motive to install a Norse king at York; no source mentions York, a Northumbrian submission, or the Great North Road in 937; Chester-le-Street's exhaustive records notice nothing; Blunt finds no numismatic break at York; and if the allies were beaten twenty-plus miles from the Humber with a friendly York behind them, the poem's flight "over deep water" to Dublin from *Dingesmere* is inexplicable. The poem's own escape-route name points west.
4. The "English soil" and "forgotten battlefield" considerations pull opposite ways for the Went (a Northumbrian frontier site would be neither the English king's *patria* nor, arguably, forgettable to Yorkshire writers such as Thomas of Castleford, who ignores Brunanburh).

**Brinsworth vs. the Went**
- *Brinsworth (1980/1999)*: better topographical fit (hill, ford, Roman road, frontier, wood, temple), worse onomastic fit (the name is *Brȳni*'s, the hill is *wiht* not *wīh*); depends wholly on the Humber entry plus Wood's frontier reconstruction; a Roman temple podium is the only "heathen" datum, and Wood himself withdrew the *wīh* link. A respectable 1980 hypothesis that Wood has effectively abandoned.
- *The Went / Burghwallis / Barnsdale Bar (2013–20)*: gains the Great North Road, a *burh* and a famous spring, and a modern river-name superficially like *Wendun*; loses everything else — no *Brun-* name at all, a *hyll* not a *dūn*, a river-name whose early forms (*Wenet*) do not match, a "spring" etymology that violates the grammar of every witness, and a "frontier" moved five miles north of the 1980 line without new evidence. It is also further from the Humber than Brinsworth's advocates ever claimed the ships to be from the battlefield in 1937. Cavill's verdict — "implausible … fundamentally damaging, if not fatal" — has not been answered in print.

**Net.** The Humber/Yorkshire case survives only as a strategic possibility resting on one 12th-century sentence; as a specific site claim (Brinsworth or the Went) it is currently the weakest of the three serious modern hypotheses (Wirral, Solway/Burnswark, Yorkshire) on the philological evidence, while retaining the strongest *political* narrative. Any future revival would need (a) a pre-Conquest or independent 12th-century witness to the Humber, (b) a recorded *Brun(n)an-*/*We(o)n-* name in the Don–Aire corridor, or (c) archaeology. None exists as of September 2026.
