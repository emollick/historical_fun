# Every reported statement that John Hay wrote the Bixby letter

Prepared from a full reading of the sources listed at the end. Quotations are given exactly as printed in the cited source (obvious OCR breaks silently repaired). "Bullard" = F. Lauriston Bullard, *Abraham Lincoln and the Widow Bixby* (New Brunswick: Rutgers University Press, 1946), page numbers as printed (archive.org id `abrahamlincolnwi00bull`). "Emerson 2008" = Jason Emerson, "New Evidence from an Ignored Voice: Robert Todd Lincoln and the Authorship of the Bixby Letter," *Lincoln Herald* 110:2 (Summer 2008), pp. 86-116. "Burlingame 1995" = Michael Burlingame, "New Light on the Bixby Letter," *Journal of the Abraham Lincoln Association* 16:1 (Winter 1995), pp. 59-71.

Headline finding: **no statement by Hay claiming the Bixby letter survives in Hay's own hand or in any document written during his lifetime (he died 1 July 1905).** Every claim is hearsay with at least two intermediaries, first written down between 1922 and 1949, and first printed between 1926 (veiled) / 1933 (explicit) and 1995.

## 2. Narrative of the claims in the order they entered the record

1. **1866-1905: nothing.** Hay's letter to Herndon (5 Sept. 1866) says he wrote letters in Lincoln's name but does not mention Bixby (see `hay_own_statements.md`). Nicolay and Hay printed the letter as Lincoln's in *Abraham Lincoln: Complete Works* (1894) 2:600, with the footnote "This is one of Lincoln's most famous letters on account of its evident sincerity and the purity of its expression" (Gettysburg edition 1905, 10:274 n.; Emerson 2008 n. 28). Robert Lincoln asked Hay about the letter c. 1902 and was told Hay "knew nothing whatever of the source of the printed copy" (row I). On 19 Jan. 1904 Hay told Chandler the letter "is genuine" (row H). In November 1904 Morley stayed at the White House (row A).
2. **1912:** Morley tells Butler (per Butler).
3. **1922:** Jackson writes to Lucas (row C), the earliest surviving *written* record of any Hay claim, itself at least three removes from Hay.
4. **1926:** Barton's veiled "very high source" (row F); Barton rejects it.
5. **1927/28:** Spender's memoir hints at a "point" discussed with Hay; Spender later disowns Hay as the source (row G).
6. **14 May 1933:** NYT editorial: earliest explicit printed assertion; Ogden names Brownell (row B).
7. **1934:** Lucas prints the Jackson letter (row C).
8. **Feb. 1939 / Feb. 1941:** Wakefield's articles in *Hobbies* build on Lucas and Butler (Bullard p. viii; Wakefield 1948 pp. 8-10).
9. **1940:** Butler's memoir (row A). Bullard p. viii misdates vol. 2 to 1939; the title page says 1940 (Emerson 2008 n. 20; Wakefield 1947).
10. **1943-46:** Basler ("Who Wrote the 'Letter to Mrs. Bixby'?", *Lincoln Herald*, Feb. 1943, pp. 3-8), Barbee (*Tyler's Quarterly*, Jan. 1945), Bullard (1946) sift the claims; Coolidge (row D) enters the record only through Bullard.
11. **1949:** Mrs Beveridge's memo (row E), printed by Burlingame in 1995.

## 3. Overall assessment

- Earliest *datable* alleged statement by Hay: November 1904, to Morley (row A). Earliest *written* record of any claim: 16 Jan. 1922 (row C). Earliest explicit *publication*: 14 May 1933 (row B).
- Every chain has at least two human intermediaries and was first written down 17 to 44 years after Hay's death. None was recorded by the first recipient (Morley, Brownell, Page) in any surviving document; Morley's own memoir is silent.
- The two most circumstantial versions contradict each other in content: the Page version has Lincoln ordering Hay to draft and then sending Hay's draft unchanged because it captured his style; the Morley version has Hay explaining the absence of the manuscript from Lincoln's papers.
- Against them stand Hay's only written statement ("genuine," 1904), Hay's reported statements to Robert Lincoln (rows H-I), and the silence of Hay's diaries, letters, memoir essay ("Life in the White House in the Time of Lincoln," *Century*, Nov. 1890, reprinted in *Addresses of John Hay* (1906) pp. 323-354, which discusses Lincoln's reading, evenings and the children but says nothing about who drafted his letters) and family.
- Burlingame (1995 p. 71) concedes that none of the pieces "clinch the case" separately and relies on their cumulation plus the scrapbooks; Emerson (2008 pp. 108-109) argues the cumulative hearsay "cannot overcome the fact that Hay never told any such thing to Robert Lincoln."
