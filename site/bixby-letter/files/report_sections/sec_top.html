Historical mysteries · The Bixby letter

The Bixby letter

In November 1864 a Boston widow received a letter of condolence of 139 words signed by Abraham Lincoln. It became one of the most often reprinted of his writings. The manuscript has never been found, and for more than a century it has been said that his young secretary John Hay wrote it. This report reads the whole printed record, reruns the one statistical study of the wording, and ends with a narrow lean to Lincoln.

The mystery

In September 1864 a widow named Lydia Bixby came to the Boston office of William Schouler, the Adjutant General of Massachusetts, the officer who kept the state's military records, and showed him five letters. Each was from an army company commander, and each reported the death of one of her sons. Schouler told the story to the governor, John A. Andrew, who sent it on to the War Department in Washington with the request that the President himself write to her. On 21 November a letter of 139 words, dated from the Executive Mansion (as the White House was then called) and signed "A. Lincoln", was sent to Boston. Schouler put it into Mrs Bixby's hands on the morning of Friday 25 November, and the Boston Evening Transcript, an evening newspaper, printed it the same day.

The letter is short enough to quote in full, and section 1 does. It tells her that the President has been shown "a statement of the Adjutant General of Massachusetts, that you are the mother of five sons who have died gloriously on the field of battle", admits "how weak and fruitless must be any words of mine" against such a loss, and offers "the thanks of the Republic they died to save" and "the solemn pride that must be yours, to have laid so costly a sacrifice upon the altar of Freedom". Other newspapers reprinted it. It has been in anthologies of American prose ever since, and in 1998 it was read aloud in the film Saving Private Ryan. Its facts were wrong: two of the sons had been killed, one had deserted, one had been honourably discharged, and one had been captured and either deserted to the enemy or died in prison. The error was Schouler's, and it has no bearing on who wrote the sentences.

Who composed those sentences is the mystery. Lincoln signed the letter, but the manuscript disappeared. No draft, copy or letter-book entry has ever been found in his papers or anyone else's, the newspaper text is the only authority for the wording, and the "facsimiles" of a manuscript that dealers sold from 1891 onward are forgeries. Lincoln had two private secretaries, John G. Nicolay and John Hay, and Hay, then 26, drafted many of the letters that went out over the President's signature. Hay went on to a long public life (he was Secretary of State from 1898 until his death in 1905). During his last years a story went round Washington that he had written the Bixby letter, and after he died several men said that he had told them so in confidence.

Why it is still argued

None of those men wrote the story down while Hay lived. The earliest record of any of the claims is a letter of 1922, seventeen years after Hay's death and fifty-eight after the letter, and every one of them reached print through at least two intermediaries. Hay himself left the opposite on paper. In January 1904 he wrote to William E. Chandler, a former senator from New Hampshire who had asked him about a suspect facsimile, that "the letter of Mr. Lincoln to Mrs. Bixby is genuine", and around 1902 he told Robert Lincoln, the President's eldest son, that he knew nothing of where the printed text had come from and had never seen a draft or copy. He never mentioned the letter in his diary, in his letters or to his family.

Both answers fit the record, which is why the argument has lasted. For Hay: drafting letters for Lincoln's signature was his job; "beguile", the letter's most unusual word, is one he used often and Lincoln never; and a clipping of the letter is pasted into two of his scrapbooks. For Lincoln: he wrote his own letters of condolence (the two that survive from his presidency are in his hand); the request came to him from a governor through the Secretary of War, Edwin M. Stanton, and was the kind of letter he kept for himself; and Hay's own statements point away from Hay. Historians have divided along these lines since the 1940s. F. Lauriston Bullard, a Boston editor whose 1946 book Abraham Lincoln and the Widow Bixby is still the fullest study, argued for Lincoln, as did Joe Nickell, an investigator of questioned documents, in 1989, and Jason Emerson, a historian of the Lincoln family, in 2006 and 2008. Michael Burlingame, the author of the fullest modern life of Lincoln, has argued for Hay since 1995. Roy P. Basler, the editor of the standard edition of Lincoln's writings (1953), printed the letter as Lincoln's and called the claim for Hay "unproved".

In 2019 the question seemed to close. Jack Grieve, a linguist at the University of Birmingham, and six colleagues applied a technique called n-gram tracing to the letter. The technique counts how many of the short strings of letters and words in a questioned text (its "n-grams") also occur somewhere in each candidate's known writing, and gives the text to the candidate who shares more of them. Tested on more than 1,600 known texts by the two men, most of them shorter than 200 words, the technique almost never erred, and on the letter it chose Hay at every one of seventeen string lengths. The authors concluded that Hay "was almost certainly its primary author", and newspapers reported the mystery as solved.

How this report judged it

Two kinds of evidence exist, and this report works through both. The first is documentary: everything Hay is reported to have said, everything he wrote himself, what he told Robert Lincoln, the paper trail of the request from Boston to Washington, and the record of what Lincoln and Hay each wrote in the week the letter was sent. All of it is in print, in Bullard's book, in Burlingame's and Emerson's articles, in Hay's published letters and diary, and in Basler's edition, which records for every Lincoln document whose handwriting the surviving copy is in. We read these sources in full rather than through summaries, dated every reported claim and traced who told whom, and counted from Basler's notes what came from the President's desk that week and in whose hand.

The second kind is stylometry, the statistical comparison of writing styles, and here the report does three things. It rebuilds the 2019 technique from the paper's description and checks that it gives the answers the paper reported, on the letter and on the known texts used to validate it (it does). It then runs three tests the paper did not run, each asking whether the technique still works when the known Lincoln texts resemble the letter in date, in kind of writing (its register, in the linguists' term) and in the form in which they survive (manuscript or print, which we call the edition). A second implementation of the same tests, written from the paper by a different hand without sight of ours, was run as a check on the first. Last, it tries the technique and three other standard methods on the evidence that matters most: known letters that Hay wrote for Lincoln's signature, to see whether any method can tell Hay writing as Lincoln from Lincoln.

The result is a lean. The 2019 attribution reproduces, and its validation reproduces, but under the added tests it proves sensitive to the design of the comparison: the same technique sends between roughly a quarter and a half of Lincoln's own elevated wartime prose to Hay (28 to 48 percent, depending on the pieces and the pool), the Gettysburg Address among it, and no method we ran detects Hay writing in Lincoln's name. We therefore treat the stylometry as neutral. What remains is documentary and close to a balance. On Hay's side are the reported claims and the scrapbook clippings; on Lincoln's, the office's practice and Hay's own written statements. Weighing those items as explicit numbers (section 5 shows each one and what happens when it changes) gives Lincoln about 53 percent, Hay about 42, and another hand in the office about 5. Changing any one weight moves Lincoln between 26 and 75 percent, and taking the 2019 study at face value puts him at 11.

The verdict

Verdict: the question is unsettled, and the evidence leans narrowly to Lincoln.Our judgement: Lincoln composed it, about 53 percent; Hay, about 42; another hand, about 5

The one piece of evidence that had seemed to settle the question, the 2019 n-gram study, reproduces under its own design and passes the validation its authors ran. Under the added tests, with Lincoln texts closer to the letter in date, register and edition, its attribution proves sensitive to all three, and no method we ran tells the two men apart in this kind of writing. The second implementation reproduces the attribution and finds the register effect in the same direction and smaller: 19 of 63 pieces of Lincoln's own elevated wartime prose (cuts of about 140 words, matched to the letter's kind of writing) go to Hay there, 30 here, and the difference lies in the choice of Lincoln texts, not in the code (section 4). What remains is documentary, and it is close to a balance. No new document was found; everything new here is analysis of evidence already in print. This report does not show that Hay did not write the letter, or that Lincoln did; nothing found excludes either man. It shows that the stylometric case for Hay depends on the comparison design, and that the rest of the evidence is a near balance that leans, on the office's practice and Hay's own words, to Lincoln.

What this report covers

Section 1 gives the text of the letter as printed and traces the request from Boston to the White House. Section 2 sets out the documentary record: what Hay is reported to have said, what he wrote himself, and what he told Robert Lincoln. Section 3 reconstructs the President's desk in the week of 21 November 1864 from Basler's edition, item by item. Section 4 is the stylometry: the 2019 study replicated, then tested in ways its authors did not run, then rerun by a second, independent implementation. Section 5 weighs the documentary items as likelihood ratios, a way of giving each item a number that is explained there, and shows how far the result moves when any one of them changes. Section 6 states the case for Hay at full strength, in ten parts, with what was found against each. Section 7 lists what would overturn the verdict in either direction. Section 8 separates what was known from what this report adds and says where it falls short. Section 9 lists the sources and the saved code and data.
