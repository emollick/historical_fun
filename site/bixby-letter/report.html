Historical mysteries · The Bixby letter

The Bixby letter

In November 1864 a Boston widow received a letter of condolence of 139 words signed by Abraham Lincoln. It became one of the most often reprinted of his writings. The manuscript has never been found, and for more than a century it has been said that his young secretary John Hay wrote it. This report reads the whole printed record, reruns the one statistical study of the wording, and ends with a narrow lean to Lincoln.

The mystery

In September 1864 a widow named Lydia Bixby came to the Boston office of William Schouler, the Adjutant General of Massachusetts, the officer who kept the state's military records, and showed him five letters. Each was from an army company commander, and each reported the death of one of her sons. Schouler told the story to the governor, John A. Andrew, who sent it on to the War Department in Washington with the request that the President himself write to her. On 21 November a letter of 139 words, dated from the Executive Mansion (as the White House was then called) and signed "A. Lincoln", was sent to Boston. Schouler put it into Mrs Bixby's hands on the morning of Friday 25 November, and the Boston Evening Transcript, an evening newspaper, printed it the same day.

The letter is short enough to quote in full, and section 1 does. It tells her that the President has been shown "a statement of the Adjutant General of Massachusetts, that you are the mother of five sons who have died gloriously on the field of battle", admits "how weak and fruitless must be any words of mine" against such a loss, and offers "the thanks of the Republic they died to save" and "the solemn pride that must be yours, to have laid so costly a sacrifice upon the altar of Freedom". Other newspapers reprinted it. It has been in anthologies of American prose ever since, and in 1998 it was read aloud in the film Saving Private Ryan. Its facts were wrong: two of the sons had been killed, one had deserted, one had been honourably discharged, and one had been captured and either deserted to the enemy or died in prison. The error was Schouler's, and it has no bearing on who wrote the sentences.

Who composed those sentences is the mystery. Lincoln signed the letter, but the manuscript disappeared. No draft, copy or letter-book entry has ever been found in his papers or anyone else's, the newspaper text is the only authority for the wording, and the "facsimiles" of a manuscript that dealers sold from 1891 onward are forgeries. Lincoln had two private secretaries, John G. Nicolay and John Hay, and Hay, then 26, drafted many of the letters that went out over the President's signature. Hay went on to a long public life (he was Secretary of State from 1898 until his death in 1905). During his last years a story went round Washington that he had written the Bixby letter, and after he died several men said that he had told them so in confidence.

Why it is still argued

None of those men wrote the story down while Hay lived. The earliest record of any of the claims is a letter of 1922, seventeen years after Hay's death and fifty-eight after the letter, and every one of them reached print through at least two intermediaries. Hay himself left the opposite on paper. In January 1904 he wrote to William E. Chandler, a former senator from New Hampshire who had asked him about a suspect facsimile, that "the letter of Mr. Lincoln to Mrs. Bixby is genuine", and around 1902 he told Robert Lincoln, the President's eldest son, that he knew nothing of where the printed text had come from and had never seen a draft or copy. He never mentioned the letter in his diary, in his letters or to his family.

Both answers fit the record, which is why the argument has lasted. For Hay: drafting letters for Lincoln's signature was his job; "beguile", the letter's most unusual word, is one he used often and Lincoln never; and a clipping of the letter is pasted into two of his scrapbooks. For Lincoln: he wrote his own letters of condolence (the two that survive from his presidency are in his hand); the request came to him from a governor through the Secretary of War, Edwin M. Stanton, and was the kind of letter he kept for himself; and Hay's own statements point away from Hay. Historians have divided along these lines since the 1940s. F. Lauriston Bullard, a Boston editor whose 1946 book Abraham Lincoln and the Widow Bixby is still the fullest study, argued for Lincoln, as did Joe Nickell, an investigator of questioned documents, in 1989, and Jason Emerson, a historian of the Lincoln family, in 2006 and 2008. Michael Burlingame, the author of the fullest modern life of Lincoln, has argued for Hay since 1995. Roy P. Basler, the editor of the standard edition of Lincoln's writings (1953), printed the letter as Lincoln's and called the claim for Hay "unproved".

In 2019 the question seemed to close. Jack Grieve, a linguist at the University of Birmingham, and six colleagues applied a technique called n-gram tracing to the letter. The technique counts how many of the short strings of letters and words in a questioned text (its "n-grams") also occur somewhere in each candidate's known writing, and gives the text to the candidate who shares more of them. Tested on more than 1,600 known texts by the two men, most of them shorter than 200 words, the technique almost never erred, and on the letter it chose Hay at every one of seventeen string lengths. The authors concluded that Hay "was almost certainly its primary author", and newspapers reported the mystery as solved.

How this report judged it

Two kinds of evidence exist, and this report works through both. The first is documentary: everything Hay is reported to have said, everything he wrote himself, what he told Robert Lincoln, the paper trail of the request from Boston to Washington, and the record of what Lincoln and Hay each wrote in the week the letter was sent. All of it is in print, in Bullard's book, in Burlingame's and Emerson's articles, in Hay's published letters and diary, and in Basler's edition, which records for every Lincoln document whose handwriting the surviving copy is in. We read these sources in full rather than through summaries, dated every reported claim and traced who told whom, and counted from Basler's notes what came from the President's desk that week and in whose hand.

The second kind is stylometry, the statistical comparison of writing styles, and here the report does three things. It rebuilds the 2019 technique from the paper's description and checks that it gives the answers the paper reported, on the letter and on the known texts used to validate it (it does). It then runs three tests the paper did not run, each asking whether the technique still works when the known Lincoln texts resemble the letter in date, in kind of writing (its register, in the linguists' term) and in the form in which they survive (manuscript or print, which we call the edition). A second implementation of the same tests, written from the paper by a different hand without sight of ours, was run as a check on the first. Last, it tries the technique and three other standard methods on the evidence that matters most: known letters that Hay wrote for Lincoln's signature, to see whether any method can tell Hay writing as Lincoln from Lincoln.

The result is a lean. The 2019 attribution reproduces, and its validation reproduces, but under the added tests it proves sensitive to the design of the comparison: the same technique sends between roughly a quarter and a half of Lincoln's own elevated wartime prose to Hay (28 to 48 percent, depending on the pieces and the pool), the Gettysburg Address among it, and no method we ran detects Hay writing in Lincoln's name. We therefore treat the stylometry as neutral. What remains is documentary and close to a balance. On Hay's side are the reported claims and the scrapbook clippings; on Lincoln's, the office's practice and Hay's own written statements. Weighing those items as explicit numbers (section 5 shows each one and what happens when it changes) gives Lincoln about 53 percent, Hay about 42, and another hand in the office about 5. Changing any one weight moves Lincoln between 26 and 75 percent, and taking the 2019 study at face value puts him at 11.

1. The letter, and what survives of it

The text below is the one printed in the first edition of the Boston Evening Transcript on Friday 25 November 1864, the day the letter reached Mrs Bixby. It is the only authority for the wording. No manuscript, draft, copy or letter-book entry has ever been found, and the "facsimiles" sold from 1891 onward were traced in pencil and inked over on the wrong paper, as the autograph dealer Charles Hamilton showed in Great Forgers and Famous Fakes (1980, pp. 29 to 37). Basler reprinted the Transcript text in his edition, The Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln (1953), volume VIII, pages 116 to 117 (cited below as CW VIII, 116 to 117), with the note that "there is no reason to question the authenticity of the text of the letter which appeared in the Transcript and other contemporary sources." Every stylistic analysis, including ours, therefore rests on the 139 words as the Transcript's compositor set them in type.

Executive Mansion, Washington, Nov. 21, 1864.
Dear Madam: I have been shown in the files of the War Department a statement of the Adjutant General of Massachusetts, that you are the mother of five sons who have died gloriously on the field of battle.
I feel how weak and fruitless must be any words of mine which should attempt to beguile you from the grief of a loss so overwhelming. But I cannot refrain from tendering to you the consolation that may be found in the thanks of the Republic they died to save.
I pray that our Heavenly Father may assuage the anguish of your bereavement, and leave you only the cherished memory of the loved and lost, and the solemn pride that must be yours, to have laid so costly a sacrifice upon the altar of Freedom.
Yours, very sincerely and respectfully, A. Lincoln.
Mrs. Bixby. Boston Evening Transcript, 25 November 1864, as transcribed by Bullard (1946, pp. 26 to 27, n. 19) and Basler (CW VIII, 116 to 117). The forged facsimiles read "any word of mine", "tendering you", "republic" and "freedom"; those readings have no authority.

The facts in the letter were wrong. Two of Lydia Bixby's sons were killed (Charles, 3 May 1863; Oliver, 30 July 1864); one deserted, one was honourably discharged, and one was captured and either deserted to the enemy or died in prison (Basler, CW VIII, 117 n.). The error was Schouler's, and it has no bearing on who composed the sentences.

How the request travelled

The paper trail from Boston to the White House is complete and was printed by Schouler himself in 1868 (History of Massachusetts in the Civil War, I, 576 to 578) and re-examined in the National Archives by Bullard in 1946 (pp. 14 to 26). On 24 September 1864 Schouler reported to Governor Andrew on the case of Otis Newhall of Lynn, a father of five soldiers who had asked for one son's discharge, and added "a word in regard to a still more remarkable case": a widow who had shown him "five letters from five different company commanders, and each letter informed the poor woman of the death of one of her sons." Andrew forwarded the papers to the War Department with the sentence that set the letter in motion: "This is a case so remarkable, that I really wish a letter might be written her by the President of the United States, taking notice of a noble mother of five dead heroes so well deserved." The War Department received the papers on 26 and 27 September; Major Thomas M. Vincent, of the Adjutant General's office in Washington, asked Schouler for names and regiments on 1 October; Schouler replied on 12 October; Colonel Samuel Breck of the same office summarised the two cases for Stanton, the Secretary of War, on 28 October, ending "Governor Andrew desires that a letter may be written Mrs. Bixby by the President." The file was signed out to Stanton that day. On 5 November Schouler called on Lincoln in Washington and "passed an agreeable half hour with him" (Schouler's report for 1864, p. 606); nothing records that the Bixby case came up. The letter is dated Monday 21 November. Schouler placed it in Mrs Bixby's hands on the morning of Friday 25 November, and the Transcript had it in type the same day (Bullard, pp. 26, 50 to 51). How the letter travelled from Washington to Schouler's office is not documented.

Two things in this trail matter for authorship. The request reached Lincoln through the Secretary of War with a governor's explicit wish that the President write, which is the kind of request that came to the President personally rather than to the mail clerks. And the letter left no trace in the White House: no draft in Lincoln's hand, no draft in Hay's hand, no copy. Both facts are consistent with either author, as section 3 explains.

What Hay wrote, and what he told Robert Lincoln

The letter of Mr. Lincoln to Mrs. Bixby is genuine, is printed in our edition of his Works, and has been frequently re-published; but the engraved copy of Mr. Lincoln's alleged manuscript, which is extensively sold, is, in my opinion, a very ingenious forgery. Hay to William E. Chandler, Department of State, 19 January 1904. Contemporary copy with Chandler's endorsement "Aug. 6, 1909, gave original of above to W. D. Chandler", Chandler Collection, New Hampshire Historical Society (Bullard 1946, pp. 122 to 128, with facsimile). The original has not been found.

Burlingame reads "genuine" narrowly: the printed text is authentic, the facsimile is not, and Hay says nothing about who composed it (1995, p. 68). Bullard read it broadly, and pointed to Hay's letter of 4 June 1903 to a Mrs Langford, who had asked about two Lincoln letters, in which he volunteered that "the signatures, of course, are genuine, but the body of the letters was written by one of the clerks in the White House. It was not Mr. Nicolay or myself" (pp. 129 to 133). Both readings are possible. The 1903 letter shows that when Hay knew a body was not Lincoln's he said so unasked; the 1904 letter shows that ten months before he allegedly confessed to Morley he described the Bixby letter to Chandler as "the letter of Mr. Lincoln".

Robert Lincoln's testimony, published by Emerson in 2008 from Robert's letters to Isaac Markens, a New York writer on Lincoln, now at the Chicago History Museum, is the nearest thing to a first-hand record. Robert showed Hay a lithograph of the letter at the State Department around 1902. "Colonel Hay knew nothing whatever of the source of the printed copy of the letter in the Nicolay and Hay book. He suggested a curious thing; namely, that my father's handwriting was very easy to imitate ... and that perhaps this lithograph had been made from a forged document" (to Markens, 5 January 1917; the "book" is the edition of Lincoln's works that Nicolay and Hay published in 1894, which printed the letter). "Your suggestion that neither Nicolay nor Hay probably had any special knowledge of the letter at the time is correct. Hay himself told me so" (24 February 1917). Robert's secretary had written in 1908 that "both Mr. Nicolay and Mr. Hay stated that they had never seen any draft or copy of this letter" (Towers to a correspondent named Tweedale, 22 April 1908; Emerson 2008, pp. 103 to 105, n. 68). Robert also wrote that his father "never dictated correspondence; he sometimes wrote a document and had his draft copied by either Nicolay or Hay", and "I do not now recall having seen any letter written by either Nicolay or by Hay which was personally signed by him" (13 February 1918; Emerson 2008, pp. 106 to 107). The last statement is wrong: Basler's notes record dozens of letters and telegrams in Hay's hand signed by Lincoln, several of them in the weeks around the Bixby letter (section 3). Robert had spent the war years away from Washington, so his knowledge of office routine is second-hand, while his report of what Hay told him is first-hand.

Hay's job, in his own words

He wrote very few letters. He did not read one in fifty that he received. At first we tried to bring them to his notice, but at last he gave the whole thing over to me, and signed without reading them the letters I wrote in his name. He wrote perhaps half a dozen a week himself, not more. Hay to William H. Herndon, Lincoln's former law partner, who was gathering material for a biography; Paris, 5 September 1866 (printed in Emanuel Hertz, The Hidden Lincoln, 1938, p. 307; facsimile Gabriel Wells, 1940).

Burlingame quotes the first four sentences; Emerson points out that the fifth is usually left out (2008, p. 88). Both halves are true. Hay composed and signed for the President as a matter of routine, and Lincoln wrote letters himself every week, the ones he cared about. The question is which kind the Bixby letter was, and the 1866 letter cannot answer it.

The scrapbooks

Burlingame's strongest documentary find (1995, pp. 70 to 71) is that a clipping of the Bixby letter is pasted into two of Hay's scrapbooks, one at Brown University filled mostly with Hay's own poems and their reviews, one at the Library of Congress (vol. 54) filled with his anonymous journalism of 1860 to 1865. "It is difficult to understand why Hay would have pasted the Bixby letter in those scrapbooks, full of his own literary creations, unless he had composed it himself." Emerson examined the same volumes (2008, pp. 94 to 96): the Brown scrapbook also holds other letters signed by Lincoln and a French newspaper notice about Mary Lincoln; the Library of Congress volume holds clippings of Lincoln's letters to John Phillips, L. B. Wyman, F. B. Loomis and a New York committee; a third oversized volume is half filled with Lincoln's writings including photostats of the Gettysburg Address and the Second Inaugural. Hay had been collecting material for a history of the administration since 1861. The scrapbooks show that Hay kept a clipping of the letter, and nothing in them records why he kept it. We weigh them as weak evidence for Hay, and note that the Phillips letter kept beside it survives only in a copy "in an unidentified handwriting" that is not Hay's (Basler, CW VIII, 118 n.), which cuts against reading the scrapbook as a record of Hay's own compositions.

3. The President's desk in the week of 21 November 1864

Two arguments about that week recur in the literature. Burlingame holds that Lincoln was "unusually preoccupied" after the election and had no time for a condolence letter, citing Hay's note of 25 November to Charles S. Spencer of New York, who had asked for a letter from the President to read at a banquet: "I regret that the President was literally crowded out of the opportunity of writing you a note for yr. banquet. He fully intended to do so himself, and for that reason, I did not prepare a letter for him. But the crush here just now is beyond endurance" (Burlingame's edition of Hay's Civil War letters, At Lincoln's Side, 2000, p. 101, as quoted in Burlingame 1999). Bullard and Emerson hold that a request of this kind, sent by a governor through the Secretary of War, was exactly what Lincoln reserved for himself.

We counted what survives. Basler's edition lists every known Lincoln document with its manuscript source. For the thirteen full weeks from 3 October to 1 January the mean is 21 items a week. The week of 21 to 27 November has 15, the same as the week before and fewer than election week (23) or the week of the annual message (35). In that week Lincoln wrote in his own hand a 154-word letter to General Nathaniel P. Banks with a draft, five telegrams and several endorsements. The Spencer note shows that Hay drafted routine acknowledgments and that Lincoln kept some letters for himself even when he then failed to write them; it does not say which kind the Bixby letter was. The "crush" was callers, General Grant's visit on 23 November, the resignation of the Attorney General, Edward Bates, on 24 November, and the annual message to Congress read to the cabinet on 25 November; it was not correspondence.

What Lincoln wrote himself, and what Hay wrote for him

Basler's notes let us sort the war-era documents by hand. Our copy of volumes IV, VII and VIII holds 3,125 items. Of those dated from 18 May 1860 onward, 1,887 survive in Lincoln's own hand (Basler's codes ALS, ADfS and AES, for an autograph letter, draft or endorsement, signed), 1,094 survive in other hands or only in print, and 31 survive only in Hay's hand, with Lincoln's signature or without it. The Hay-hand items are telegrams, orders, a draft to the Shakers, acknowledgments of gifts (a congressman's salmon trout: to John F. Driggs, 3 February 1865), a declined invitation (Charles Butler, 9 November 1864) and a letter of thanks to Garrison (7 February 1865). Lincoln's two surviving letters of condolence, to the parents of Elmer Ellsworth, the young colonel and friend of his killed in May 1861 (25 May 1861), and to Fanny McCullough, the daughter of an Illinois friend killed that month (23 December 1862), are in his own hand, and so is his letter of religious reflection to Eliza Gurney, a Quaker minister who had visited him in 1862 (4 September 1864). Hay's known compositions in Lincoln's name are courteous and flat: "I could not ask, and no one could merit, a better reward" (to the Philadelphia poet George H. Boker, 26 October 1863); "I beg that you will convey to Mr. Williams my cordial thanks for his kind thoughtfulness" (to Driggs). Nothing Hay is known to have written for Lincoln is a letter of condolence. This record is the starting point of the weighing in section 5, and section 4 asks whether the stylometry changes it.

4. Stylometry, validated before it was used

The only published stylometric study of the letter is by Jack Grieve and six colleagues (Isobelle Clarke, Emily Chiang, Hannah Gideon, Annina Heini, Andrea Nini and Emily Waibel), "Attributing the Bixby Letter using n-gram tracing," in the journal Digital Scholarship in the Humanities 34 (2019), pp. 493–512. It concluded that Hay "was almost certainly its primary author." No replication has been published since, as far as we could find (section 8). We rebuilt the method from the paper's description, ran it on corpora we built ourselves, and then ran the tests the paper did not run: whether the method still works when the known texts resemble the letter in date and register, and whether its answer depends on which texts of each man are put in the comparison pool.

The corpora

Every Lincoln text comes from Basler's Collected Works, volumes I, IV, VII and VIII (the volumes for 1849 to 1859 and November 1861 to August 1863 are not available in full text; the 1849 to 1860 gap was filled from Arthur Brooks Lapsley's edition of 1905, an older printed collection). Basler's source codes let us keep only what survives in Lincoln's own hand (ALS, ADS, ADfS and the like) and set aside the items Basler says are in Hay's handwriting. Hay's writing comes from the three volumes of his letters and diary that his widow had printed privately in 1908 (Letters of John Hay and Extracts from Diary), from Thayer's biography, and from his books; only his 1860 to 1865 letters and diary are contemporary with the letter. Twenty other writers of the period supply a control set, used to check that a method separates Lincoln from Hay rather than from writers in general: William H. Seward, Salmon P. Chase and Gideon Welles of Lincoln's cabinet; Senator Charles Sumner; the secretaries John G. Nicolay and William O. Stoddard; the journalists Noah Brooks, Horace Greeley and George William Curtis; the orator Edward Everett; the writers Ralph Waldo Emerson, James Russell Lowell, John Lothrop Motley, Bayard Taylor, Mark Twain, Walt Whitman and Henry Adams; Generals Grant and Sherman; and Frederick Douglass.

Grieve's method, and what it reproduces

N-gram tracing takes every distinct string of one, two or three consecutive words and every distinct string of 3 to 16 consecutive characters (spaces included) in the questioned text, its word and character n-grams, and asks what share of them occurs anywhere in an equal-sized random sample of each candidate's writing. The candidate with the larger share wins, one vote per n-gram length, and the authors take the majority of the character 4- to 10-gram votes as the result. We reimplemented it exactly as described (capitals lowered, punctuation kept, no string running across a sentence boundary, samples of 0.95 times the smaller pool, and the random sampling repeated 10 times for validation and 50 times for the letter, with the shares averaged).

On known texts it behaves as the paper says. Under Grieve's design, Lincoln before May 1860 against all of Hay, leave-one-out attribution (each known text removed from its pool and attributed as if unknown) of 423 Lincoln and 588 Hay texts of 50 to 400 words is correct by the majority vote for every text. At single lengths the only misses are one Lincoln text at 3 characters and one or two Hay texts at 3 characters and at each length from 10 to 16. Under our own-hand design, Lincoln's autograph writing of 1860 to 1865 against Hay's letters and diary of the same years, the vote is right for 643 of the 645 Lincoln texts and for all 93 Hay texts. No single character length from 5 upward misses more than four Lincoln texts, and no length misses a Hay text. The 182 Lincoln and 31 Hay texts of 100 to 200 words are all attributed correctly by the vote. A 139-word text is not too short for the method when the text is an ordinary letter, telegram or diary entry. Grieve's confidence rests on this validation, and the validation holds.

The letter also comes out as the paper reports. Under Grieve's design all seventeen analyses give it to Hay: the 3-character analysis by four parts in a hundred thousand, the rest by margins of 0.01 to 0.14, with 0.04 to 0.10 for character 5- to 12-grams and 0.14 for word 2-grams, where the margin is Lincoln's share minus Hay's. (The paper reports the 5-word n-gram analysis for Lincoln on one phrase; we did not run 4- or 5-word n-grams.) Three tests the paper did not run

The authors had anticipated the second of these tests, on register. Their first presentation of the work (Aston University, 13 April 2016) noted that they had "not looked at detail at the effect of ... register variation" and that they "also need to test sensitivity to register variation", and its last slide listed testing "across different registers" as work still to do. The published paper says that "a systematic analysis of the writings of many authors and many registers is needed" and was being pursued. The tests below are new, and a second implementation of them, written independently, is reported at the end of this subsection.

Period. Grieve's Lincoln pool ends in May 1860 because the presidential correspondence is contaminated by secretaries. But Lincoln's style changed. When his own-hand writing of 1860 to 1865 is tested against Grieve's pool, 14 percent of the 182 texts of 100 to 200 words are attributed to Hay by the majority vote, and 18 percent of the 377 texts of 50 to 100 words; the figure for his pre-1860 texts is zero. Tested against each other, Lincoln before 1860 and Lincoln in the White House are separated by the method with 100 percent accuracy at character lengths 5 to 16. The method sees a difference between the two Lincolns as clearly as it sees a difference between Lincoln and Hay. The Bixby letter, tested against the two Lincoln periods alone, sits between them.

Register. Both pools are dominated by business prose, and the letter is not business prose. On the register-matched pieces the picture changes. Under Grieve's design the majority vote gives 30 of the 63 Lincoln pieces to Hay, including the Gettysburg Address, the Ellsworth letter and the McCullough letter, while 56 of the 57 Hay pieces go to Hay. The second implementation, on the same pieces with a smaller Lincoln pool, gives 19 of the 63 to Hay; the cause is traced below. At character lengths 4 to 6 the method is right about Lincoln's elevated prose only 21 to 38 percent of the time. Under the own-hand design the vote misses 17 of the 63 Lincoln pieces and none of Hay's. Short character strings, which carry function words (the, of, and), word endings and spelling, are the ones that fail; they mark the register, and the register of an elevated Lincoln text looks like Hay to a pool of Lincoln's telegrams. Long strings, which carry phrases, recover: at character lengths 11 to 16 the own-hand design is right about 89 to 94 percent of Lincoln's pieces. Figure 2 plots, for each string length and each design, how often the method attributes the register-matched pieces correctly, beside its rate on ordinary known texts.

The register set has limits of its own. The 63 Lincoln pieces come from 30 documents, and the 57 Hay pieces from three: two eulogies of 1861 and one short letter. The validation used below, which leaves out one source document at a time, therefore tests the classifier on only three Hay sources, and a Hay eulogy is not a Hay condolence letter. The test shows that the 2019 design misreads between a third and a half of these pieces of Lincoln's elevated prose, the share depending on the pool. It cannot show how that design would treat a wider range of Hay's writing in this register, because no such range survives from the 1860s.

Edition. Lincoln's own-hand texts are transcribed from manuscripts and keep his habits ("can not," "it's," dashes); the letter survives only in print, and the Lapsley texts used for 1849 to 1860 and for the 1862 to 1863 register pieces are print. We found 95 Lincoln texts that appear in both editions. The print version of the same words scores 0.010 to 0.016 nearer to Hay than the manuscript version at every n-gram length (at character 4-grams, in 85 of 95 pairs), and its majority-vote accuracy falls from 0.93 to 0.74 at 4-grams. In every design whose Lincoln pool is built from manuscripts, a printed Lincoln text therefore scores slightly nearer Hay than the same text in manuscript would.

A second implementation, and why its rate is lower

A second implementation of n-gram tracing was written from the paper alone, by a different hand, without sight of our code or our results. It rebuilt the two pools from our corpus files under the paper's design and reran the tests. Its report is cited in section 9 and its files are saved beside ours. On the letter it agrees with the paper and with us: Hay at all seventeen lengths, by margins of 0.002 at 3 characters to 0.145 at word 2-grams. Its leave-one-out validation of all 1,397 texts in its pools is right by the vote for 1,380, with the paper's accuracy curve (its F1 score, a standard accuracy measure that counts both wrong attributions and missed ones) at every length (the paper: all 1,662 of its texts; ours: all 1,011 of 50 to 400 words); its misses are Lincoln's verse, his 1842 eulogy, his temperance address and reported speech. On our 63 register pieces it sends 19 to Hay by the vote, 30 percent, against our 30, 48 percent; on 97 pieces of its own from 38 Lincoln documents of 1861 to 1865, 27, 28 percent. It agrees with us on which lengths fail (4 to 6 characters, at 25 to 44 percent accuracy) and on the Gettysburg and Ellsworth pieces, and it disagrees on the McCullough letter, which it gives to Lincoln four votes to three. Under period-matched pools of its own it finds what we found under the own-hand design: the letter splits, five lengths to two for Lincoln on the character vote and nine to eight for Hay over all seventeen, and all four of Hay's letters for Lincoln's signature, and Hay's own condolence, are called Lincoln.

The gap between 30 and 19 is in the Lincoln pool, not in the code. The two implementations agree piece by piece on 48 of the 63; on twelve of the fifteen they disagree about, one side or the other is a vote of four to three. Our code on the second implementation's exact pool gives 19 of 63 under the paper's rule for drawing the random samples of each man's writing, repeated 50 times, 19 under our rule repeated 20 times, and 20 repeated 10 times. Its code on our pool gives 26. Repeating our own run with three different random draws gives 30, 30 and 28, and under the paper's sampling rule (the whole smaller pool, repeated 50 times) 29. The one difference that matters is a set of 19 items from Lapsley's third and fourth volumes: 83,000 words of Lincoln's 1858 campaign speeches and his side of the seven debates with Stephen A. Douglas, his opponent for the Senate, which the second implementation dropped because Lapsley prints Douglas's letters and some interrogatories inside a few of them (a few hundred words at most that are not Lincoln's). With those 19 items removed, our code gives 18 to 20 of 63; with its selection of texts applied to our own copies, 18; and when the second implementation restored the substantive Lapsley items to its own pool, with Douglas's words cut out, its code gave 27 of 63. On its 97 pieces, our code gives 28 with its pool (its own figure is 27) and 40 with ours. Nothing else moved the figure.

The mechanism is the method's sampling rule. Each candidate is represented by a random sample the size of the smaller pool, so adding the debates raises Lincoln's pool from about 159,000 to about 243,000 words and Hay's sample by the same amount. Both halves of that matter. At an equal sample of 159,000 words, a Lincoln sample that includes the debates in proportion misses 24 of the 63 pieces where one without them misses 20: stump oratory supplies few of the strings of a condolence letter. Raising both samples to 243,000 takes the figure to 29: the extra words of Hay's mixed prose cover more of those strings than the same number of extra words of Lincoln's oratory do. (Raising Hay's sample alone, with Lincoln's held at 159,000, sends 54 of the 63 to Hay, which is why the method insists on equal samples.) Accuracy at 4 to 9 characters falls by 0.06 to 0.14 at every length when the debates are in. Which pool is the right one is a question about the paper's design, not about either implementation. The paper's Lincoln corpus, 1,085 texts and 400,747 words from Basler's first four volumes with texts up to 17,003 words long, included his speeches, and its samples were 261,000 words each, the size of its Hay corpus; our pool with the debates, 243,000 words, is the nearer reconstruction on both counts, and the second implementation's, 192,000, is the more cautious about Douglas. Neither is the paper's corpus, which was not obtainable. We therefore report a range: under the paper's design between 19 and 30 of the 63 register-matched Lincoln pieces go to Hay, 30 to 48 percent, and between 28 and 40 of the second implementation's 97, and the rate rises with the amount of Lincoln's oratory in the pool. The attribution therefore depends on which Lincoln texts are in the pool, and the range above measures the size of that dependence.

The letter under the own-hand design

Under the own-hand design the seventeen analyses split, eight to Hay and nine to Lincoln. Character 3- to 8-grams, 10-grams and word 1-grams go to Hay, by margins of 0.002 to 0.046; character 9-grams, 11- to 16-grams and word 2- and 3-grams go to Lincoln, by margins of 0.000 to 0.018. The 4- to 10-gram vote is Hay, six lengths to one. A rerun with punctuation removed gives the same split. Read against the register-matched pieces, the short-string margins are typical of Hay's eulogies and outside the range of Lincoln's elevated pieces (at 4-grams the letter is below every one of the 63 Lincoln pieces), and the long-string margins are typical of Lincoln's pieces and outside Hay's (at 15-grams it is above every one of the Hay pieces). A logistic regression model (a standard statistical classifier) trained on the full set of margins of the 120 register pieces, validated by leaving out one source document at a time, classifies 92 to 95 percent of the Lincoln pieces and 89 to 90 percent of the Hay pieces correctly, and gives the letter a probability of Hay of 0.62 with all Hay pieces and 0.50 when the sixteen Hay pieces that are more than a quarter quotation are dropped. The same model trained under Grieve's design gives 0.98, and under a third design (all of Lincoln's autograph writing against all of Hay's) 0.61 to 0.73, a design that also gives the Gettysburg Address to Hay at 0.79 to 0.87.

What the letter shares with each man

The margins can be read directly. The strings of the letter that occur in Lincoln's own-hand war writing and in none of Hay's are the office's: "in the files of the War Department," "a statement of the Adjutant General," "that may be found in," "should attempt to," "of mine which," "and leave you," "tendering to you," "and respectfully." Nearly all of Hay's are in his post-war books, not his war-time letters. The letter's business idiom is Lincoln's office idiom, which any drafter in that office would have used; its elegiac idiom is one that Lincoln's surviving writing hardly ever had occasion for.

What 139 words can tell

On ordinary texts the answer is "a great deal": at 100 to 200 words the methods are 95 to 100 percent accurate. On texts of this letter's register the same methods are 73 to 95 percent accurate, the two designs give opposite answers for the letter, and the genuine Hay condolence and the four genuine Hay-for-Lincoln letters pass as Lincoln under every method but one, which catches one of the four. Taking the methods together, the likelihood ratio the stylometry supports for Hay against Lincoln lies somewhere between one to three and three to one. We treat it as one. On our tests the 2019 result depends on the corpus design as much as on the letter. A second implementation, written independently, agrees on the direction; the size of the register effect depends on the Lincoln pool, as shown above. Neither implementation used the paper's own corpus.

5. Weighing it

The question has three answers: Lincoln composed the letter; Hay composed it and Lincoln signed it, perhaps after touching it; or someone else in the office did. The third is a residual. Nicolay was ill in St Louis from 17 to 30 November, and the clerks Hay mentioned to Mrs Langford are recorded only as writing the bodies of ordinary letters. We give it about 5 percent and do not analyse it further.

Between the first two the evidence is documentary, and each item is weak. We set out the items with the weight we give each, as a likelihood ratio: a number saying how many times more likely the item would be if one man wrote the letter than if the other did (a ratio of 2.4 for Hay means the item is 2.4 times more likely under Hay's authorship). A reader who weighs an item differently can see what changes. The ratios are judgments. The starting point, the prior before any item is counted, is the practice of the office as the record shows it: Lincoln wrote the letters that mattered to him personally, and the two condolence letters that survive from his presidency, to the Ellsworths in 1861 and to Fanny McCullough in 1862, are in his hand, while Hay wrote most of the rest and was writing routine letters for him that same week. We start at 65 percent for Lincoln.

6. The strongest case for Hay

Michael Burlingame built the case for Hay in 1995 and 1999, and the 2019 study added a statistical argument to it. Burlingame's articles and the 2019 study were read in full; his 2000 chapter in At Lincoln's Side could not be obtained and is cited through Emerson and through Burlingame's own later summaries of it. The case has ten parts. Each is stated here as its advocates state it, followed by what we found.

  1. Hay's job. Hay to Herndon, 1866: Lincoln "gave the whole thing over to me, and signed without reading them the letters I wrote in his name." Basler's notes confirm the practice in the week itself. Hay drafted Lincoln's letter of 9 November declining Charles Butler's dinner, referred letters to Stanton for him on 23 and 27 November, acknowledged a gift of beef on 25 November, and wrote the body of the letter to Henry F. Harrington of 28 November (CW VIII, 97 n., 122, 568 to 569). Found: all true, and all routine. The same 1866 letter says Lincoln wrote "perhaps half a dozen a week himself", and Basler's source codes show what those were: letters to generals, governors and editors, telegrams, endorsements, and both surviving condolence letters of his presidency. The practice makes Hay's authorship possible but does not make it likely for this letter.
  2. The reported confessions. Hay is said to have told John Morley (1904), W. C. Brownell and Walter Hines Page (before 1905) that he wrote the letter; two further stories are anonymous. Burlingame concedes that none of these "clinch the case" and relies on their number (1995, p. 71). Found: none is first-hand, none was written down before 1922, and the one tied to a datable occasion, Morley's visit of November 1904, is contradicted by Hay's own letter to Chandler ten months earlier, which calls it "the letter of Mr. Lincoln", and by Hay's diary of those days, which records Morley's visits and says nothing of the letter. The story was "afloat in Washington" by 1904 (Bullard, p. 127), so the chains are not independent witnesses. Section 2 sets them out.
  3. Lincoln was too busy. Hay to Charles S. Spencer, 25 November 1864: the President "was literally crowded out of the opportunity of writing you a note ... the crush here just now is beyond endurance." Burlingame reads the letter as showing that Lincoln lacked time for the Bixby letter as well, so that "for the president's signature Hay wrote a letter of condolence" (the unedited manuscript of his Abraham Lincoln: A Life, II, ch. 35). Found: the same passage says Lincoln "fully intended to do so himself, and for that reason, I did not prepare a letter for him." Hay did not draft the banquet note Lincoln had reserved to himself, and it went unwritten. Basler's items for 21 to 27 November number 15, against 23 in election week and 35 in the week of the annual message (section 3). On 21 November itself Lincoln wrote a telegram and an endorsement in his own hand.
  4. The words. "Beguile" occurs in Hay's writing at least thirteen times by Burlingame's itemised count and "at least thirty" by his later one, and never in Lincoln's Collected Works. "I cannot refrain from" is in Hay's letters of 1858 and 1864. "Heavenly Father", "gloriously", "altar", "anguish" and "costly" are said to be Hay's words. Found: the table in section 4 gives the counts in the corpora. "Beguile" is Hay's: none in 338,000 words of Lincoln, eight in 502,000 words of Hay, and more in journalism outside our corpus. The rest divide. "I cannot refrain from" is Lincoln's too (to Salmon P. Chase, then governor of Ohio, 21 September 1859), and the 2019 study's own table lists "cannot refrain from" and "tendering" among the strings found only in its Lincoln corpus. "Our Heavenly Father" is in Lincoln's proclamations of 1862 and 1864. "Tendering to you", "the files of the War Department" and "should attempt to" occur in Lincoln's own-hand writing and nowhere in Hay's. "The altar of", "solemn pride" and "gloriously" occur in Hay's writing and nowhere in Lincoln's, and nearly all of Hay's uses are from after 1865. The letter's office idiom is Lincoln's and its elegiac idiom is Hay's. The counts on each side are single digits and carry little weight.
  5. Hay the mimic. "Hay would obviously try to imitate the president's voice while composing the Bixby letter, and Hay was a gifted literary mimic" (Burlingame 1995, p. 65); in the Page version Lincoln sent the draft unchanged because Hay "had so perfectly captured his style". Found: this cannot be tested, and it works in both directions: every Lincoln-like feature becomes good mimicry and every Hay-like feature a lapse. Jean Pival's syntactic markers (the letter's "should" where Hay would write "would", its active voice, its two passives) are the features a mimic is least likely to reproduce, and the letter has them Lincoln's way (Nickell 1989, p. 139). We give the argument no weight either way.
  6. The scrapbooks. A clipping of the letter is pasted into two of Hay's scrapbooks, one of them otherwise filled with his own poems and their reviews. Found: section 2. Both volumes also hold Lincoln material Hay did not write, and the Phillips letter kept beside the Bixby clipping survives only in a hand that is not Hay's. They are weak evidence for Hay, and the best documentary item on his side.
  7. The 2019 study. All seventeen n-gram analyses attributed the letter to Hay, and the method had attributed over 1,600 known texts of both men, most of them shorter than 200 words, with almost no errors. Found: section 4. The result reproduces under the study's own design. It also passes the validation the authors ran. On register-matched known texts, a test the authors did not run, the same design sends 30 of 63 pieces of Lincoln's wartime prose, the Gettysburg Address among them, to Hay. The design compares Lincoln before 1860 with Hay's whole life, and its Lincoln corpus is printed text, whereas the Lincoln texts in our own-hand design are manuscripts. Under the own-hand design the n-gram evidence splits eight lengths to nine, and under that design 18 of the 19 Basler items in Hay's own hand are called Lincoln's.
  8. Hay's "genuine" read narrowly. Hay's 1904 letter answers a question about the forged facsimile, so "genuine" means only that the printed text is authentic (Burlingame 1995, p. 68). Found: the reading is possible. Hay's 1903 letter to Mrs Langford shows that when he knew a body was a clerk's he said so unasked. We weigh the 1904 letter at a ratio of 1.2 for Lincoln, which is nearly nothing.
  9. Butler's reasons. Lincoln "wrote very few letters that bore his signature"; Hay could imitate Lincoln's hand "in well-nigh perfect fashion" (Butler 1940, II, 392). Found: handwriting is beside the point when no manuscript survives. Butler attributed the claim about imitation to Robert Lincoln, who wrote the opposite to Markens in 1917, and Butler's other stories from Robert Lincoln were shown false by David Mearns of the Library of Congress (Emerson 2008, p. 91).
  10. The historians' lean. Tyler Dennett, Hay's biographer (1940), James G. Randall and Richard N. Current in the last volume of Randall's life of Lincoln (1955), and the editor of the Lincoln Herald, the journal that printed Emerson's article (2008), leaned to Hay, and after 2019 the press reported the question as settled. Found: the lean rests on the nine items above. Basler's headnote of 1953 kept the question open, "with the claim remaining unproved" (CW VIII, 117 n.).

The case for Hay is cumulative and circumstantial. Its testimony is hearsay throughout. Its two strongest items, the scrapbooks and the 2019 study, each fail a specific test. It remains a respectable hypothesis: nothing we found excludes it, and the verdict above gives it about two chances in five.

7. What would overturn the verdict

The verdict is a lean of 56 to 44 between two men, on documentary evidence that is weak on both sides. Small additions to the evidence would move it; the following would move it substantially.

Toward Hay

  • A draft or copy of the letter in Hay's hand, or a diary entry, letter or memorandum by Hay claiming it, in the Hay Papers at Brown University or the Library of Congress, or in the papers of Morley, Page or Brownell. None of the three first recipients is known to have written the story down. A letter from any of them dated before 1922 would turn hearsay into testimony.
  • The original manuscript in Hay's hand over Lincoln's signature, as the letter to Harrington of 28 November 1864 is. A manuscript in Lincoln's hand would not settle the matter the other way: Robert Lincoln said his father copied out his own drafts, and a fair copy of a Hay draft would look the same.
  • A use by Hay, before November 1864, of any of the letter's rarer phrases: "beguile you from the grief", "the altar of freedom", "the solemn pride", "assuage the anguish". The uses we found are all later.
  • An identification of the "unidentified handwriting" of the Phillips copy of 21 November as Hay's. That would make the day's other composed letter his and strengthen the reading of the scrapbook as a record of his own work.
  • A stylometric method that still attributes the letter to Hay after it has been validated on Hay's known letters for Lincoln's signature and on register-matched Lincoln texts. We could not build one. A corpus of Hay's condolence letters from the 1860s, of which we have one, would be the place to start.
  • A rerun of the register test that finds no register effect. That would restore the 2019 study's standing and move the verdict toward Hay. The second implementation finds the effect, at a lower rate than ours (section 4).

Toward Lincoln

  • The original manuscript in Lincoln's hand with corrections, which is how Robert Lincoln said his father wrote "a careful letter".
  • A statement by Hay, in his hand, that Lincoln wrote it, or a first-hand denial to any of the men who later claimed the opposite.
  • A use by Lincoln of "beguile" in any surviving document. Burlingame reports that the word occurs nowhere in the Collected Works (1995, p. 65); we confirmed this for the four volumes we could read.

Two things would not change the verdict. More reported confessions of the same kind, at two or more removes and written down decades later, would add little, because the story was in circulation by 1904 and the chains share a source. A further n-gram study under the 2019 design would add little, because both implementations of our tests find that design misreading between roughly a quarter and a half of Lincoln's elevated prose (28 to 48 percent, depending on the pieces and the pool). A rerun on the paper's own corpus, by a third party, is the check that remains.

8. What this report adds, and where it falls short

No new evidence is presented here: no document was found that was not already in print. The documentary case on both sides was assembled between 1946 and 2008. What is new is analysis of old evidence, mainly the added tests of the 2019 design. A second implementation of those tests, written independently, reproduces the attribution, finds the register effect in the same direction, and finds it smaller (section 4).

Established before this report

  • Bullard (1946): the route of the request, the Transcript text, the forged facsimiles, the chains behind the Morley, Brownell and Page stories, Hay's letters to Chandler (1904) and Langford (1903), and the argument from Lincoln's other writing.
  • Nickell and Pival (1989): the parallels in Lincoln's own letters ("tender", "our Father in Heaven", "sacredness of your sorrow"), the alliteration, and the syntactic markers ("should", the active voice) that favour Lincoln.
  • Burlingame (1995, 1999): the five reported claims assembled, "beguile" and the other Hay-marked words, the Spencer letter, and the scrapbooks at Brown and the Library of Congress.
  • Emerson (2006, 2008): Robert Lincoln's Markens letters, with Hay's statement that he knew nothing of the letter's source; the counter-argument on the scrapbooks; the fifth sentence of the Herndon letter.
  • Grieve and colleagues (2019): n-gram tracing as a method for very short texts, its validation on 1,662 known texts, and the attribution of the letter to Hay under their design.
  • Grieve and colleagues (2016 presentation): the caution, in their own words, that the method's sensitivity to register variation still needed testing.
  • Thomas Proisl and colleagues, computational linguists in Germany (2018): that n-gram tracing is "highly sensitive to the choice of authors and texts in the comparison corpus".

Added here

  • The first independent replication of the 2019 attribution, with the method re-implemented from the paper's description and validated on the same kinds of texts.
  • Three tests of that design's sensitivity, which its authors had named as untested: period (Lincoln's wartime own-hand texts of 100 to 200 words go to Hay 14 percent of the time under a pre-1860 Lincoln corpus), register (30 of 63 register-matched Lincoln pieces go to Hay in our implementation and 19 of 63 in the second, among them in both the Gettysburg Address and the Ellsworth condolence), and edition (print versions of 95 Lincoln texts sit nearer Hay than their autograph versions at every string length).
  • A corpus design built from Basler's source codes, so that Lincoln's side holds only what survives in his hand from 1860 to 1865, and a register-matched validation set of Hay's own war prose of 1861.
  • The test that matters most: under the own-hand design 18 of the 19 Basler items in Hay's hand, three of Hay's four known letters for Lincoln's signature, and Hay's own condolence of 1864 are called Lincoln by the vote, and the classifiers and Delta call all five of the known Hay texts Lincoln. The methods cannot detect the hypothesis at issue, so a result on the letter under them is not evidence for either man.
  • A word and phrase table across four corpora that places the individual examples of Burlingame and Nickell in their base rates.
  • The documentary items weighed as explicit likelihood ratios, with the sensitivity of the result to each published beside the verdict.
  • A count of Basler's items for every week of the autumn of 1864, which puts a number on Bullard's answer to the "too busy" argument.
  • The observation that the Phillips copy of 21 November 1864, kept beside the Bixby clipping in Hay's scrapbook, is in a hand that is not Hay's. We did not find this pointed out before.

Since 2019

We found no published replication, re-analysis or rebuttal of the 2019 attribution between its appearance and September 2026, in the journals, in the 41 works citing it that the Semantic Scholar database lists, or in the Lincoln periodicals we could reach. The only independent test of the method is Proisl and colleagues (2018), on literary texts, published before the journal version of the study. A new book, Jan Cigliano Hartman's Lincoln's Speechwriter (2026), argues for a large Hay hand in Lincoln's prose generally; the Lincoln scholar Harold Holzer's review of it (on the history website Emerging Civil War, 23 July 2026) calls the Bixby question open and names the scrapbook as the principal argument for Hay. We did not read the book. The journal and book databases Project MUSE, JSTOR, HathiTrust and Google Books could not be searched, so the search is not complete.

Where this report falls short

  • No archive was visited and no new document was found. The Hay Papers, the Morley papers and the Bixby family papers were not searched.
  • Basler's volumes II, III, V and VI (1848 to 1863) were lending-only and are absent from the Lincoln corpus. The own-hand design covers 1860 to 1865 from volumes IV, VII and VIII, and the pre-1860 design uses volume I and the Lapsley edition. The McCullough condolence of 1862 enters the register set from a printed text.
  • Burlingame's 2000 chapter, the fullest statement of the case for Hay, was not available and is cited through Emerson and Burlingame's own later summaries; Edward Steers's Lincoln Legends (2007), which discusses the letter, was not read.
  • Hay's letter to Spencer of 25 November 1864 is quoted from Burlingame's quotations of it, which differ in one word between his 1999 article and his later book.
  • The register-matched Hay set is journalism and memoir of 1861, because Hay's surviving condolence writing from the 1860s is a single letter of 55 words. A true test of the hypothesis would need Hay condolences that do not exist in print.
  • The impostors method (a check that asks whether a candidate still wins when many unrelated writers are added to the comparison), planned as a further check for section 4, did not finish and is not reported.
  • The added tests exist in two implementations, written independently of each other. They agree on the direction of the register effect and differ on its rate, and section 4 traces the difference as far as we could. Neither used the paper's own corpus, which was not obtainable, and no one else has rerun them.
  • The likelihood ratios in section 5 are judgments. The sensitivity table shows how far the result moves when any one of them is changed; a reader who disagrees with several at once can end up on the other side.
