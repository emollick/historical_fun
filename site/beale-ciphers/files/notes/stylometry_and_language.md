# The "Beale" letters and the 1885 narrative: computational stylometry and language dating

All code is in `code/` (prefixes `stylo_`, `lang_`), all machine-readable outputs in `results/`, the
control corpus (raw downloads, cut samples, manifest with source URLs) in `data/controls/`.
Every number below can be regenerated by running the scripts listed in "Reproducibility".

## 1. Conclusions first

**A. Same hand?** Yes, with high probability. On every distance measure that discriminates authors
reasonably well in this corpus (Burrows/Cosine Delta on 200-500 most frequent words, pronoun-free
Delta, character 4-grams), the three "Beale" letters (2,716 words, dated 1822) and the anonymous
author's own narrative (3,954 words, written c. 1885) are closer to each other than **99-100 % of the
6,352 pairs of samples by different control authors**, and as close as typical pairs of samples by the
**same** author. With the texts cut to the same length as the controls (2,000 words each), the
letters-narrative distance is matched by 0.2-1.0 % of different-author pairs and by 15-31 % of
same-author pairs; the likelihood ratio (same hand : different hands) from kernel-density estimates is
about 10-25 for the length-matched pair and 70-650 for the full-length pair. Pronoun-free and
function-word-only variants give the same answer, so the result is not driven by shared subject
matter or by the first-person register of letters. Nine pairs of different control authors who wrote
on the *same topic* (e.g. Gregg vs. Thomas James on the Santa Fe trade, three Lynchburg local
historians, Cooke's Life of Lee vs. Lee's own letters) all come out farther apart than the Beale pair.
The narrative is the nearest "author" to the letters by a wide margin (Cosine Delta 0.69 vs. 0.90 for
the runner-up, Jefferson), and the letters are the nearest text to the narrative (0.55 vs. 0.85 for
the runner-up, Eggleston).

Turning the likelihood ratios into a probability requires a prior. With even odds a priori, the
length-matched results give P(same hand) ≈ 0.9-0.96; the full-length results give > 0.98. I would
state the result as: **the observed stylistic closeness would arise between two different authors in
roughly 1 case in 100 to 1 in 500 in a corpus of 27 nineteenth-century American authors, but is
ordinary for two texts by one author.** The important non-stylometric caveat is that the anonymous
author admits he "read over and over again the letters ... endeavoring to impress each syllable they
contained on my memory", so an alternative reading (a hoaxer imitating letters he had memorised, or
a narrator whose prose was contaminated by them) cannot be excluded by stylometry alone — but the
shared traits are habits nobody imitates deliberately (rates of *until*, *myself*, *such*, *may*,
*each*, untriggered reflexive pronouns, negative infinitival passives), which is what one expects
from a single writer.

Specific corroboration of Nickell/Pival: the "misuse of reflexive pronouns" that Jean Pival reported
in 1982 is real and rare. Untriggered reflexives (*like myself*, *not so reliable as yourself*, *for
myself and family*, *as sanguine as himself*, *more fortunate than myself*) occur 3.7 and 2.3 times
per 1,000 words in the letters and the narrative; the control mean is 0.4 (sd 0.5) and **only 1 of 115
control samples (a Jefferson letter sample) reaches the lower of the two Beale rates.** The
construction *not/never to be + participle* (*not to be delivered*, *never to be realized*, *not to be
resisted*, *not to be numbered with the living*) is at 0.4-0.5 per 1,000 in both against a control
mean of 0.05. On the other hand the crude punctuation/syntax rates that summaries of Nickell's article
emphasise (semicolons, relative clauses, infinitives, conjunctions, sentence length) barely
discriminate authors at all in this calibration (likelihood ratio ≈ 0.7-0.9, i.e. uninformative), and
the two texts actually *differ* on semicolons (1.5 vs 7.1 per 1,000) and on *that* (8.5 vs 15.2).

**Poe** is excluded. The Gold-Bug's nearest neighbours are Bagby, Cable and Twain; Poe's letters'
nearest neighbours are Twain's letters, Lee and Randolph; Poe's samples sit at ordinary
different-author distances from both Beale texts (e.g. 0.95 and 0.90 on the pronoun-free measure,
against 0.55-0.70 for letters vs. narrative). This agrees with Poundstone's 1983 test as summarised on
Wikipedia.

**Morriss's quoted statement** (865 words) is as close to the narrative as the two halves of the
narrative are to each other (0.61 vs 0.65, pronoun-free Cosine Delta; both at the 0.1 percentile of
different-author pairs). This is expected — the author says he "reduced [it] to writing" — and it
serves as an internal control: a passage the narrator admits to having written in his own words
behaves exactly like the "Beale" letters do.

**B. Language of 1822 or later?** The letters contain a small but, by calibration, abnormal amount of
vocabulary that was rare or unknown in American print before 1822 and common after 1850:

Calibrated against twelve 2,716-word samples of genuine American writing of 1805-1843 (Jefferson,
Randolph, Jackson and J. Q. Adams from the 1820s; Edwin James, Flint, Hall and Paulding from
1817-28; Pike, Biddle, Irving and Gregg), the letters have **4-5 times the normal share of "late"
vocabulary**: 0.85 % of their content-word types have a pre-1822 Google-Books frequency below one
tenth of their 1850-1900 frequency, against a control mean of 0.19 % (sd 0.16, maximum 0.47 %; z =
+4.1; 0 of 12 controls as high). Two words (*stampeding*, *grizzlies*) are "strong" (later common,
essentially unattested before 1822); the only control with two such words is Gregg's 1844 Santa Fe
narrative (*ranchero, vaqueros*). On a looser threshold (ratio < 0.25) Gregg 1844 and Irving 1835 come
level with the letters, so the honest reading is that the letters' vocabulary resembles a Western
narrative of the late 1830s-1840s or after, and is unlike every 1810-1828 text in the set (0.0-0.5 %
on the strict measure). The count is small in absolute terms (a handful of words), which is why the
individual items are dated below.

The reverse check (deliberate archaising) finds a mixture: early-favoured items are present —
*&c.* (twice), *enquiring*, *such an one*, *winter quarters*, *punctillio* and *exhilerating*
(spellings already old-fashioned in 1822), *the States*, the capitalised season *Fall* — alongside the
later-favoured items above. All of these, and the idioms *well nigh*, *worth the candle*, *ho for the*,
*encounter the expense* and *personating*, are verified in American newspapers of 1800-1835 (section
3.3), many of them in Richmond papers. That pattern fits an 1880s writer lightly archaising (or an 1822 manuscript
modernised by an 1885 printer) better than it fits an unretouched 1822 letter; the lexical items that a
compositor could not have introduced (*objective point*, *grizzlies*, *stampeding*) are the decisive
ones. Function-word style, interestingly, cannot date the letters: on nearest-period classification
(72-80 % accurate on controls) the letters look *early* and the narrative *late*, and within the
letter genre the letters are nearer to Jefferson/Randolph/Jackson than to Lee/Twain/Poe. The
epistolary register of 1822 was imitable; the vocabulary of 1822 was not.

**Overall.** The two lines of evidence are independent and point the same way: the letters were most
probably composed by the pamphlet's author, in the 1880s, in a deliberately older epistolary register.
Confidence: high for common authorship (≈ 95 % on the calibrated stylometry alone, before considering
the anachronisms), moderate-to-high for a post-1822 date of composition (the calibrated anachronism
density is > 4 sd above genuine texts, but the strongest single items are "probable", not "certain",
anachronisms). The OCR-verified newspaper record sharpens the picture without closing it: in 1834 a
military writer still had to explain *stampedo* as "a North American attempt to adopt a Spanish
word", *stampede* reaches American newspapers in 1836 and *stampeding* in 1842, *grizzlies* is
unattested before 1838, *objective point* before 1847, and *improvised* means only "composed
extempore" through 1840 — while every archaism in the letters is ordinary newspaper English of
1800-1835. A Virginian writing in January 1822 would have had to be ahead of the printed record on
three or four independent words at once.

## 2. What is new relative to Nickell (1982)

Nickell, "Discovered: The Secret of Beale's Treasure", *Virginia Magazine of History and Biography*
90:3 (July 1982) 310-324 (JSTOR 4248566, paywalled; I did not see the article and rely on secondary
summaries — see Sources) argued (i) impressionistically, with a tabulation by the linguist Jean G.
Pival of syntactic features (sentence length; shares of *the*, *and*, *of*; reflexive-pronoun
"misuse"; negative passives such as "never to be told", "never to be realized"), that Beale and Ward
are one writer, and (ii) that *stampede/stampeding* and *improvise(d)* were unknown before the 1840s.
This note adds:

1. A calibrated, reproducible authorship test: 27 control authors, 115 samples, four families of
   features, empirical same-author and different-author distance distributions, leave-one-out
   attribution accuracy, author-level bootstrap, pronoun-free and function-word-only controls for
   genre, and same-topic different-author controls for subject matter. The verdict is the same as
   Nickell's but now carries a number (≈ 1 % of different-author pairs are as close) instead of an
   impression.
2. Quantification of Pival's reflexive-pronoun feature (1 of 115 control samples matches) and a
   negative result for the punctuation/syntax rates (uninformative).
3. Correction of the anachronism dates: *stampede(d)* is in English by 1823 (Austin Papers, Texas)
   and in print by 1826-28; *improvise* by 1788/1808. Neither is a hard anachronism for 1822. An
   OCR-verified newspaper chronology (Chronicling America, 597 + 183 hits checked) shows, however, that
   *stampede* entered American newspapers only in 1836 and *stampeding* in 1842, that *improvise(d)*
   before 1836 meant only "compose extempore", and that every "archaic" item in the letters was
   ordinary newspaper English of 1800-1835.
4. Two new candidate anachronisms — *objective point* (earliest verified use Richmond, 5 Jan. 1847;
   Civil-War vogue) and
   *grizzlies* (1850s) — plus *reliable* (post-1850 vogue), and the compounding/spelling pattern.
5. A calibration of the anachronism density against genuine 1805-1843 texts (the letters are 4-5×
   normal, z ≈ +4).
6. A negative result on style-based dating: by function words the letters look like 1820s letters,
   i.e. the writer's register imitation was good; vocabulary betrays him, style does not.
7. Poe tested and excluded quantitatively; Morriss's statement shown to behave as the narrator's prose.

The measure is topic-sensitive: the letters' "late" words are mostly frontier words (*stampeding,
grizzlies, buffalo, ravine, tethered, outfits, appliances*) whose printed frequency exploded with
westward expansion after 1840, and the 1885 narrative, whose subject is not the frontier, scores
*lower* than the letters. The relevant comparison is therefore with the seven frontier/Western
controls in the calibration set, all written by men who had been on the plains or the frontier:
Biddle 1814 (0.1 % / 1.2 %), Pike 1810 (0.2 / 1.3), Edwin James 1823 (0.5 / 2.8), Flint 1826 (0.1 /
1.0), Hall 1828 (0.0 / 2.0), Irving 1835 (0.4 / 3.2) and Gregg 1844 (0.3 / 3.5; "strong" words
*ranchero, vaqueros*). On the strict measure (ratio < 0.1) the letters (0.85 %) exceed every one of
them; on the loose measure (ratio < 0.25) Gregg's 1844 Santa Fe narrative matches them and Irving's
1835 tour comes close. The fair summary is that the letters' vocabulary profile resembles a Western
narrative of the 1835-1845 vintage (or later) more than any text of 1810-1828 in the set — the
strict-measure gap between the letters and the genuine 1820s texts (Jefferson, Randolph, Jackson,
Adams, James, Flint, Hall, Paulding: 0.0-0.5 %) is the load-bearing comparison, and the z-scores
above should be read with the small n (12) and the non-normal spread in mind.

returned no pre-1846 item at all for *stampede/stampeded/stampeding*, *grizzlies*, *objective point*,

hits, which for the common words only reflects the ranking.

## 4. Methods

**Texts.** Primary texts as parsed from the Wikisource transcription of the Library of Congress copy
(data/primary). The 403 words that follow Morriss's quotation in `morriss_statement.txt` ("Such, in
substance, was the statement…") are the anonymous author's and were moved to the narrative; the
narrative used here is therefore 3,954 words. "Beale letters" = the three letters concatenated
(2,716 words); "letter 1" = the letter of 4 Jan. 1822 alone (2,149 words); the cipher-2 plaintext
(150 words) is too short for stylometry and is only included in the vocabulary lists.

**Control corpus.** `code/stylo_download_controls.py` fetches the raw texts (Project Gutenberg plain
text; archive.org OCR for the rest); `code/stylo_build_samples.py` strips editorial matter,
footnotes, running heads, chapter synopses and dialect passages, isolates the author's own letters
where a volume mixes editor and author (Jackson, Lee, Poe, Twain letters), and cuts up to four
2,000-word samples per work at paragraph boundaries, evenly spaced through the text so that samples
of one author are never adjacent. `data/controls/manifest.csv` lists every sample with author, title,
date written, date published, source URL, word count and genre; `data/controls/raw/download_log.tsv`
lists the source URLs of the raw files. Kennedy's *Memoirs of William Wirt* was downloaded but not
used (Wirt's letters cannot be separated from Kennedy's narrative automatically); Fowler's 1821-22
journal was downloaded but excluded from the style tests because of its phonetic spelling; the
Southern Historical Society Papers volume mixes authors and is labelled as such.

**Distances.** `code/stylo_delta.py`. Words: lower-cased alphabetic tokens (apostrophes kept, hyphens
split, "&c." kept as a token). Feature lists (the N most frequent words; the 2,000 most frequent
character 4-grams including spaces; a fixed list of 150 function words) are taken from the control
samples only, and z-scores use the control mean and sd only, so the Beale texts do not influence the
standardisation. Burrows Delta = mean |Δz|; Cosine Delta = cosine distance between z-vectors (Evert et
al. 2017); "raw cosine" = cosine on relative frequencies without standardisation. Same-author pairs =
two samples of one control author (Cooke's two works and Twain's two works count as one author each,
as do Poe's letters and tale); different-author pairs = samples of two authors. Percentiles are
empirical; likelihood ratios are ratios of Gaussian kernel-density estimates of the two distance
distributions at the observed distance (they are unstable in the extreme tails, so the length-matched
ratios of 10-25 are the ones to quote). The author-level bootstrap resamples the 27 authors with
replacement 300 times. Leave-one-out accuracy: nearest-neighbour attribution of each control sample
among the others. Period classification: mean distance to 1805-1846 samples vs 1858-1904 samples with
the sample's own author held out; the letters-only variant restricts both groups to letter-genre
samples. Classical MDS map: results/stylo_delta_mds.svg; density plot: results/stylo_delta_calibration.svg.

**Punctuation/syntax.** `code/stylo_features.py`, regular-expression heuristics applied identically
to every text: sentences split at . ! ? followed by a capital, with abbreviations protected;
infinitives = *to* + a word that cannot be a determiner/pronoun/adverb/derived noun; relativisers =
*which/who/whom/whose*; passives = form of *be* (+ adverb) + word ending in -ed/-en/-wn/-ne; negative
passives = a negator within three words of a passive; untriggered reflexives = *-self/-selves* after
*than, like, as, and, or, for, from, to, with, by, of, regarding, besides, except, but, between, upon,
on, in*. The distance in feature space is the RMS of z-differences over the rate features.

**Vocabulary dating.** `code/lang_ngram_query.py` queries the Google Books Ngram Viewer JSON endpoint
(corpus en-US-2019, smoothing 0, 1780-1900, case-insensitive) for every word type and caches the raw
series (results/lang_ngram_cache*.json); `code/lang_ngram_analysis.py` computes the ratio statistics
and the calibration; `code/lang_ngram_plot.py` draws results/lang_ngram_timeseries.svg;
`code/lang_spelling_check.py` counts the orthographic conventions; `code/lang_attest_search.py`
searches Chronicling America (loc.gov JSON API, issues dated 1800-1835) and the archive.org full-text
API for each candidate word/phrase and verifies each hit against the page OCR (the search engine
matches fuzzily, so unverified hits are discarded); `code/lang_attest_window.py TERM Y0 Y1 REGEX`
does the same for one term in a chosen year window, ordered by date, and was used to find the
earliest verified newspaper uses after 1835 (results/lang_attest_window_*.csv). Dictionary dates were
read from merriam-webster.com and etymonline.com pages fetched on 2026-09-14; the OED is paywalled and
its dates are quoted at second hand (search-engine summaries of oed.com; Mashed Radish 2015). The
HathiTrust full-text search returned HTTP 403 to this environment and could not be used; the Google
Books API returned "quota exceeded"; the Ngram endpoint's pre-1830 data are thin and include misdated
books, so a handful of non-zero pre-1822 values for *stampede* etc. were treated as noise unless a
dated page could be verified.

**Caveats.** (1) Size: 2,000-3,000-word samples give leave-one-out accuracies of only 0.6-0.7 here,
which is why the comparison is framed as a calibrated distance rather than a classifier verdict. (2)
Genre: the letters are letters and the narrative is a narrative; pronoun removal, function-word-only
features, a letters-only period test and the same-author cross-genre pairs available (Poe, Twain)
address this, and the genre penalty in this corpus is small (cross-genre different-author mean 1.025
vs 1.015 overall; cross-genre same-author 0.851 vs 0.806). (3) Editing: the letters exist only as
printed in 1885; the compositor (or the author) could have normalised spelling, punctuation and
compounding, which is why spelling evidence is weighted lightly and the semicolon difference is not
taken as evidence of different hands. (4) Influence: the narrator says he memorised the letters. (5)
Google Books frequencies are corpus artefacts (OCR, misdating, genre mix); they are used only for
screening and for the calibration, which compares like with like. (6) Nickell's article itself was
not available; his feature list is known only from secondary summaries.

## Addendum: the likelihood ratios as quoted in the report

The report quotes the length-matched likelihood ratios as the full range across the five reported measures, 2.7
(Burrows Delta, 300 MFW, 2,000 words; the measure with the best leave-one-out accuracy on the controls, 69 %) to 25
(pronoun-free Cosine Delta and character 4-grams), rather than "10 to 25", and gives the share of different-author
pairs as close as the Beale pair as 0.0 to 4.9 % depending on the measure (results/stylo_delta_summary.json). With even
prior odds that is P(same hand) 0.73 to 0.96. The same-topic control pairs are farther apart than the Beale pair on
average (means 0.85 to 0.99 against 0.70) but one individual sample pair, Christian vs Pollock, is closer (0.628). The
control rate for the "not/never to be + participle" construction is 0.03 per thousand words (ctrl_mean 0.0287 in
results/stylo_features_summary.json), not 0.05.
