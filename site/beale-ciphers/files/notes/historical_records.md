# Historical records check: the Beale Papers (1885)

Scope: (1) Robert Morriss and the Washington Hotel; (2) any trace of Thomas J. Beale c. 1817-22;
(3) plausibility of the Santa Fe story; (4) James B. Ward and the pamphlet's publication and reception; (5) inventory of
scholarship. Method: primary newspaper pages from the Library of Congress *Chronicling America* collection (full-page OCR
fetched and read), public-domain books on archive.org, NSA FOIA releases, open-access papers, and bibliographic APIs.
Every capture relied on is in `sources/records/` and described in `sources/records/manifest.md` (URLs, access date
2026-09-14, what each shows). "PRIMARY" below means a contemporary record (newspaper page, printed pamphlet); "SECONDARY"
means later narrative, recollection, genealogy, forum transcription or scholarship. Items that could not be reached are
marked **not accessible** with the best pointer available. Nothing below is quoted from a source I did not read.

Pamphlet references are to the project transcription (`data/primary/*.txt`, from Wikisource pages of the 1885 print).

## 2. Plausibility of the Santa Fe story (1817-1821)

What the pamphlet says (letter dated 4 Jan 1822): about thirty men "left St. Louis the 19th May [1817], to be absent two
years, our objective point being Santa Fé, which we intended to reach in the ensuing Fall"; they wintered in Santa Fe,
found gold in March 1818 "some 250 or 300 miles" north, mined with Indian labour for some eighteen months, obtained
"everything necessary ... from Santa Fé", carried a first consignment to Virginia (deposited November 1819), and a second
(December 1821); the decoded cipher 2 adds jewels "obtained in St. Louis in exchange for silver to save transportation".

What the trade histories record for the same years (all SECONDARY, but standard):

- Gregg, *Commerce of the Prairies* (1844), vol. 1, ch. I, pp. 19-20: "In 1812, an expedition was fitted out under the
  auspices of Messrs. McKnight, Beard, Chambers, and several others (in all about a dozen) ... the luckless traders,
  immediately upon their arrival, were seized as spies, their goods and chattels confiscated, and themselves thrown into
  the calabozos of Chihuahua, where most of them were kept in rigorous confinement for the space of nine years". The first
  successful American ventures Gregg names are Glenn's and Becknell's in 1821, after Mexican independence; wheeled vehicles
  were first taken over the route on Becknell's second journey (1822) ("First Introduction of wheeled Vehicles", pp. 21-23).
  Source capture: `sources/records/books/commerceofprair01greg.txt`.
- Chittenden, *The American Fur Trade of the Far West* (1902), vol. 2, pp. 496-501: McKnight, Baird and Chambers (1812)
  "remained upwards of nine years" in prison; A. P. Chouteau and Julius De Munn (1815-17) were refused permission to trap,
  "ordered with all his people to withdraw from the Spanish dominions", escorted by "two hundred armed men", and on 24 May
  1817 "a force of Spanish troops appeared with orders to escort the whole party to Santa Fe ... all the furs and property
  of the traders were confiscated. The party were thrown into prison and for a time even confined in irons. After forty-eight
  days' imprisonment they were tried by court-martial and sentenced to leave the dominions of Spain, forfeiting all their
  property except one horse each" (Missouri Gazette, 13 Sept 1817, quoted); a claim "in the sum of about thirty thousand
  dollars" followed. "The outrageous treatment of Chouteau and De Munn, and the knowledge that a party of Americans were
  even then languishing in the dungeons of Chihuahua, seems to have deterred further adventure in that direction until the
  overthrow of Spanish power in Mexico in 1821." The only recorded journey in 1818-20 is D. Meriwether's (1819), who "was
  taken prisoner, carried to Santa Fe, and placed in confinement". "The period of Spanish dominion in New Mexico, which
  terminated in 1821, was thus marked by total failure on the part of American traders to gain any foothold in the Santa Fe
  trade". Chapter synopsis: "First wagons on the Santa Fe Trail" only after Becknell's second journey (1822). Source capture:
  `sources/records/books/americanfurtrade00chit_0.txt`.
- Nelson, BCSC report of 15 April 1972 (NSA FOIA doc656726): scanned the Library of Congress microfilm of the Spanish
  Archives of New Mexico, rolls 18 (1815-17), 19 (1818-19), 20 (1820-21), 21 and 22: "NO BEALE PARTY REFERENCES WERE
  FOUND"; he notes Roll 18 frame 862 (Nov. 1817; the last digit of the year is unclear in the OCR), "Travel Regulations" for visitors, and Roll 19 frame 441 (11 Nov 1818),
  "instructions on PASSPORTS" - i.e. the province registered and controlled foreigners in exactly these years. (Nelson
  remained a believer; the negative result is his own.) Source capture: `sources/records/nsa_foia/doc656726.txt`.

Assessment.

1. Thirty armed Americans arriving at Santa Fe in December 1817 - six months after the Chouteau-De Munn arrests and while
   the McKnight party sat in Chihuahua - and being allowed to winter, to buy "everything necessary" for mining, to hire
   Indian labour and to work a mine for two years, is contrary to everything recorded about Spanish policy in New Mexico in
   1812-21. Every documented American party of the period was expelled or imprisoned. An undocumented party could in
   principle have escaped notice, but the pamphlet has them dealing openly with Santa Fe for supplies.
2. Nothing of the party appears in the Spanish administrative records for 1815-21 that Nelson searched, nor in the
   Missouri/Illinois/Richmond newspapers he read for 1817 and 1820, nor in any newspaper available in Chronicling America.
   A departure of thirty gentlemen "well born, and well educated" from St. Louis in May 1817 would be a notable event; the
   St. Louis papers of 1817 (Missouri Gazette; Western Journal) are not online here and should be read before this point is
   pressed (Nelson used the Missouri Historical Society's holdings for Franklin but reports nothing for St. Louis 1817
   beyond the Gazette items on Sibley and the Osages).
3. Transport. Cipher 2's totals are 2,921 lb of gold and 5,100 lb of silver (about 3.6 short tons, plus jewels). The
   pamphlet does not say how it was carried. Pack animals (roughly 150-200 lb each) would need about 25-30 mules for the 4,826 lb of 1819 and 16-21 for the 3,195 lb of 1821;
   wagons were not taken over the plains route until 1822 (Gregg; Chittenden). Either way, a caravan of that kind moving
   from northern New Mexico to Missouri in 1819 and 1821 (through the same Spanish frontier that arrested Meriwether in
   1819) and on to Bedford County without leaving a trace is hard to credit. At the prices of 1885 (gold $20.67, silver about $1.06 per troy
   ounce) the metal would be worth about $880,000 (gold, some 42,600 troy oz) plus about $79,000 (silver, some 74,400 troy oz)
   - the article of 1893 already spoke of "more than half a million dollars' worth".
4. Jewels. Trading 1,288 lb of silver for $13,000 in jewels in St. Louis in 1821 assumes a jeweller in a river town of a
   few thousand people holding $13,000 in stock and willing to take a ton of unrefined silver. No record of such a
   transaction exists; the detail reads as narrative convenience (it explains why the second deposit is lighter).
5. Anachronisms already noted by others (Nickell, per Wikipedia): "stampede" and "improvise" in Beale's 1822 letters are
   not recorded in American English before the late 1820s-1830s. Not re-examined here.

Conclusion: the Santa Fe narrative is incompatible with the documented conditions of 1817-21 and left no trace in the
Spanish or American records that have been searched. This is the strongest historical objection after the Morriss dating.

## 3. The people

### 3.1 Robert Morriss (c. 1777 - 3 Jan 1863)

Documented outline (P = primary capture in `sources/records/chronicling_america/ocr/`; S = secondary):

- 1813: "Robert Morris" on the committee to build the new market house (S: Christian 1900).
- Jan 1819: "Robert Morris, Treasurer of Lynchburg Toll Bridge Company, declared a dividend of two per cent" (S: Christian 1900).
- c. 1820: owner of "the large dwelling opposite the residence of Dr. Robert Early"; "a man of wealth"; "a sudden reverse
  depriving him of his fortune, himself and his excellent wife there established a house for receiving boarders" (S: Cabell 1858 p. 239).
- 1823: his former house "was, in 1823, the residence of Mrs. Brown, of Amherst" (S: Cabell p. 196). Sept 1823: lodger "at
  Mr. Robert Morris's"; depositions "at the tavern-house of Robert Morriss" (P: Virginian 8 Sep, 14 and 24 Oct 1823).
- 20 Sept 1823: notice that he has rented the Washington (Third Street, corner above David & William Kyle's, "lately
  occupied by Mr. Henry Moorman") and will open on 1 October (P: Virginian 30 Sep 1823 p4); revised notice dated 7 Oct
  ("now prepared") printed 10 Oct-9 Dec 1823. He advertises "a large yard, in which droves of horses can be accommodated".
- Dec 1823, Apr-May 1825: "Robert Morriss's tavern" / "tavern of Robert Morriss" (P: Virginian 9 and 12 Dec 1823; 14, 18 Apr, 26 May 1825).
- June 1825: "Subscribers to Lucas' Atlas are requested to call at the Washington Hotel ... ROB. MORRISS" (P: Virginian 23 Jun 1825 p4).
- 30 Dec 1825: "removed from the Washington, which he has occupied for more than two years past, to the Franklin Hotel"
  (P: Virginian 23 Mar-27 Apr 1826 p4); "Mr. Morriss' Franklin Hotel" in adverts from 26 Jan 1826 (P).
- Dec 1827: Masonic ball at the Franklin Hotel, "such a ball and supper as only Mrs. Robert Morriss knew how to provide"
  (S: Cabell p. 185). Autumn 1827: a family takes "the house formerly occupied by Robert Morriss, Esq." (S: Cabell p. 192).
- Sept 1829: the Washington Hotel is kept by "JAS. C. MOORMAN", who "has extended his lease" (P: Lynchburg Virginian 5 Oct 1829 p4).
- 1842: Universalist church lot "just back of Morriss' hotel" (S: Christian 1900). Cabell (1858): Morriss leased the
  Franklin after Mr. Hoyle and ran it "for many years"; the couple "survive, residing in Lynchburg" (pp. 241, 297).
- Death: "On Saturday, 3d January, 1863, at Roslin, the residence of his niece, Mrs. David Saunders, ROBERT MORRISS, Esq.,
  in the eighty-sixth year of age" (Lynchburg Virginian 8 Jan 1863 p3 c2, as transcribed on TreasureNet; original **not
  accessible**). Age implies birth c. 1777, consistent with the pamphlet's "Robert Morriss ... born in 1778".
- Wife: the pamphlet's "Sarah Mitchell", married 1803 in Loudoun: no record consulted. Death: 1861 (pamphlet) or 1865
  (Wikipedia's citation of a Lynchburg Virginian obituary of 21 May 1865, age 77) - unresolved.

Reading: the pamphlet's portrait of Morriss (wealth, reverse, hotel-keeping, universal esteem, longevity) is accurate in
outline and could have been written by anyone who knew Lynchburg; the one checkable date it gives for the Beale
episodes - the Washington Hotel in January 1820 and January 1822 - is wrong by more than three years. Morriss himself
cannot be the source of that error (he kept the Washington for only 27 months and never in 1820-22); it is the pamphlet's.

### 3.2 Thomas J. Beale

- No contemporary record of a "Thomas J. Beale" or "Thomas Jefferson Beale" in Virginia, Missouri or New Mexico was
  found or is cited by anyone. The pamphlet itself says he "registered simply from Virginia" and never spoke of his family.
- Missouri: Nelson (1972) found "THOMAS BEALL" in the Franklin, Mo., post-office list of the Missouri Intelligencer, 1 April
  1820 (confirmed with the Missouri Historical Society; not in later lists); Mental Floss (secondary) adds an "S. T. Beall"
  in the Missouri Gazette of October 1817 (not seen). Wikipedia's "St. Louis postmaster's list of 1820" appears to be
  this Franklin item misplaced. Nelson found no "Beale party" items in the Missouri, Illinois or Richmond papers he read
  (see manifest). Chronicling America has no Missouri pages for 1817-22 (searches returned nothing).
- Census (Wikipedia's Ancestry summary; subscription indexes **not accessible**): 1810: two Thomas Beales (CT, NH),
  schedules missing for 18 Virginia counties; 1820: three Thomas Beales (LA, TN, VA "Thomas K.") and eleven Thomas Bealls;
  only heads of household are named before 1850, so absence proves little.
- Botetourt County Beales (S: Hodges, *Genealogy of the Beale Family 1399-1956*, pp. 93-94; undated): Thomas Beale, merchant,
  clerk in his brothers John and Charles Beale's Alexandria china store; engaged to "Miss Judy Hancock of Fincastle"; "a
  Mr. Risk" spread a slander; "they fought a duel, Beale uninjured"; he "went to New Orleans until he heard of Risk's
  recovery & then he sailed for Europe, but returned & died in New Orleans where he had married a French lady, Celeste
  Grandpierre"; children Celeste, Octavia, James. Charles Beale (1771-1842) sat in the House of Delegates 1804-07. Nelson
  (1972) notes Charles Beale of Botetourt on a 1817 Washington Monument commission list and a Richmond letter for "Reuben
  Beale" (1817). Pelling (Cipher Mysteries, 23 May 2015; S, web) identifies the duellist as James Beverly Risqué, dates the
  duel to c. 1806, has Thomas Beale (Sr.) commanding "Beale's Rifles" at New Orleans in 1815, dying in New Orleans in
  September 1820, and a son Thomas Beale Jr. (by Chloe Delancy) dying there in October 1823 - both from New Orleans
  probate records I could not consult. If those dates hold, neither man was in Lynchburg in January-March 1820 and
  January-March 1822 or in St. Louis in May 1822.
- 1806 "duel": Hodges gives no date; Pelling's c. 1806 rests on Risqué family sources (not seen). Not verified.
- Net: the name "Thomas Beale" belonged to a real Botetourt family with a colourful story attached (duel, flight, New
  Orleans), which a Lynchburg author of 1885 could have known; the pamphlet's Thomas J. Beale has no independent existence
  in any record examined or reported.

### 3.3 James B. Ward and the pamphlet

- Identity. Title page and copyright line: "J. B. Ward" (P). Hart (1964): "James B. Ward, of Campbell County, Virginia,
  contiguous to Lynchburg" (S). Wikisource's author page gives 1822-1903; a TreasureNet contributor gives birth 27 Jan
  1822, parents Giles Ward and Adeline Risqué, and a grandfather James Beverly Risqué - the same Risqué who duelled with
  the Botetourt Thomas Beale. That genealogy is **unverified** (no source cited; not checked against any register). If it
  is right it is the most economical explanation of where the name Beale came from. Wikipedia (citing Nickell) has Ward
  made a Master Mason in 1863 and owning the house in which Sarah Morriss died; the pamphlet itself says Mrs. Morriss died
  at the agent's house. Nickell 1982 **not accessible** to check.
- The pamphlet's own account: the anonymous "friend" received the papers from Morriss in 1862, worked on them for twenty
  years, decoded No. 2 with the Declaration of Independence, and handed everything to an agent (Ward) for publication.
  Ward's role is only that of "agent"; no statement by Ward on authorship survives in any source read. The nearest thing is
  Hart's report that in about 1903 Ward "confirmed all that is contained in the pamphlet; and his son, then U.S. Mail
  transfer clerk at the union station, Lynchburg, added his own confirmation". George Hart's private view in 1964: "I have
  wondered if Ward might have written his manuscript based upon some figures he found, or made up; and yet, we have the word
  of Ward, his son, and friends to the contrary. Inquiry among some aged neighbors of Ward showed the high respect they had
  for him, and brought forth the statement that Ward would never practice deception." (both S: Hart 1964, NSA FOIA doc656779).
- Printing and sale. "LYNCHBURG: Virginian Book and Job Print, 1885. PRICE FIFTY CENTS" (P). The job office of the
  Lynchburg Virginian was a real printer; the 1885 copyright entry is recorded on the verso (P) but the Copyright Office
  record book is not online (**not accessible**). The often-repeated claim that the application was filed in 1884 on Adams
  Bros. & Paynes letterhead is unsourced in anything I read and is not confirmed. The fire story rests solely on Ward's
  word to Clayton Hart c. 1903 as reported by George Hart in 1964; no press report of a fire at the Virginian job office in
  1885-86 was found (Lynchburg papers for those years are not digitised, so this is weak negative evidence).
- Reception 1885-1900. No mention of the pamphlet in any Chronicling America page for 1885-92 (searches: "beale papers",
  beale + treasure/cipher + bedford/lynchburg/morriss; every Virginia hit for 1885-99 read). Earliest found: The Roanoke
  Times, 20 Jan 1893, p. 1 (P), a full-column retelling that already states the treasure lies "within less than twenty
  miles of Roanoke" and that "the inhabitants of Bedford county have hunted for this treasure but so far in vain". Clayton
  Hart first heard the story from N. H. Hazlewood at Montvale (formerly Buford) in summer 1897; the Hart brothers dug from
  1898 (S: Hart 1964). Friedman's 1949 letters show the story had reached professional cryptologists by then (P for the
  letters; NSA REF ID A69983).
- Later organisation. The Hart Papers (George L. Hart Sr., 1952 version not seen; 1964 version read) were circulated
  privately and released by NSA under FOIA. Carl Hammer's computer studies (1965-71) led to a Beale Cypher Study
  Committee/Beale Cypher Association (symposium in Washington 1969 per Mental Floss; Nelson's 1972 report is addressed to
  the BCSC), with a newsletter and several hundred members; it had dissolved by the late 1990s (S: Mental Floss; not
  verified further). Viemeister, *The Beale Treasure: History of a Mystery* (Hamilton's, Bedford, 1987; rev. 1997) is the
  standard believer's compendium and was **not accessible**.

## 5. What is new here

Relative to Nickell (1982, as reported by Wikipedia and Poundstone) and the current Wikipedia article, which say only that
"contemporary records show he did not start in that position until at least 1823":

1. The primary documents themselves. Morriss's own notices fix his tenure of the Washington precisely: rented by a notice
   dated 20 Sept 1823 to open on 1 Oct 1823; "more than two years" ending 30 Dec 1825; the house stood on Third Street at
   the corner above David & William Kyle's store and was "lately occupied by Mr. Henry Moorman"; by Sept 1829 it was kept
   by Jas. C. Moorman under an extended lease. Cabell's 1858 recollection ("In the year 1824") independently confirms the
   order of events and adds that in 1820 Morriss was a wealthy householder, not a hotel-keeper.
2. A nuance that cuts the other way: Morriss was keeping a "tavern-house"/boarding house in his own dwelling by September
   1823, i.e. before the Washington, so a guest could have lodged "at Mr. Robert Morriss's" before October 1823. The
   pamphlet's error is specific to the Washington Hotel and to the years 1820-22, for which no boarding-house activity is
   documented either.
3. The earliest press retelling located, The Roanoke Times of 20 Jan 1893, shows the story and the digging established in
   Bedford County within eight years of publication, and gives the pamphlet's provenance in one sentence ("a friend of
   Morriss, who learned them in 1863").
4. Corrections to Wikipedia: the "Thomas Beall" post-office notice of 1820 is from Franklin, Missouri (Missouri
   Intelligencer, per Nelson), not St. Louis; Wikipedia's Sarah Morriss sentence is internally inconsistent (died 1863 vs
   obituary 1865) and conflicts with the pamphlet (1861).
5. The declassified Nelson report's negative search of the Spanish Archives of New Mexico (1815-21) and of the Richmond
   Enquirer, and Hart's own 1964 doubts about Ward, are not used in the standard accounts.
6. Two leads worth a records search: the Ward-Risqué kinship (would connect the pamphlet's author to the family that
   duelled with the real Thomas Beale of Botetourt) and the Cabell/Christian references to the "Norvell(e) House"/"Arlington"
   (to test the pamphlet's "known now as the Arlington").
