FOREMOTHERS TO BLAME.
Perhaps it was a great lack of thought in our
foremothers not to have rebelled against unequal
rights, at the same time as did our forefathers re
bel against taxation without representation, but un
doubtedly it was as some one has wittily said:
“They not only had the conditions of a new coun
try to contend with, but they had to put up with
our forefathers besides.” But, without dwelling too
much on the shortcomings of cur foremothers, let
us take decided action now, so that in the years
to come the finger of derision may not be pointed
toward us, and that the women at the beginning of
the twentieth century may be regarded as women
of mental strength, equal to cope in every way
with the affairs of life, and commanding a position
to exercise their authority equal with men,
Until woman votes she cannot command equal
money for equal work, nor will she have equal
opportunity for obtaining work. Some men claim
that women vote by proxy. If that is such a satis
factory way, let them take a turn at trying it. But
such sophistical reasoning is unworthy of a sensi
ble man. There is a great cry that the polis are
dangerous places for women. Are they not danger
ous for some men? The absurdity of this argument
is not to be considered, for woman would purify
the voting places, as she puriues all other places,
by her very presence.
Why are the multitudes of uneducated, murder
ous foreigners who, besides reducing the standard
of the country, have no knowledge of its laws, no
love for its institutions, no sense of loyalty, allowed
to vote, when women with property rights, love of
country, and a natural Instinet to further the prog
ress of all that is moral and good, are deprived
of this invaluable right? Many of the aliens do not
know whether they are living under a monarchy
or a republic, nor have they any sense of the mean
ing of Independence Day. In the present day of
voluminous ballots, requiring the alert eye of the
well informed citizen to cast an intelligent vote,
it seems a travesty upon the great right of suf
frage for any person to be permitted to deposit one
of these ballots to that individual's credit, unless
that individual be sufficiently qualified by education
to read and understand every word impressed
thereon.
WOMEN'S MENTALITY.
Statistics show a greater percentage of female
attendance than male attendance at the educational
institutions of the United States. This points to
ward a higher mental development in woman, and
with this tendency must come better restlts in
every direction where woman may be allowed to
exercise her better judgment. Political methods
would be improved, the fearful results from the
abuse of liquor would be abolished, and beginning
at the very initial the right of suffrage would be
given only to such as were entitled to it educa
tionally and by the proof, of their being selfsus
taining individuals.
What greater indignity could be visited upon an
intelligent woman than to be deprived of the very
liberty of her being, and the manner in which her
estates are left, not only in the hands of ignorance,
but with those having no realty rights nor invest
ments in the business of the country, and often
times in the very hands of men incapable of work
ing out even a decent living for themselves?
The great injustice of thus debarring women from
realizing to the fullest extent the glorious gift of
liberty cries aloud for its undoing, and not until
woman ig admitted to the foremost ranks of the
human army will the march for the betterment of
the world be rapid.
Tne tendency of the times seems to be toward a
swifter approach to the complete unfolding of the
general plan of the Great Designer of Creation, and
as people come to a fuller realization of this fact
selfishness and egotism will be eliminated from
their natures, and no longer will it be thought that
because she is a woman she is not qualified to co
operate with man in the furthering of human
progress, but the man will seek her co-operation

In springtime the poets sing of heautiful flowers
and balmy skies, but in point of fact the spring
season seems to exercise a baneful influence over
humanity in general. It not unusual for a
woman who has gore through the winter with good
health and a clear skin to find an inexplicable crop
of blotches appearing on her face. It is more than
probable that these are the protest of nature
against the constant use of powder necessary where
one is in attendance at social functions. One of
the best and simplest modes of treatment is the
use of an oatmeal bag in bathing. The effect is
cleansing, softening and nourishing to the skin.
One may as well prepare a reasonable guantity
at a time, so it is wise to srart with five pounds
of finely ground oatmeal. Add half a pound of pure
Castile scap, pulverized, and a pound of powdered
Italian orris root, mixing them thoroughly. Make
bags of checsecloth about four inches square, sew
ing firmly. A space is left in one seam through
which to fill the bag. After filling. the opening
must be sewed, of course. One of these hags may
be used several times, affording a velvety lather,
The presence of the orris root imparts a faint fra
grance to the skin that is extremely pleasant. It
Is well to handle the powdered soap carefully, as
if accidentally inhaled it produces violent sneezing.
A generous diet of fruit is another admirable aid

William V. King, the superintendent of the Cot
ton Exchange, is the defendant in a suit which
came to trial in the Supreme Court before Justice
Andrews vesterday. in which his wife, Bella PF.
King, is the plaintiff. She asks that he be com
pelled to convey to her certain real estate which,
she says. he has wrongfully withheld from her.
Some time ago. while Mrs. King was absent In
Europe, a suit for a divorce brought by her hus
band was called in the Supreme Court before Jus
tice Truax. Mrs. King entered a defence, and was
subsequently awarded counsel! fee and alimony.
Mrs. King testified that when she married the
defendant she had no money, but between that
time and the next few vears she saved $700. She
said they were married in 1886. In 183% she pur
chased the house. No. 712 Madison-ave., for 330.000,
subject to a mortgage of $15.00. She subsequently
sold this house and bought the house No. 3 West
Eighty-eighth-st.. which is in dispute, for 327.500,
subject to a mortgage. Her husband told her in
1865 that he had a purchaser, a Chicago packer,
who would give a price for the house which would
give her a large profit, and advised her to sell to
him. She consented, on the understanding that
the purchase money was to be invested in another
house. Secon afterward she fell ill. and they re
mained in the house until she went to Europe, in
1857. Before this her husband told her the sale had
not been made, and she naturally believed the
property had remained in her name. Her daugh

came home she was informed of the divorce pro=
ceedings taken against her.

back the deed of the Eighty-eighth-st. house, and
she said she had not, because her mother had re

suit for a divorce against her. He added that he
had got the property reconveyed to Fim in his own
name.

THREE SUSPICIOUS FIRES IN ONE HOUSE.
TENANTS OF HARLEM APARTMENT HOUSE MUCH
EXCITED OVER HAPPENINGS-UNKNOWN
MAN ATTACKS WOMAN.
The tenants of the five story apartment house at
No. 230 West One-hundred-and-twenty-first-st.
were much excited vesterday over threa small fires
that were discovered in the building within a few
hours of each other, and which, they believe, were
of incendiary origin.
The first blaze was discovered at 9 o'clock Mon
day night. It had just been put out when one of
the tenants returning to her rooms found a small
blaze on the stairway between the fourth and
fifth tloors. At T o'clock vesterday merning a third
fire was discovered on the dumbwaliter. A big
bunch of papers was lying on top of it, the whole
blazing smartly.
An hour later Miss Mary Lang, who has an
apartment on the fourth floor, was going out of
the building. As she reached the long hallway
leading to the street door she met a medium
man walking in. He accosted her, saying,
morning,” and tipped his hat. Thinking him to
be one of the tenants, she greeted him and started
to pass out, when the man suddenly gra
about the throat with both hands and began chok
ing her. She screamed loudly for help. and when
she did so her assailant threw her violently to
the floor and ran out into the street. No one A
Miss Lang saw the man, and she was unable to
give an accurate description of him.
