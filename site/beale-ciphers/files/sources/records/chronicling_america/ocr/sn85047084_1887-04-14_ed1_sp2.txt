WHAT MIUHT HAVE BEEN.
It is an old saying tWat "it is useless
to cry over spilt milk but while one
may refrain from the expression of use
less regrets, that is no reason why one
should not occasionally point out what
might have been. If it serves no
other useful purpose it reminds people
of their present duty and obligations to
the future. In this country especially
men need frequent reminders, for
whether it be the effect of climate or
isolation from the busy world, they are
apt to drift into careless habits of
thought and action.
Now, let us ask what might have
been? The Hawaiian Kingdom might
have been the most prosperous and con
tented little State in the world. Every
condition was favorable to this consum
mation at the start. It certainly is pros
perous in a degree, but it is not con
tented. Everyone admits that. In
deed it is impossible for a stranger to be
a day in this city without realizing this
truth. This of itself is an evil. It
creates a feeling of distrust among
visitors, and suggests a line of hostile in
vestigation which cannot do any good to
the country. First impressions go a long
way. If these be unfavorable they are
hard to eradicate, and usually grow.
And the consequence of all is that they
injure the business interests of the com
munity. A satisfied assurance of one's
surroundings begets a feeling of confi
dence at home and abroad and strength
ens business. If our population had
been content and energetic, mutually
helping instead of tearing each other to
pieces, business would have been much
better than it is, and instead of the
gloom of "dull times" there would have
been the rush and bustle of successful
enterprise.
This general statement covers all
points so far as we are concerned, and
we leave our readers to fill in the de
tails for themselves. Just who is to
blame, and in what ratio the blame is to
be distributed, are problems we shall
not attempt to solve. If we tried we
might do an injustice to some, and we
have no wish to be unjust to any one.
We think, however, that a candid ex
amination of this point would result in a
pretty general censure all around. There
were faults everywhere faults of omis
sion and commission. It is full time
now to turn over a new leaf and unite in
promoting the common good. The
country abounds in natural wealth let
its riches be developed. The harbor of
Honolulu might be greatly .increased in
its efficiency as a maritime resort let
this be done. Wharves and docks are
to be built, the deep water area increased
and the bar deepened. Railroads and
tramroads are to be built and operated.
Productive industries are to be estab
lished and multiplied colonization is to
be encouraged; .education is to be
broadened in its scope, and the influence
of liw and sound morals upheld. This
is the present duty of all classes of cit"
zacs, and by its loyal fulfilment the past
may in part be redeemed. What might
have been may yet be, and the Hawaiian
Islands become a model of contented
prosperity and progress.

London, February 221.
Parliament was opened on January 2Sth
by the Lords High Commissioners, ap
pointed for the purpose by the Queen. The
Queen's speech did not contain any very
debateable matter; nevertheless, since the
opening of Parliament the debate on the
address, in reply to the speech from the
Throne, which is in reality a vote of con
fidence in the Government of the day, has
been dragging on its apparently intermin
able length. Numerous amendments, f
more or less importance, have been laid
down for discussion. The principal of
them, proposed by Mr. Parnell, in relation
to Ireland, was defeated by the Unionists
by a majority of 10o. At length, after six
teen days of continuous debate, the Speak
er arose and stated that in his opinion the
questions at issue had been sufficiently de
bated, and that the feeling of the House
was evidently in favor of ending the dis
cuson. Thus, by an application of the
cloture, the debate was closed amidst a
scene of great excitement on the part of
the Irish obstructionists, who, by talking
against time, boasted that they would
make legislation impossible unless their
demands were conceded. The House
adopted the address by the enormous ma
jority of 240. The consequence of this
great waste of public time has been the
delay of measures necessary for the good
government of Ireland, which country
still remains in a most unsatisfactory con
dition. The plan of campaign, described
in my letter of December last, is still in
full operation, and, up to tbe present
time, the Government have found the
legal means at hand insufficient to cope
with the conspiracy for the non-payment
of rent.
Soon after the opening of Parliament
Lord Randolph Churchill made his prom
ised explanation of the causes which led
to his sudden retirement from Lord Salis
bury's Government. The reasons which
he gave were the apparent unwillingness
of the heads of the Admiralty and. War
Office to reduce the estimates for the year.
He said that being pledged to a policy of
economy in public expenditure, he was un
able to give his assent to proposals that
failed in any one Item to reduce the na
tional expenditure. His explanation was
deemed most unsatisfactory by the Eng
lish Press, with but few exceptions, and
his avowal that he would have retained his
seat in the Cabinet if a reduction of even
half a million pounds sterling had been
made, has not only brought much ridicule
on him, but has not failed to leave a pre
judicial effect on his future career, inas
much as his sincereity is questioned when
the small sum of 500.000 in a budget of
90,000,000 caused him to imperil the posi
tion of the Unionist Cabinet at a critical
juncture in both home and foreign poli
tics. Lord Dunraven, Under Secretary of
State for the Colonies, has this week fol
lowed Lord Randolph Churchill's exam
ple, and resigned for the same reasons.

A series of articles appearing in the
"Fortnightly Review," said to have been
written by Sir Charles Dilke, has attracted
much attention in England and on the
Continent. The first appeared on the 1st
January, and treated of Germany, and
the second on the 1st February, treated of
France. The writer describes the relative
positions of these two great Powers with a
mastery that only a thorough knowledge
of these countries could comznand. Facts
only are given and no contioversial matter
is introduced into the articles. The de
scription of Germany is particularly strik
ing. The character of the army, the in
fluence of Bismarck on the Emperor, and
the irreconcilable natures of the Crown
Princess and Bismarck are described, to
gether with their probable consequences on
the peace of Europe and the future of
the German Empire. The owning sen
tence of this article is the keynote to its
character. Never, says the writer, since
the days of Napoleon the Great, has force
counted for so much in European politics
as it does at present. The second article is
devoted to France, and after a thorough
examination of the curious position France
occupies among the great European Pow
ers, the writer comes to the conclusion that
the Republic is likely to fall in case of a
war, be it successful or non-successful. In
the former case the danger lies in the am
bition of her generalissimo, and in the lat
ter it is the exasperation of the nation.

THE MAGAZINE GUN.

Single Loader Used by Oar Ar.j.
It is an indisputable fact that any good
magazine gun will discharge the con
tents of its magazine in a shorter time
than any single loader can throw an
equal number of shots; but to say that
from that fact a magazine gun wastes
ammunition is absurd. One might just
as well say because a sportsman can
shoot two shots from a double-barrel
shotgun in a shorter time than he can
from a single-barrel gun that the
double-barrel gun is wasteful of ammu
nition, whereas it is a well-established
truth that it is economical of ammuni
tion, and that a good shot can kill a
great deal more game with a double
barrel than he can with a single one.
He may consume more amrnVmition, but
that's what he goes out for; and it i;
believed by those who have had most
experience with magazine guns that
they really consume, wear out and
waste less ammunition than the single
loader.
But whether this be so or not is im
material, and it is certain that in the
shock of close conflict in the open field,
that line of soldiers will prevail which
can throw the greatest number of shots
in the first ten seconds,- rather than in
the first ten minutes; and all the world
except ourselves has learned that the
magazine, from which the gun is
charged and discharged by machinery
with remarkable rapidity, is indispen
sable for great emergencies.
Every first-class European power has
adopted one form or another of maga
zine guns, of which we have about
twenty different patterns, all of which
are excellent and several of which have
almost reached the limit of perfection.
It is quite within the range of possi
bility that we may get into a war with
a European nation at any time, and it
is dead certain that we shall be caught
totally unprepared for it unless our
Government adopts and purchases a
supply of magazine guns. X. Y. Si,n,
