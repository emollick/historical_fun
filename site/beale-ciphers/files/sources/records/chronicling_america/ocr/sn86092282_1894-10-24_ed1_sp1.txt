Spontaneous Combustion ol Coal.
According to L. Hoepke, it is to a
slow oxidation and to the resulting
disengagement of heat that must bo
attributed the spontaneous combustion
of cargoes of coal. The danger is so
much the greater in proportion as the
surface exposed to the air is wider. It
is maximum with coal dust. The load
ing and trimming should, therefore,
be so done as to avoid as much as pos
sible the crumbling of the coal under
the influence of the ship’s motion. The
smallest vessels are preferable for the
carriage of coal.
Mr. Hoepke does not believe in the
possibility of the spontaneous oom
bustion of cargoes of damp cotton.
But it is possible that a spark fulliug
accidentally upon a bale may remain
ignited for weeks and afterward set
Are to the mass. Greasy cotton, on
the contrary, very easily takes fire
spontaneously. The same is the case
with flax, jute and tow. Stacks of hay,
and bales of tobacco and hops are like
wise liable to spontaneous combustion.
