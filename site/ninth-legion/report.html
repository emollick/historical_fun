The Ninth's last decade

Some time between December 107 and December 108 the Ninth Legion, Legio IX Hispana, a unit of about five thousand Roman soldiers, finished a gate of its fortress at York in northern England. It had been in Britain since the Roman invasion of AD 43. It had been mauled by the rebels of the British queen Boudica in 60 and refilled with two thousand men from Germany, and it had been attacked by night in its camp in Caledonia, now Scotland, in the 80s. A slab was carved for the new gate recording that the emperor Trajan had built it "through the agency of the Ninth Legion Hispana". Part of that slab survives, and it is the last dated text that names the legion anywhere in the Roman world.

After that, no inscription, papyrus or historian names the Ninth again with a date attached. Soon after 165 the army's legions were listed in order on a column in Rome, thirty-three of them. The Ninth is not there, and nor is the Twenty-Second Legion Deiotariana, from Egypt. At some point in the half-century between the York gate and that list the legion ceased to exist, and no surviving writer records where or how. Rosemary Sutcliff's novel The Eagle of the Ninth (1954), in which the legion marches north into Scotland and is destroyed, made the disappearance famous.

It is a mystery because the evidence is indirect. The legion's last years are known only from the tombstones and honorific inscriptions of officers who served in it and went on to other posts, from roof tiles stamped with its name, and from a few sentences in later writers about wars in which Roman soldiers died. Historians have read those scraps in four ways. The oldest view, going back to the German historian Theodor Mommsen in 1885, is that the legion was destroyed in Britain, in a war in the north soon after the York gate; the modern form of it, argued in 2021 by Nick Hodgson, an archaeologist of Hadrian's Wall (the frontier barrier built across northern Britain from 122), and explored in 2025 by the Dutch archaeologist Erik Graafstal, puts the loss in fighting that followed the emperor Hadrian's visit to Britain in 122. Against that, three stamped tiles of the Ninth found at Nijmegen, a Roman fortress on the Rhine in the Netherlands, have been taken since the 1960s to show that the legion left Britain for the Continent and was lost later somewhere else: in Judaea, in the Jewish revolt of 132 to 136 known as the Bar Kokhba war, in which Rome admitted heavy losses; or in Armenia in 161, where the Parthians, Rome's eastern rivals, wiped out a Roman legion whose name no source gives. Duncan Campbell, a historian of the Roman army, and Werner Eck, a German historian of Roman inscriptions, hold versions of that eastern view. A fourth possibility is that the legion was quietly broken up or merged into another and left no record at all.

This page judges the case from the inscriptions themselves. Every inscription that names the legion was collected: all 133 records from Britain, and every entry in the two large online databases of Latin inscriptions for the rest of the empire. The careers of the three senators who served in the legion as young officers, and whose later posts are dated, were worked back to find the latest year in which the legion must still have existed, and that arithmetic was run as a simulation to show how firm the answer is. The Nijmegen finds were counted against the other finds from the same fortress. Then the five main pieces of evidence were weighed against the four possible endings, with the weight of each piece given as a range from a base reading to a sceptical one, so that a reader who disagrees can see how far the answer moves. The result is that the legion certainly existed in 122 and almost certainly in 124, that nothing places it outside Britain after that, and that Britain in the years 122 to 130 is the probable ending, with a probability of about one half to two thirds. That probability is a judgement rather than a measurement; the British ending is probable but not proven. No new evidence is presented; the contribution is the catalogue, the career model and the explicit weighing.

Assessment: a British ending is probable. The legion most probably ended in Britain, destroyed or defeated and then disbanded, in the years after Hadrian's visit of 122, between about 122 and 130. This is the reading Hodgson argued and Graafstal explores. The evidence clearly favours it over the other endings but does not prove it, and the percentage moves when the same evidence is weighed differently: given the four possible endings and the ranges of weight set out under "Weighing it", the British ending has a probability of about one half to two thirds on central readings; it ranks first under nearly every weighting, and a sceptical weighting brings it to about a third.

Last secure date and place as a unit: York (Eboracum), 10 December 107 to 9 December 108, building a fortress gate (the dedication slab RIB 665; the reference is explained under "Fixed points"). The careers of its officers require the legion to exist until c. 123/124 (Sextius Florentinus, its commander, and Aemilius Karus, a junior officer) and probably until c. 125–128, the end of the service of Novius Crispinus, another junior officer, whose tribunate falls c. 124–128. That is the latest date the careers require the legion to have existed, and it is not the date it ended: the end lies somewhere between the late 120s and 165, when the list of legions omits it, and the case for the early part of that window is the absence of any later trace together with the British crisis of 122–125. If the legion ended in Britain, the evidence supports no finer date than c. 122–130. The Nijmegen finds come from a detachment of the legion rather than the whole unit. No inscription, papyrus, discharge certificate or text places the legion, or any soldier of it, outside Britain after the early 120s.

Fixed points

The question is first of all one of dating. Everything below is sorted by how it is dated: by an emperor's titles or the names of the year's two consuls (the senior magistrates, by whom Romans dated their years) on the stone (secure), by the arithmetic of a senator's career whose later posts are dated (career), or by the archaeological context of an undated object (context). The assessment above rests on the first two classes only.

A word on the references, which follow the conventions of the field. Inscriptions are cited by their numbers in the standard collections, and each link opens the record. RIB is Roman Inscriptions of Britain, the corpus of every Roman inscription found in Britain, now online. CIL is the Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum, the Berlin corpus of all Latin inscriptions, in volumes numbered by region. ILS is Hermann Dessau's selection of the most important Latin inscriptions (1892 to 1916). AE is L'Année épigraphique, the annual bulletin of new finds since 1888. EDCS and HD numbers are records in the two large online databases, Clauss-Slaby and Heidelberg. Legions are named by number and title, so VI Victrix is the Sixth Legion Victrix; the Ninth's title Hispana records service in Spain under Augustus, the first emperor (27 BC to AD 14). Roman emperors dated their inscriptions by their yearly renewed tribunician power, so "trib. pot. XII" means the twelfth year of Trajan's. The ancient writers cited are Tacitus, whose Annals and Agricola were written about 98 to 117; the Historia Augusta (SHA), a set of imperial biographies from the late fourth century; Fronto, tutor to the emperor Marcus Aurelius, whose letters date from the 160s; Cassius Dio, whose Roman History was written in the early third century and survives for these years only in a summary made by Xiphilinus, an eleventh-century Byzantine monk; and Lucian, a satirist of the second century.

Two conclusions follow from the table. The traditional date of 117 to 119 for the legion's end is impossible: Florentinus' command cannot be pushed back into Trajan's reign, which ended in 117, because his governorship of Narbonensis (the province of southern Gaul) in 123/4 or 124/5 and his governorship of Arabia (the province annexed in 106, roughly modern Jordan) in 127 leave no room for it (Eck 1972; Anthony Birley, the historian of Roman Britain's governors, 2005, 228; Hodgson 2021, 6). And the legion vanished from the army list at some point between the late 120s and 165. The problem is to determine what happened in that window, and where.

Britain

Every entry for the legion in Roman Inscriptions of Britain was retrieved and read: 133 records. They are 11 stone inscriptions; 116 stamped tiles (legions stamped their name on the roof tiles and bricks they made) from at least 16 dies (the carved stamps pressed into the wet clay) at 14 findspots; two graffiti, one a scratched mention on a London vessel of an imaginifer, the soldier who carried the emperor's portrait (RIB 2501.7), the other a single letter traced with a finger on one of the Carlisle tiles before firing (RIB 2491.72, the same object as one of the stamps); two fragments that cannot be assigned; one rejected stamp (RIB 2462.17*, a modern misreading); and one forgery (RIB 3542*). One of the eleven stones, an altar from Godmanstone in Dorset, RIB 3047, does not surface in the RIB search index and was found by walking the numbers.

The last dated act of the legion is the York gate. The text, with the emperor's twelfth tribunician year, runs:

[I]mp(erator) Caesar [divi N]ervae fil(ius) Ne[rva Trai]anus Aug(ustus) Ger[m(anicus) Dacicus po]ntifex maximu[s tribuniciae po]testatis XII imp(erator) V[I co(n)s(ul) V p(ater) p(atriae) portam] per leg(ionem) VIIII Hi[sp(anam) fecit] RIB 665, York. Trajan's twelfth tribunician power ran from 10 December 107 to 9 December 108. Translation: "The Emperor Caesar Nerva Trajan Augustus, son of the deified Nerva, conqueror of Germany and Dacia, pontifex maximus, in his twelfth year of tribunician power, six times acclaimed imperator, five times consul, father of his country, built this gate through the agency of the Ninth Legion Hispana."

Four features of this record bear on the question. York is the legion's home to the end of the dated record. The RIB editors place the base there "until the legion's departure from Britain, probably towards the end of Trajan's reign", but the stones themselves only show the Sixth Legion, which took over York, present there "about a.d. 120" (RIB 659, 680) and on the river Tyne, at the eastern end of the later Wall, under the governor Platorius Nepos in 122 (RIB 1319). Pontius Laelianus, a young senatorial officer of the Sixth, crossed with it "from Germany into Britain" (cum qua ex Germania in Britanniam transiit, CIL VI 41146), and his consulship of 145 puts that crossing at about 122. The Ninth had therefore left York by about 122.

The north-western stamps form a distinct group. All 85 legible York dies write the numeral as IX; the stamps from Carlisle, Stanwix and Scalesceugh, three sites in Cumbria near the western end of the later Wall, write it VIIII, as do the single Nijmegen die and the York gate itself. The form of the numeral is not a date, but the Carlisle and Scalesceugh tiles are the only British evidence of the legion that could be later than the York gate, and Eric Birley, the Durham historian of the Roman army, built on them in 1971 the idea that the legion moved to a new base at Carlisle in the early 120s. Because the tiles are undated, the idea is possible but unproven.

No inscription of the Ninth has been found on Hadrian's Wall. The legions that built it recorded their stretches on small inscribed stones set into the work, and those name the Second Legion Augusta, the Sixth Victrix and the Twentieth Valeria Victrix. Under any hypothesis the Ninth was gone or elsewhere before the main building seasons; under a British loss in the mid-120s it could have worked a season or two at most, and the earliest sector's third legion is in any case unnamed. This absence is weak evidence and is weighed as such below.

Fourth, there is no record on any material other than stone and tile. No military diploma (the bronze discharge certificate given to soldiers on completing their service), writing tablet, lead sealing or papyrus names the legion at any date, and for the whole empire no record of any kind names it after the early 120s.

The officers

Eighteen men are known to have served in the legion, and two more are useful comparisons. Their posts need a word of explanation. A legion was commanded by a legate, a senator in his thirties. Under him served one senatorial tribune, a young man of about twenty at the start of his career (the tribunus laticlavius, named for the broad purple stripe on his tunic), and five equestrian tribunes from the second rank of Roman society. The camp prefect (praefectus castrorum) was the senior professional soldier, third in command, usually a former primus pilus, the chief centurion. Centurions commanded the eighty-man centuries; a miles is a private soldier. Senators moved through a fixed ladder of civil and military posts, and each senatorial career here was re-dated from its fixed points using the standard intervals: the senatorial tribunate at about 19 to 23; the quaestorship, the first magistracy, at 25; the praetorship, the second magistracy, at 30 or a little later; the consulship for a new man (the first of his family in the senate) at about 42; a legionary command of about three years; and a praetorian proconsulship, a one-year governorship of a minor province, held a few years after the praetorship. The only item that would carry the legion to 140 therefore depends on an identification that is open, and it cannot serve as a secure latest date.

Two men are added to the usual list. An anonymous equestrian honoured in Rome (CIL VI 41280) served in the Ninth and went on to be procurator (financial administrator) of "the new province of Arabia", so after 106 and, on the wording, not long after; the Heidelberg database dates him 106 to 161 on the explicit ground that 161 was "the destruction of legio IX Hispana", so its date presupposes the Elegeia theory and cannot be used as evidence for it. M. Cocceius Severus (CIL V 7159), chief centurion of the Ninth and then camp prefect of the Tenth Legion Gemina, was used by Eric Birley in 1948 to argue for a late date, on the ground that his family name Cocceius shows citizenship granted by the emperor Nerva (96 to 98); the name could as well be an ancestor's, and the stone gives no usable date. Aelius Asclepiades, a private soldier of "legio IX" from Cilicia in southern Turkey, buried at Naples (CIL X 1769), has been cited as a recruit enlisted in the East under Hadrian, whose family name Aelius was; without a first name the name proves no personal grant of citizenship, the legion has no title on the stone, and even on the favourable reading his eight years' service ends about 125 (Keppie 1989, 252–3; Hodgson 2021, 18–19).

From careers to years

Statements like "hardly earlier than the mid-120s" hide a range of possible years, some more likely than others. To make that range explicit, each of the three datable careers was simulated 200,000 times from its fixed point, drawing each interval between posts at random from a span with a most likely value in the middle (a triangular distribution); the script career_model.py and its output are published with this page, and the random seed 20260914 makes the run repeatable. Karus' tribunate is drawn as 144 minus 17 to 27 years, with 22 the most likely value; Crispinus' as 150 minus 18 to 30, with 24 most likely; Florentinus' command ends one to three years before a proconsulate of 123/4 or 124/5 and lasts two to four years. The quantity of interest is the latest year in which any of the three was still serving in the legion: the year to which the officers alone require the legion to exist. The model uses nothing that Ritterling did not have in 1925, and its output depends entirely on the career intervals assumed, which are the standard ones and are stated. One check of those intervals on a known case is possible. Applied to Pontius Laelianus, the tribune of the Sixth Legion who was consul in 145, the same distribution puts the start of his tribunate at a middle value of 123, with 90% of the simulated values between 120 and 126, and the year in which he is known to have crossed with the legion, 122, falls inside that span. A single case shows that the intervals are consistent with a known career; it does not validate them.

The result is stable at the early end. Under every setting the legion existed in 122 and almost certainly in 124. Under every setting except the disputed Numisius identification, the officers stop requiring it to exist by 131. The median last-attested year is 126, and 90% of the simulated values fall between 123 and 130.

The model answers one question and not a second. The question it answers is the latest year to which the officers' records require the legion to have existed. The question it does not answer is the year the legion ceased to exist, which it cannot estimate. The last inferred attestation is a lower limit on the date of the end. A legion that was still in being in 128 leaves no more trace in these careers than one that ended in 135, because the next generation of tribunes would only be attested through consulships of the 150s, and the last officers whose consulships we happen to have are those of 144 and 150. The absence of later attestations is weighed below as one argument among the others; it does not measure the end. That is why the date and place of the end have to be argued from the whole record, and why the interval given for a British ending is c. 122–130 and not narrower.

Set against this, the fortress is 16.5 hectares (a full legionary fortress is usually 20 or more), which Jules Bogaers and Jan Kees Haalebos, its excavators, themselves conceded in 1975 "offered insufficient room even for a full legion" once the British detachment is allowed for; its civilian settlement outside the walls, the canabae, was largely abandoned by 105 (the coin specialist Fleur Kemmers 2006); and its structural occupation ended about 125 to 130 (P.J.M. van der Veen, in a 2025 study of the fortress, ch. 4 and 10, from coins and from the potters' stamps on samian, the fine red tableware), with 221 of the 227 post-104 tile stamps belonging to units of 104 to about 130. The Ninth's share of those stamps is three, from one die: about 1.3%, against 86% for the vexillatio Britannica, the task force from the British legions. No other base on the Lower Rhine had room for it. Of the other legionary bases, Neuss ceased to be one in 103, Vetera II near Xanten held the Sixth Victrix and then the Thirtieth Ulpia Victrix, and Bonn held the First Minervia. After 122 the two German provinces had two legions each and every base is accounted for.

Duncan Campbell's best point against the detachment reading (2010, 52) is the altar from Aachen, the spa town 100 km from Nijmegen, set up by Latinius Macer, a camp prefect of the Ninth: a camp prefect belongs with a legion, not with a detachment, and "it would have been odd for the primus pilus to receive a promotion while absent in charge of a vexillation". Three considerations weigh against it. Macer's altar is a private vow at a spa and dates nothing; a praefectus castrorum is exactly the officer one would second to a large detachment holding a fortress, as Haalebos suggested; and if the whole legion had sat at Nijmegen for a decade it would have left more than one tile die, when X Gemina left hundreds. Campbell is right that the 1959 tile was found in a datable layer (Bogaers placed it in the level of the fortress's third building phase or the destruction layer above it, in the third officer's house north of the east gate), but a tile in a destruction layer dates the moment the layer formed and says nothing about how long the legion had been there. The archaeologist Miles Russell has made the opposite claim, that the tiles "all seem to date to the 80s"; it has no support in the excavation record, because the Tenth Legion's fortress of the 80s and 90s is the best-dated horizon on the site, and the Ninth's stamps are not in it.

So the Nijmegen stamps show a detachment, most probably part of the British task force that held the fortress from about 104 until the Thirtieth Legion took over about 120. They do not show the whole legion on the Rhine in the 120s, and the fortress had no room for it.

Three endings

Britain, c. 122–130 probable, c. 50–65%

The evidence for a serious war in Britain under Hadrian (emperor 117 to 138) is independent of the Ninth and is the best-attested of the three settings.

To these add the tombstone from Vindolanda, a fort just south of the Wall, of Titus Annius, centurion of the First Cohort of Tungrians, an auxiliary unit, "killed in the war" (in bell[o] … [inter]fectus, RIB 3364, c. 90–150); the expeditio Britannica, an emergency reinforcement of 3,000 legionaries drawn from three legions in Spain and Upper Germany, the province on the middle Rhine (ILS 2726), which only makes sense if legionary strength in Britain had collapsed; the arrival of the Sixth Legion as the one new legion, so that Britain's garrison stayed at three, which is a replacement, not a reinforcement; the Spaniards' refusal of a levy of recruits in the winter of 122/3; the interruption of building on Hadrian's Wall in about 123 and the burning of London about 125 (Graafstal 2025). None of these sources names the Ninth. Together they show a British crisis in the same years in which the officers' careers cease to attest the legion, and a Roman response of the kind that the loss of a legion would call for.

Whether the legion was annihilated or, as Hodgson and Graafstal prefer, defeated and then disbanded in disgrace, the evidence cannot say. A legion disbanded in disgrace would explain why no survivors' inscriptions name it after the 120s and why the loss is unrecorded in a reign whose narrative sources are, in any case, a later abridgement of Dio and the Historia Augusta biography. The objection that a disaster in or near the emperor's presence could not have gone unmentioned (the frontier historians David Breeze, Brian Dobson and Valerie Maxfield, 2012, 20; Matthew Symonds, a historian of the Wall, 2021) overrates our sources: the disappearance of the Twenty-Second Legion Deiotariana in the same generation is equally unmentioned.

The Bar Kokhba war, 132–136 c. 14–19%

τότε δὴ τότε τοὺς κρατίστους τῶν στρατηγῶν ὁ Ἁδριανὸς ἐπ᾽ αὐτοὺς ἔπεμψεν, ὧν πρῶτος Ἰούλιος Σεουῆρος ὑπῆρχεν, ἀπὸ Βρεττανίας ἧς ἦρχεν ἐπὶ τοὺς Ἰουδαίους σταλείς. … Πολλοὶ μέντοι ἐν τῷ πολέμῳ τούτῳ καὶ τῶν Ῥωμαίων ἀπώλοντο· διὸ καὶ ὁ Ἁδριανὸς γράφων πρὸς τὴν βουλὴν οὐκ ἐχρήσατο τῷ προοιμίῳ τῷ συνήθει τοῖς αὐτοκράτορσιν, ὅτι «εἰ αὐτοί τε καὶ οἱ παῖδες ὑμῶν ὑγιαίνετε, εὖ ἂν ἔχοι· ἐγὼ καὶ τὰ στρατεύματα ὑγιαίνομεν». Dio 69.13.2 and 69.14.3, in Xiphilinus' summary. "Then indeed Hadrian sent his best generals against them, the first of whom was Julius Severus, dispatched from Britain, where he was governor … Many Romans, too, perished in this war; and so Hadrian, writing to the senate, did not use the opening customary for emperors, 'if you and your children are well, it is well; I and the armies are well'."

The case for this ending rests on three facts: a governor was summoned from Britain, Hadrian admitted heavy losses, and a legion is missing afterwards. Against it, not one inscription of the Ninth exists in Judaea, Syria, Arabia or Egypt, in the best-documented military theatre of the period, and Menahem Mor, the Israeli historian of the revolt, in his surveys of the war's forces (1986, 2016) found the legion's participation "unfounded". The legion would have needed a base from about 124 to 132, and none is available on the Rhine, the Danube or in the East. The summons of Julius Severus shows that Hadrian took a general from Britain; it does not show that he took a legion. The only legion that can be shown to vanish in the East in these years is the Twenty-Second Deiotariana, last attested at its base at Nicopolis outside Alexandria on 4 August 119 (BGU I 140, a papyrus in Berlin) and, like the Ninth, absent from the list of 165. Eck (1999) has argued from Fronto that the war cost Rome a legion; if so, the Egyptian legion, which was already stationed in the East, is the better candidate.

Elegeia, Armenia, 161 c. 3–5%

… Οὐολόγαισος δὲ ὁ τῶν Πάρθων βασιλεὺς … στρατόπεδόν τε ὅλον Ῥωμαϊκὸν τὸ ὑπὸ Σεβηριανῷ ἐν Ἐλεγείᾳ … περισχὼν … κατετόξευσε καὶ διέφθειρε. Dio 71.2.1, in Xiphilinus' summary: the Parthian king Vologaeses "surrounded a whole Roman legion under Severianus at Elegeia … shot it down and destroyed it" (Severianus was governor of Cappadocia, the province in eastern Turkey facing Armenia; Elegeia lay in Armenia). Lucian, Alexander 27, a satire on a false prophet who had encouraged the campaign, has Severianus "cut to pieces with his whole army by Osroes" (αὐτῇ στρατιᾷ ὑπὸ τοῦ Ὀσρόου κατακοπείς). Neither author names the legion.

Werner Eck proposed the Ninth in 1972 on the strength of the Numisius diploma: if the tribune of CIL XI 5670 was consul in 161, the legion existed under Antoninus Pius, and the only recorded loss of a legion after that is Elegeia. Each step of the argument is weak. The Numisius identification is the less likely reading of the stone, as shown above. Cappadocia's two legions, the Twelfth Fulminata and the Fifteenth Apollinaris, both survive on the list of 165, so Severianus' "legion" must be an unattested third legion, or a legion-sized force of detachments, or Dio's rhetoric. No inscription of the Ninth exists in Cappadocia or anywhere in Asia Minor (Turkey) later than Caristanius Fronto, who commanded the legion in the 70s. The hypothesis also requires a full legion to have left no inscription for more than thirty years, in the decades when Romans set up more inscriptions than at any other time, and then to have been destroyed without any of its former soldiers leaving a stone.

Elsewhere, or disbanded without a record c. 17–23%

This residual covers a quiet end: a legion so reduced in the British war that it was merged into the Sixth Victrix or the Thirtieth Ulpia Victrix, a disbandment on the Continent after a withdrawal, or a loss in some episode our sources do not preserve (the fighting against the Moors in North Africa or the Sarmatians on the Danube in the 120s, an unrecorded eastern movement before 132, a disbandment later than 130). Its share is kept wide for three reasons: the weights in the next section are the author's judgements; the officers' silence after c. 128 is compatible with any of these endings; and one legion, the Twenty-Second, did disappear in this generation without any record of how.

Weighing it

Five pieces of evidence bear on where the legion ended. They were weighed in the way evidence is weighed in any inquiry: start with a prior probability for each ending, before the evidence is considered; ask how likely each piece of evidence would be if that ending were true (its likelihood); and combine them to get the probability of each ending given all the evidence (the posterior). Each likelihood was assigned not as a single number but as a range from a base reading to a sceptical one, the sceptical end being what a determined critic of the British hypothesis could reasonably assume. The prior for Britain was likewise ranged from 30% down to 15%, with the remainder split between the alternatives in the proportions 30:25:15. The posterior was then computed for 100,000 random draws across those ranges (the script scenario_weights_v2.py and its output are published with this page). The British crisis enters once, as item C: the attested losses and the reinforcement that answered them are one event, and counting them separately would count the same crisis twice.

Every percentage in it is conditional on the four hypotheses being exhaustive and on the ranges assigned; a different reader with different ranges will get different numbers, which is why the ranges are printed. Dropping any one item at the midpoint reading moves Britain from 52% to 29% (without the careers, or without the no-base argument), 43% (without the crisis) or 73% (without the Wall silence, which counts against it). Two cautions apply to the items themselves. A and B are not fully independent: both are, in part, the absence of later evidence, and a reader who thinks they are the same argument twice should weight the result toward the "without A" row. And item C describes a war that happened whatever became of the Ninth; it shows that there was an occasion for a British loss, and it does not show that one occurred.

The date within Britain cannot be fixed closely. The Sixth had York by 122 and the Ninth still had a commander and a senatorial tribune in 122 to 124, so the legion was then somewhere else in the province, plausibly the north-west where its VIIII-stamped tiles lie. The reinforcement of 3,000 legionaries and the Spanish levy belong to about 123 to 125, and Graafstal places the crisis in early 123. Those are the years in which a loss would best fit the surrounding evidence, but the careers are compatible with an end anywhere up to about 130, and nothing in the record excludes it. The interval given here is therefore c. 122–130, with the years just after Hadrian's visit as the most likely part of it; a two- or three-year range would claim more than the evidence supports.

The argument so far

The hypotheses were developed in the following order. Mommsen (1885) had the legion destroyed at York soon after 108. Ritterling (1925) saw from Karus and Crispinus that the end must fall after 120, and thought of a second British revolt about 125. Eric Birley (1948, 1971) built the eastern alternative on Cocceius Severus, the Carlisle tiles and the Nijmegen finds, and Bogaers (1967) placed the whole legion at Nijmegen in the 120s. Eck (1972) took it east to Elegeia on the Numisius diploma. Keppie (1989) dismantled the eastern case item by item, and Haalebos (2000) and the later Nijmegen excavators reduced the continental presence to a detachment of 104 to 120. Hodgson (2021) argued for a defeat followed by disbandment in Britain, in 122 or shortly after. Graafstal (2025) explores the connections between the legion's disappearance, a security crisis and the interruption of work on the Wall, and frames the result as a possibility to be examined, not a settled identification: the convergence of evidence, in his abstract's words, "invites a fresh examination of the possibility that a major security crisis erupted following the imperial visit of a.d. 122". Neither paper has yet drawn a published response, and Campbell, Anthony Birley and Keppie hold different positions. Because the field has no settled verdict, this page cannot confirm one; it can support one interpretation over the others and state how strongly. Hodgson's own summary of the historiography is worth quoting: "since the 1970s the possibility that the Ninth was in Britain when it ceased to exist has disappeared so thoroughly from the educated public consciousness that many believe its departure from the island to be a matter of fact" (2021, 16, as quoted by Graafstal 2025, 47). Campbell's book of 2018, The Fate of the Ninth, is the strongest sustained case against a British ending, and it has not been read. It (Glasgow 2018, 155 pages; Kindle edition 2020) has no preview in any bookseller's or library database, no scanned copy exists, and no journal reviewed it; two searches in September 2026 found nothing that could be read legitimately. What was read in full is everything else he has published on the question: his 2010 article in the magazine Ancient Warfare, all 57 posts of his blog (2019–2025), and all 25 of his contributions on the Ninth to RomanArmyTalk, an online forum on the Roman army, from 2010 to February 2026, which include his only recorded reaction to Hodgson 2021. The account of his case that follows is drawn from those. Where the book adds arguments, this page does not know them; the Finnish historian Kai Juntunen, in the journal Arctos (54, 2020, 168 n. 7), cites its pp. 134–39 for the arguments for and against Elegeia.

His case, in his own words where possible, is this. The "British war of 119" and the link between the Sixth's arrival and the Ninth's disappearance rest on an assumption of the nineteenth-century epigraphist Bartolomeo Borghesi and on the coin specialist Harold Mattingly's dating of a coin issue, and "there is no direct evidence for any of the required elements" (2010, 50, 53). The officers' careers push the legion's existence to at least c. 122–3 (Florentinus, Karus, Crispinus) and, if Numisius Iunior is the consul of 161, to c. 140; he reports Keppie's father-and-son solution without rebutting it (2010, 53). The Nijmegen tile is not a stray: Bogaers "had a securely stratified artefact" (forum, 2011), and with the stamped mixing bowl from De Holdeurn, the legion's tile and pottery works outside Nijmegen, the harness pendant from Ewijk and the Aachen camp prefect it places the legion, not a detachment, in Lower Germany, the province on the lower Rhine, so that "evidence of the legion's departure from Britain does at least exist, whereas there is no evidence that it remained" (blog, 27 May 2023). Ian Richmond's story that the legion was cashiered in disgrace is "fantastical", and the burden lies on whoever revives it. Between the eastern options he was undecided in 2010; by 2023 he leaned to Elegeia, observing that "nobody in the last 40 years has suggested" a loss to the Jews, "whereas Cassius Dio records the destruction of a legion by the Parthians"; he states no probability. To Hodgson's argument that every eastern legionary base is known and none is vacant, his reply (forum, 20 February 2026) is that it is "probably the biggest red herring. We need to be aware of unknown unknowns! How many forts/fortresses in Cappadocia can you name?" He has abandoned his earlier argument from the VIIII and IX spellings (blog, 2025). Nowhere in the texts read does he argue that a loss in Britain in the mid or late 120s is impossible; he records, without answering, Ritterling's allowance for "a second British revolt towards the middle or in the second half of the AD 120s" (2010, 51).

The evidence set out above bears on his case at three points, and a reader should weigh them rather than take them as a refutation of a book that has not been read. First, the interval given here for a British ending is c. 122–130, not 117–119, so his strongest point, the absence of evidence for a war in 119, is accepted here. Second, on Nijmegen, the stratified tile shows that men of the Ninth were there, which nobody disputes; the quantities are what carry the argument for a detachment, three stamps from one die beside 196 of the British vexillation, in a fortress the excavators now hold to have emptied by c. 125/130. The Aachen prefect remains his best evidence for a large presence, and it is undated. Third, his "unknown unknowns" objection is aimed at exactly item B of the weighting above. The sceptical end of that item's range and the residual hypothesis are the places in the calculation where his objection applies, and the "without B" row shows what accepting it in full does: Britain falls to 29% and Bar Kokhba moves ahead of it. His last point applies equally to his own view. No stone places the legion as a whole anywhere after the York gate of 108; there is no evidence that it remained in Britain, and equally no evidence that it left, apart from a handful of objects from a fortress held by a detachment.

Russell's account of 2011, for the BBC, places the end in the right province, but on two grounds that fail: the officers show the legion alive past his date, and the redating of the Nijmegen tiles to the 80s has no basis in the excavated layers. The assessment here is closer to Ritterling's of 1925, to Hodgson's of 2021 and to Graafstal's of 2025 than to either popular account.

What would change the answer

Any inscription, diploma or papyrus naming the legion, or a serving soldier of it, with a date or context after about 130 would move the answer east at once: a tombstone in Judaea, Syria or Cappadocia, a building stone, a discharge list. So would proof that the tribune of the Numisius stone (CIL XI 5670) is the consul of 161, for instance a new copy of the stone with a consulship, or a military diploma giving the consul's full name Q. Camurius Numisius Iunior; that alone would push the legion's existence to about 140 and revive Eck's hypothesis. Nijmegen finds of the Ninth in contexts of Hadrian's reign at legion scale (several dies, a stamped building, an inscription of a serving officer dated after 122) would do the same for the Rhine. A Ninth building stone on Hadrian's Wall in a sector dated after 124 would show a legion at work rather than lost. In the other direction, a firmly dated York or Carlisle context showing the legion still in garrison in 125 or later would tighten the British date without changing the place.

The answer would not be shaken by a new career inscription from the reign of Trajan or the early years of Hadrian, by a further Nijmegen tile of the known die, or by an undated stray such as the graffito from Linz noted below.

What is new here

The conclusion is not new. The hypothesis of a defeat followed by disbandment in Britain in or soon after 122 was argued by Nick Hodgson in volume 52 (2021) of Britannia, the journal of Romano-British studies, and Erik Graafstal in volume 56 (2025) of the same journal connected the legion's disappearance with the military crisis after Hadrian's visit and the interruption of building on the Wall. Ritterling had already placed the end in Britain after 120 in 1925. This page develops their line of argument and finds it probable; it does not establish it, and it does not show that the field already favours it. This page presents no new evidence. No inscription, papyrus or object unknown to the literature was found.

New analysis of existing evidence, each item to be judged on its own terms and none validated by the conclusion it reaches, comes to five things. The first is a complete catalogue, with a link to the database record for each item: all 133 RIB records for Britain, and an empire-wide sweep of the Heidelberg and Clauss-Slaby databases (245 rows, about 195 distinct monuments once duplicates between the two are matched) with every query recorded. It shows that no attestation of the legion in either database survives scrutiny as later than the 120s: the two "late" database dates, 141–160 for Asclepiades and 106–161 for CIL VI 41280, rest on no criteria and on the Elegeia theory respectively. The second is the career model, which converts the standard career intervals into a probability curve and shows the officers require the legion in 124 at 88%, in 126 at 51% and in 130 at 6%; it measures the floor on the legion's existence, not its end. The third is the explicit likelihood table with ranges and a distribution of posteriors, which states what each reading of the evidence implies and what a sceptic has to assume, and which shows that the ordering of the hypotheses is robust while the probability of the leading one is not. The fourth is the quantified Nijmegen ratio: three stamps from one die against about 227 post-104 stamps, alongside the fortress size and end date. The fifth is two under-used items, CIL VI 41280 and the circularity of its database dating, and CIL V 7159 read with its Clauss-Slaby text.

Positions already argued by others that this analysis supports: the impossibility of 117–119 (Ritterling 1925; Anthony Birley 2005); the detachment reading of Nijmegen (Haalebos 2000; van der Veen 2025); the father-son reading of Numisius (Keppie 1989); the absence of any eastern evidence (Keppie; Mor); and the British setting in the early 120s, argued by Hodgson 2021 and explored by Graafstal 2025. An analysis that reaches the same conclusion as its proponents by weighing the same evidence has not tested that conclusion independently; the agreement is support for it, and it should not be read as confirmation. What this page adds is the synthesis and the explicit statement of how much the answer depends on judgement.

Notes on references and limits

Sextius Florentinus' tomb is CIL III 87 = 14148,10; it is not in Dessau's ILS, and the reference "ILS 1057" sometimes given for it is wrong. Keppie's paper on the eastern hypothesis is "The fate of the Ninth Legion: a problem for the eastern provinces?", in French and Lightfoot (eds), The Eastern Frontier of the Roman Empire (British Archaeological Reports, international series 553, 1989), 247–255, reprinted with addenda in Legions and Veterans (2000); it is not a 1996 supplement to the Journal of Roman Archaeology. The last attestation of XXII Deiotariana is the papyrus BGU I 140 of 4 August 119; "CIL III 14147 = ILS 2483" is not a terminus for that legion. The papyrus text could not be re-read online for this page and is cited from the standard editions. Novius Crispinus' intermediate posts were iuridicus (the emperor's judicial deputy) in north-west Spain, commander of the First Legion Italica and governor of Narbonensis, not a Spanish and Cappadocian sequence.

A recently published graffito from Linz in Austria, Im(m)un(is) Vo() leg(ionis) IX (AE 2020, 932), "Vo…, a soldier exempt from fatigues, of the ninth legion", names a ninth legion without title and without date. It is flagged in the catalogue and given no weight. Two unprovenanced auction objects inscribed IX HISP are likewise flagged. A dedication of 161–169 for the safety of the emperors Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus from Corporales in the province of León, Spain (AE 1984, 556), contains "leg viiii" only in a corrupt transcription and is excluded.

Two of the most important modern works could not be read in full. Campbell's book The Fate of the Ninth (2018) could not be obtained: no preview, no scan, no review copy, no journal review. The account of his case above rests on his 2010 article, his blog and his forum posts, all read in full; any argument that exists only in the book is unknown to this page, and the page does not claim to have answered it. Hodgson's 2021 article is behind the publisher's paywall. An exhaustive attempt (the publisher's site, the open-access finders Unpaywall, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, CORE and OpenAIRE, the Newcastle and Durham university repositories, Google Scholar and the Wayback Machine) found no legitimate copy. It was read in abstract and through the fourteen citations and three verbatim quotations in Graafstal 2025 and the citations in Symonds 2022; its treatment of the Aachen altar, of Numisius Iunior and of Campbell's book is not recoverable from those and is not characterised here. Haalebos 2000 and Ritterling's article in the Realencyclopädie, the German encyclopaedia of classical antiquity, were likewise reached through citation.

How the evidence was gathered

The evidence was collected along six lines. The British corpus was every record for the legion in RIB Online, read in full. The officers' inscriptions were taken from the Clauss-Slaby and Heidelberg databases and from Dessau, with Eck 1972 and Graafstal 2025. The Rhineland evidence came from Bogaers 1965 and 1967, Bogaers and Haalebos 1975, Kemmers 2006, van der Veen 2025, and the Heidelberg and Clauss-Slaby records. The literary sources were read in Latin and Greek from the online collections LacusCurtius, the Latin Library, remacle.org and Wikisource, and from scans of the Loeb editions. The scholarship was read from Mommsen to Graafstal, with Campbell's and Russell's texts where they are online. The sixth line was an empire-wide sweep through the search interfaces of the Heidelberg and Clauss-Slaby databases. The career simulation and the scenario weighting are short Python scripts with fixed random seeds, so every number above can be regenerated exactly.

The files behind this page are published with it: the British catalogue, the officer file with the Latin texts and the arithmetic, the Rhineland file, the literary sources with texts and translations, the scholarship survey, the database catalogue with its comparison-legion counts, the two model scripts with their outputs and the chart script, and the text of Graafstal 2025 with every passage in it that cites Hodgson.
