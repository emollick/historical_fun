Strasbourg · July to August 1518

The dancing plague of 1518

What made the poor of Strasbourg dance in the streets for weeks in July and August 1518, how many danced, and did anyone die?

What happened. On 14 or 15 July 1518, in Strasbourg, then a free city of the Holy Roman Empire on the Rhine, a woman began to dance in the street and kept it up for days. The city council had her taken to a shrine of St Vitus, the saint believed both to send this sickness and to cure it, in the hills above Saverne, a small town to the north-west; while she was on the road, others began to dance, about thirty-four within four days. The council restricted drums on 22 July, lodged the dancers in guild halls, sent them to the shrine by wagon on 23 July, wrote to its bishop on 25 July that the physicians called it a natural illness of the heat and the stars, banned all public dancing on 2 August, and voted a hundredweight candle for the saint on 31 August. The council's decree speaks of up to fifty dancing at once; the chronicle of the Imlin family of Strasbourg, written at the time, counts more than two hundred over four weeks.

Why it is a mystery. The cause was not diagnosed in 1518 and is still disputed: the physicians said natural illness; the chroniclers of the next century said a curse, a punishment and fraud; E. Louis Backman, a Swedish physician and historian of religious dance, proposed in 1952 that the dancers were poisoned by ergot, a fungus of rye whose poison causes convulsions and gangrene, and that became the popular explanation; John Waller, a historian of medicine, argued in 2008 for a trance brought on by hunger, fear and belief in the saint's curse, spread by sight; and Gregor Rohmann, a German historian of the Middle Ages, and Robert Bartholomew, a sociologist who studies outbreaks of mass hysteria, read the dancing as a religious rite. The facts are disputed too: the popular story has four hundred dancers, fifteen deaths a day, a first dancer named Frau Troffea (a name found only in Paracelsus, the Swiss physician, who wrote on the sickness about 1526 to 1532) and a council that built a stage and hired musicians and dancers to keep the sick dancing, while the surviving texts give figures from fifty to four hundred (the four hundred rests on one nineteenth-century reading of one word in one manuscript). The first reports of deaths come sixty and a hundred years after the event, from Daniel Specklin, the city architect, writing about 1580, and from Michael Kleinlawel's rhymed chronicle of 1625; the council's own minutes burned with the city library in 1870 and are known only from extracts made in the eighteenth century, though a few originals survive, among them the minutes of the three councillors who questioned the dancers' families on 23 July and the letter to the bishop. The American historian Erik Midelfort (1999) gave the sober account of the sources, and the Strasbourg historians Élisabeth Clementz and Georges Bischoff, in a 2018 exhibition, reduced the outbreak to about fifty dancers with no contemporary death.

How it was judged. Every text that describes the dancing was read in the order it was written, from the council's papers of 1518 to the chronicles of the following century, in the printed editions (above all the medical historian Alfred Martin's study of 1914, which printed the council's papers) and in the page images, with one exception, the account of the cathedral schoolmaster Hieronymus Gebwiler from the early 1520s, known only through later writers; each element of the modern story was traced to the text in which it first appears, the phantom outbreak of 1418 listed in half the modern literature, for instance, to the jurist Johann Schilter, who printed the council's orders under that wrong year in 1698; and the explanations were tested against that record and against three kinds of evidence the earlier writers did not have: the weather of the growing seasons of 1515 to 1518 reconstructed from tree rings and written weather records, the grain prices of those years, and the modern medical case series of outbreaks that spread through schools and villages by suggestion. What tipped the balance was the record of 1518 itself: the texts written that year describe poor people set off by watching others dance or by the sound of a bagpipe, a verdict of natural illness from the physicians, up to fifty afflicted at once and no death, while the four hundred, the daily deaths, the stage and the name Troffea each enter the story later at a date that can be fixed, and ergot fails on eight counts, among them that the rye eaten in July 1518 had grown in the summer of 1517, the third driest of the century at Strasbourg. The verdict is a behavioural epidemic, an outbreak spread by sight and sound rather than by anything eaten or caught, among the poor of a city at the end of two years of dearth (failed harvests and high grain prices), at about 85 percent, with ergot rejected; a document of 1518 recording deaths among the dancers would cut that share to about 70 percent and reopen the organic causes, a poison or a disease.

Verdict

A behavioural epidemic among the poor of a city at the end of two years of dearth, spread by watching and by music, and given its shape by the belief that St Vitus sends the dance and cures it: about 85 percent that the cause was behavioural rather than a poison or an infection, and ergot rejected. Up to fifty dancing at a time by the council's count, more than two hundred over four weeks by a chronicle written at the time, and no death in any text read from the years 1518 to about 1580.

Probable

Every percentage is a judgment rather than a measurement: it depends on the five explanations weighed and on the weight given to each piece of evidence, and the table of what moves it is printed beside the headline. Backman's book of 1952 was not obtained, Waller's book was read only in library search snippets and Rohmann's book of 2013 was not read, so the opposing works are answered through their articles and through other writers.

About 65 percent that it was, more exactly, a psychogenic epidemic, one in which the symptoms pass from person to person by suggestion rather than by any germ or poison (mass psychogenic illness of the motor type, the modern name for such an outbreak of abnormal movement; "a natural illness" in the words of the physicians of 1518), and about 20 percent that the dancing was a culturally scripted rite, the reading of Martin 1914, Bartholomew 2000 and Rohmann 2013; the line between those two is partly vocabulary. Ergot about 5 percent, another organic disease about 5 percent, mostly fraud and begging about 5 percent. Accepting Specklin's and Kleinlawel's late reports of deaths as fact cuts the behavioural share to about 70; the most sceptical case, which also allows an ergot route through old granary stock and reads the physicians' diagnosis as an infection, leaves it at about 55, still leading, with ergot at 18 and another organic disease at 20. In the base case neither ergot nor another organic disease reaches ten percent.

The size of the outbreak, in the texts written at the time: one unnamed woman on Wednesday 14 or Thursday 15 July 1518; about 34 dancers within four days (Imlin chronicle); "uff fünfzig personen", up to fifty, afflicted at once in the council's own decree (its words survive in an eighteenth-century extract from the burned minutes, probably taken from the preamble of the dancing ban of 2 August), a figure for one moment; "more than 200" over four weeks in the Imlin chronicle, the only running total from the period, and nothing in the record contradicts it; "over 100 at once" only in Specklin, about 1580. The council restricted drums on 22 July, sent the sick to St Vitus at the Hohlenstein, a hollow rock above Saverne, on 23 July, told the bishop on 25 July that the physicians called it a natural illness of the stars and the heat and that it was coming to an end, banned public dancing until Michaelmas (29 September) on 2 August, made families and brotherhoods responsible for their sick on 11 August, and voted a hundredweight candle for the saint on 31 August. Nobody is recorded as having died in any of these texts. That is silence in the sources read; it is not proof that nobody died, and no parish or hospital register was searched.

The strongest rival is ergot poisoning, Backman's explanation of 1952 and the popular one. It fails on eight counts: the season (onset before the 1518 harvest), the weather of the crop year (1517 was the third driest summer of the century at Strasbourg in the Old World Drought Atlas, a reconstruction of summer soil moisture across Europe from tree rings, with a dry spring and a warm flowering), the regional form of the disease (the ergotism of Alsace, the region round Strasbourg, was the gangrenous form called St Anthony's fire, which produces burning pain and loss of limbs, and nothing in 1518 mentions burning limbs, gangrene, formication (a feeling of insects crawling under the skin) or cramps), its clinical course (convulsive ergotism keeps its victims in bed with spasms for weeks, and these dancers were on their feet in the streets for days), the way the dancing spread (a boy who watched, a woman who followed a bagpipe), the absence of recorded deaths, the physicians' diagnosis, and the end in August. The argument usually made against ergot, that its alkaloids would have decayed within a year, is unsound (Barger 1931, the monograph by George Barger that is still the standard history of ergot) and is not used: the rye harvest of 1515, which had classic ergot weather, could in principle have reached the poor through the granary, and ergot keeps a few percent because one poisoned person inside a larger behavioural outbreak cannot be excluded.

What is new here

  • "Fifteen a day" has one candidate source: the sentence of 1518 by Wilhelm Rem, a merchant of Augsburg who recorded the outbreak that year, "daß ain tag wol bei 15 menschen ankam", a count of new cases rather than deaths. Martin (1914, p. 114) quoted it only to fix the year. Waller's essay in the medical journal the Lancet (February 2009, "about 15 lives a day"; further reading Backman and Midelfort only) and his book ("fifteen were dying each day") give a death rate; Wikipedia took it from the Lancet on 8 April 2016. The descent from Rem to Waller is an inference from the wording, Waller's endnote and Backman's translation not having been seen. A new reading of a printed text.
  • 200 against 400: the Strasbourg librarian Rodolphe Reuss's 1875 edition of the Imlin chronicle from the manuscript reads "mehr dan 200" (page image checked); the Kleine Strassburger Chronik, an anonymous Strasbourg chronicle of the early seventeenth century, independently has "über 200"; 400 is the reading of 1879 by the psychiatrist Georg Witkowski, carried by Martin (1914), Midelfort (1999) and Waller. A press interview about Waller's book put 400 into the Wikipedia article on the day it was created, 8 August 2008. A new reading of printed editions.
  • The anonymous Basel chronicle (written before 1537) restored to 1518: its manuscript reads 1518; its editor, the Basel historian August Bernoulli, moved it to 1418 in 1902 because of Schilter's misdated council orders. It is the earliest narrative outside Strasbourg; its wording matches, in prose, six verses from an unidentified Strasbourg manuscript chronicle that Schilter printed in 1698 under the same wrong year; and it is the earliest source for "hundreds" as a round plural (the Imlin chronicle, written at the time, gives the figure of more than 200): its "several hundred" is the formula that the verses give as "many hundreds", a stock expression rather than a tally. A new reading of a printed edition.
  • The ergot hypothesis tested against reconstructed weather, for the first time in the literature that could be reached: a drought index of −6.8 for 1517 at Strasbourg in the Old World Drought Atlas (third driest summer of 1500–1600), a dry spring and a warm May and June; the cold June of 1518 is the one favourable sign, and it belongs to a crop not yet cut. The climate and price datasets are the one body of evidence not brought to the question before; the datasets are the published work of others, and their application here is new. The extraction from the atlas was run a second time from scratch and gives the same value (−6.787 at the Strasbourg cell).
  • The outbreak ended in August: "Dienstag post Adolphi" is 31 August 1518 (St Adelphus falls on 29 August), rather than 29 September as Martin read it; the letter of 25 July says the sickness was ending. A calendar correction.
  • The council's policy was containment rather than encouragement: drums restricted on 22 July, wagons to the shrine on 23 July, on 25 July a cooling diet and string music only, a general ban on 2 August, families and brotherhoods made responsible on 11 August. The platform on the horse market with people hired to dance with the sick, to drums and pipes, is Specklin's, written about 1580. The pilgrimage was imposed by the council (the Instruction of 23 July for the transport to the shrine), which weakens the reading of the dancing as a chosen rite; dancers who chose the rite appear only in Johann Wencker, a Strasbourg magistrate whose chronicle of 1637 copies Specklin. A new reading of the documents Martin printed.
  • Waller's supporting details corrected: the English sweat of 1517, an epidemic fever of the period, is a misdating of 1529; the "smallpox hospital" is the Blatterhaus, the house for syphilis; the leper house shows no surge (eight inmates and two deaths in 1518 to 1519); the Bundschuh of 1517 (a peasant plot on the Upper Rhine) was a conspiracy scare rather than a series of rebellions. A new reading of published work.
  • Two bad arguments retired: the claim that ergot alkaloids decay within a year (unsound; Barger 1931), and the 1951 mass poisoning at Pont-Saint-Esprit in southern France as a case of ergot-induced mass behaviour (organic mercury, according to the European Food Safety Authority in 2012). The case against ergot rests on season, weather, form, course, spread, fatality, diagnosis and end rather than on chemistry. Published literature applied.
  • No Strasbourg record of dancers in 1374, the year of the dancers whom the chronicle of Limburg on the Lahn condemned as devilry and heresy; the fifteenth-century cases on the Upper Rhine sorted (Zurich 1418 or 1428 and 1452, the cluster of 1463 at Metz, Obernai and Eberhardsklausen, dancers in the name of St John rather than St Vitus, and the town law of Rottweil, in Swabia, which in 1485 set a fine for the curse "sant Vitstantz", St Vitus's dance called down on another person). "Strasbourg 1418" is Schilter's error. A new reading of printed editions.

Already established by others. The verdict is the one Élisabeth Clementz and Georges Bischoff reached for the 2018 exhibition "1518, la fièvre de la danse": about fifty dancers, no contemporary death, Troffea an invention of Paracelsus, ergot rejected. They had already questioned how much contemporary testimony lies behind the familiar story, and this report (the full report is linked from this page) builds on their correction. Erik Midelfort (1999) gave the sober English account, one woman about 14 July, thirty-four within four days, the sick sent to Saverne, deaths only in Kleinlawel. John Waller's premise, a city in dearth and fear, is borne out by the climate and price record, and his account of how the dancing spread, by sight, is the one described in the surviving minutes of the three councillors of 23 July.

Where this verdict stands among historians

Agrees withÉlisabeth Clementz and Georges Bischoff (2018), Erik Midelfort (1999) and Alfred Martin (1914); shifts the popular account of 400 dancers, dozens of deaths and fifteen dying a day (Waller 2008 and 2009; Wikipedia)

What historians have said. Alfred Martin (1914) dated the outbreak to 1518, printed the council's papers and disposed of Schilter's 1418. Erik Midelfort (1999) gave the sober English account, with deaths only in Kleinlawel. Élisabeth Clementz and Georges Bischoff, for the 2018 exhibition, reduced the outbreak to "quelque cinquante personnes", found no death in any contemporary document, exposed Troffea as Paracelsus's invention and dismissed ergot. Martin and Midelfort carried Witkowski's 400 reading of the Imlin chronicle, and Waller took it from them. The popular account, from Waller's book of 2008 and articles of 2009 to Wikipedia, has 400 dancers, dozens of deaths, fifteen dying a day and a council that built a stage to keep them dancing. E. Louis Backman (1952) argued for ergot; Gregor Rohmann (2013) and Robert Bartholomew (2000) read the dancing as a religious rite rather than an illness.

The finding agrees with the Strasbourg historians of 2018, with Midelfort and with Martin, and builds on the 2018 exhibition's correction. It shifts the popular account: 400 dancers become up to 50 at a time and more than 200 over four weeks; dozens of deaths become none recorded in the sources read before about 1580; "fifteen dying a day" has one candidate source, Rem's count of fifteen new cases on one day, and its descent into the modern death rate is an inference rather than a traced citation; the council that staged the dancing becomes a council that contained it, Specklin (about 1580) being the only source for the stage. Ergot (Backman 1952) is rejected on eight counts.

What would overturn it

  • A document of 1518 that records deaths among the dancers: a hospital or parish register, or the lost account of Sebastian Brant, the author of The Ship of Fools and the city's chancellor in 1518, the full narrative that a note in the council's own register says can be found "in my chronicle", meaning Brant's. Deaths would cut the behavioural share to about 70 percent and reopen the organic causes.
  • The German original of the council's letter to the bishop of 25 July 1518 (ADBR 12 J 1495, its shelfmark in the departmental archives of the Bas-Rhin), read here only in a 2018 French translation, if it describes burning limbs, gangrene or cramps of the hands and feet.
  • A chronicle notice of a wet May and June 1517 at Strasbourg: the tree-ring drought value for 1517 is regional and safe because it is extreme, but the seasonal rainfall reconstructions for the sixteenth century are weak, and one wet fortnight at flowering would not show in them.
  • An ergotism epidemic recorded in Alsace or Baden in 1518, for instance in the accounts of the Antonite house at Isenheim, one of the hospitals of the Order of St Anthony that treated ergotism; none is known to Barger or to the later literature.
  • Evidence that the sick went to St Vitus of their own motion before the council organised the transport of 23 July, which would move weight from illness to rite without touching the verdict against ergot.
  • The Imlin manuscript reading 400, which would restore Witkowski without changing the council's fifty or the absence of deaths.

Limits

  • Gebwiler's contemporary account (ed. Stenzel 1926) was not read; it is known here through Midelfort and the material for teachers published by the 2018 exhibition.
  • The letter of 25 July 1518 was read only in the French translation published in 2018; the mandate of 2 August is not transcribed anywhere that could be reached.
  • Witkowski's paper of 1879 was not found online. Backman's book of 1952 was not obtained (about sixty snippets from a library full-text search). Waller's articles in the journals Endeavour, the Lancet and the Psychologist were read in full, but his book only in about 120 such snippets, so his endnote for "fifteen were dying each day" was not seen. Midelfort's chapter was read from the text of an archive.org scan whose provenance could not be confirmed.
  • Rohmann's book of 2013 was not read (his 2020 article, reviews of the book and an interview were used); Clementz's transcriptions of the 1518 documents in the 2018 catalogue (pp. 136–152) were not obtained.
  • The Alsatian economic historian Charles-Auguste Hanauer's price volume of 1878, the source of the Strasbourg rye prices, could not be opened; two later transcriptions disagree on the 1516 rye price.
  • The gridded climate reconstructions have low skill (they reproduce known weather poorly) before 1659; the drought value for 1517 is safe because it is extreme and regional; the seasonal rainfall figures are not.
  • Three words in the council's papers are unexplained ("pfrymen", "termen", "brucken" or "beucken").
  • No parish or hospital register was searched; the absence of deaths is an argument from the silence of every text written at the time.
