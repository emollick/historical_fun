======================================================================================================
PART 1. CAMBRIDGE CORE 'EXTRACT' (verbatim; = opening of the article, p. 45)
======================================================================================================
Classical sources describing the Hanging Gardens give a wealth of detail which has never matched up with information from cuneiform sources or archaeological finds. This study reconciles them. In doing so it shows how some confusions in Classical accounts may have their origin in Akkadian sources, and are not due simply to misunderstanding and error. The Hanging Gardens of Babylon, one of the Seven Wonders of the world in Classical tradition, were marvellous not merely for being raised upon vaults, but also for an innovative system for watering them. Popularly attributed to Nebuchadnezzar II, of the 6th century B.C., they are not mentioned in any of the copious and remarkably complete written sources for that king's reign, nor have they come to light in extensive excavation of his palaces in Babylon, carried out by a large German team over more than a quarter of a century.

PART 2. PAGE-BY-PAGE RECONSTRUCTION OF THE ARGUMENT
======================================================================================================
Conventions: "B-R n. X (p. Y)" = Bichler & Rollinger 2005 footnote X citing Dalley 1994, p. Y (their full footnotes and the sentences that carry them are in Part 4). Verbatim English inside German B-R sentences is Dalley's own wording. Footnote numbers "fn N" are Dalley's own notes (Part 3), whose order fixes the sequence of topics. Figures: Fig. 1 = the Ashurbanipal garden relief BM 124939 (cited B-R n. 86, 143: "Dalley 1994, 51 mit Fig. 1"); Fig. 3 = "Reconstruction drawing of Sennacherib's palace garden at Nineveh incorporating new evidence" by Terry Ball (fn 75; B-R n. 147: "Dalley 1994, 57f. mit Fig. 3"); Fig. 2 is presumably the British Museum Original Drawing cited in fn 74 (BM Or. Dr. V.1, reproduced in SAA III fig. 6), but this is inferred.

--- p. 45 (Introduction; the Classical problem) ---
* Opening paragraphs = the Extract above: the Classical descriptions "give a wealth of detail which has never matched up with information from cuneiform sources or archaeological finds. This study reconciles them"; "some confusions in Classical accounts may have their origin in Akkadian sources, and are not due simply to misunderstanding and error"; the gardens "were marvellous not merely for being raised upon vaults, but also for an innovative system for watering them"; "Popularly attributed to Nebuchadnezzar II ... they are not mentioned in any of the copious and remarkably complete written sources for that king's reign, nor have they come to light in extensive excavation of his palaces in Babylon" (fn 2-3: Woolley's Ur; Parrot on ziggurats/Tower of Babel — i.e. the earlier attempts to find the gardens at Babylon, including the ziggurat and the vaulted building in the Southern Palace; cf. fn 8 Finkel's survey of proposed locations and George on the Euphrates changing course).
* The Persian diversion of the Euphrates, VERBATIM (B-R n. 177, p. 45): "When the Persians captured Babylon in 539 B.C., a mere 23 years after the death of Nebuchadnezzar, or perhaps in 482 when Xerxes levelled the city wall, they diverted the Euphrates river and it continued in its diverted course long afterwards. For centuries it no longer ran alongside the north-west part of Nebuchadnezzar's palace. Any machinery set up there to water the gardens from the river would have become useless, and all the trees in the garden would soon have withered and died". [B-R n. 258 note that on p. 46 Dalley "selbst noch von der Existenz königlicher Gärten in Babylon ausgegangen [ist] — allerdings nur vor der vermeintlichen Flussumleitung durch die Perser", i.e. she allowed that Babylon had royal gardens before the diversion.]
* Herodotus: he did not mention the gardens although he described Babylon; "his informants were, as he declares, Chaldaeans" (VERBATIM, B-R n. 200, p. 45; cf. fn 4 quoting Sayce's verdict that Herodotus never visited Babylonia). Her long-standing argument (first in Dalley 1993b, 8f., "Vgl. auch Dalley 1994, 45", B-R n. 186): "By locating the Hanging Gardens in Nineveh another puzzle has been solved. When Herodotus described the city of Babylon in the fifth century B.C., he failed to mention the Hanging Gardens, and has been vilified for his unreliability in this matter. But with the gardens at Nineveh, his account is vindicated" (verbatim from the 1993 article, cited by B-R as the same point in 1994, 45).
* Xenophon's silence about gardens in Babylon (Cyropaedia) taken as a further indication that there was nothing to report there (B-R n. 92, p. 45).
* Ctesias dismissed as "not a reliable historian" and Diodorus' Hanging-Garden section attributed to Ctesias (B-R n. 192, pp. 45f.; cf. fn 5-6 on Diodorus II.7.1 and II.8.5 quoting Ctesias, and fn 15 MacGinnis on Ctesias and the fall of Nineveh); the reliefs described by Diodorus/Ctesias in the palaces of "Babylon" appreciated "als Reflex assyrischer Reliefkunst", i.e. as reflecting Assyrian palace sculpture (B-R n. 100, pp. 45f.).

--- pp. 46-47 (Berossus; the confusion of Nineveh and Babylon in Jewish, Arabic and Classical tradition) ---
* Berossus (via Josephus, fn 7: Jewish Antiquities X.220ff. and Contra Apionem I.128ff.) is treated with respect: his "account has generally been taken as authoritative" (VERBATIM, B-R n. 174, p. 46).
* The core claim of a "direct confusion" (VERBATIM phrase, B-R n. 115, p. 46) between Babylon and Nineveh, and between Sennacherib and Nebuchadnezzar, in later tradition: Jewish scholars (fn 9 Jewish Encyclopaedia s.v. Nebuchadnezzar), Islamic tradition (fn 10 Khoury on Babylon in the earliest Arabic prophet-stories), and the Greeks (fn 11-12 Nöldeke and Helm on "Assyria"/"Syria" and on Old Testament passages where Assyria means Babylon: Lam. 5:6, Micah 7:12, Zech. 10:10; fn 13 Ninos as founder at Aphrodisias; fn 14 Chronicles). Semiramis as builder of Babylon belongs to the same confusion (B-R n. 116, p. 46). B-R n. 91: "Vgl. Dalley 1994, 46ff." for the thesis that both later Jewish scholars and the Classical reporters confused the two cities; by 1996 Dalley wrote "It now seems certain that Greeks as well as Jews confused Nineveh and Babylon, Sennacherib and Nebuchadnezzar" (Dalley 1996, 529, quoted by B-R).
* Greek historiography of Assyria (fn 17 Bury, Drews, Kuhrt; fn 18 Castor's list of Assyrian kings from Ninos to "another Ninos" after Sardanapallos, via Eusebius' Armenian Chronicle).
* p. 47: B-R n. 137 ("Dalley 1994, 47"): the one piece of evidence for Nineveh being called Babylon that is not pure inference — introduced here and developed on pp. 48-49 (the Toledan/Azarqiel tables, see below).
* Herodotus I.185: the waterworks and geography Herodotus attributes to Babylon "oberhalb von Babylon" are argued to describe the Nineveh region (B-R n. 201, pp. 47f.).

--- p. 48 (Herodotus I.178; the Harran tradition; the astronomical tables) ---
* Herodotus I.178, the seat of kingship transferred to Babylon after the fall of Ninos, is read as a direct trace of an "indigenous tradition" in which Babylonia was part of Assyria — the same view held by part of the Chaldaean (Neo-Babylonian) inscriptions (B-R n. 202, 204, p. 48; B-R note that Dalley cites I.179 by mistake). This "Harran tradition" = the Harran stelae of Nabonidus/Adad-guppi (fn 19 Gadd, Anatolian Studies 8; fn 20 Langdon VAB IV no. 9 = the "Pognon stela", B-R n. 205: "Dalley 1994, 48 mit Anm. 20"), in which the regnal years of Assyrian and Babylonian kings are counted in one sequence, taken as showing that Babylonians could regard the Assyrian and Babylonian kingdoms as one; fn 21 Saggs on Sargon II's texts; fn 22 ABL 923 (SAA X 174); fn 23 the Tawilan tablet (Dalley 1984); fn 24 Athanassiadi on late paganism (Harran).
* pp. 48f.: the astronomical argument (fn 25 Becker & Becker, "'Altes' und 'Neues' Babylon", BaM 22, 1991; fn 26 MUL.APIN): the medieval Toledan tables of Azarqiel record longest-day values for "Old Babylon", "Other/Second Babylon" and "New Babylon"; the longest day of 14h 24min for "Old Babylon" gives a latitude of "ziemlich genau 35° Nord", i.e. "vicinity of Nineveh" (VERBATIM phrase; B-R n. 138: "Dalley 1994, 48f."). Dalley infers that an Assyrian city — Nineveh (B-R: the figure actually suits Nimrud) — was once called "Old Babylon".

--- p. 49 (Sennacherib's destruction of Babylon 689 BC; "Old" and "New" Babylon; Enuma Elish) ---
* After destroying Babylon in 689 (fn 27 Galter; fn 28 Borger, Esarhaddon Episode 7), Sennacherib is argued to have called Nineveh "old Babylon", while Esarhaddon's rebuilt Babylon was the "new Babylon" (B-R n. 123, 125, p. 49). Dalley cites the Assyrian recension of Enuma Elish found at Assur and Nineveh, in which Marduk is replaced by Assur; VERBATIM (B-R n. 125): "No texts have been found in which the name of the city built by the hero god is altered from Babylon" (fn 29 Jeremias; von Soden on Etemenanki; fn 30-32 Frame; W. G. Lambert, CRRAI Heidelberg 1992; fn 33 SAA III no. 39, the Marduk Ordeal).
* Flood/inundation motifs: Dalley points to the long literary tradition of cities destroyed by water, from Sumerian laments to the inscriptions of Sennacherib and Esarhaddon, as background to Herodotus' story of the river diversion at the capture of Babylon (B-R n. 109, p. 49; cf. n. 107-108 citing Dalley 1996 on "romantic, historical fiction ... under Persian rule").

--- p. 50 (Sennacherib as king of "Babylon"; Tintir; the Babylonian Chronicle) ---
* The "experiment to replace Marduk with Assur" (VERBATIM) such that "an Assyrian city had become Babylon" (VERBATIM; B-R n. 131, p. 50). Supporting "indications that Sennacherib saw himself as king of 'Babylon' though he did not take the title" (VERBATIM, B-R n. 142, p. 50): Assyrian monumental inscriptions dated by regnal years in the Babylonian manner instead of by eponyms (B-R n. 136, p. 50); Sennacherib's renaming of Nineveh's gates on the model of Babylon's (fn 34-35 George, Babylonian Topographical Texts, 3-4, 176-7 — the topographical series Tintir = Babylon, and George's note that Sennacherib "extended the text with a section listing new gates for Esharra in Assur"; fn 36 Borger, Esarhaddon 22, 25; fn 37 Grayson ARI II; fn 38 Rassam).
* pp. 50f., VERBATIM (B-R n. 258): "This negative statement bears considerable weight" and "'The Topography of Babylon' does not mention any gardens either" — i.e. Nebuchadnezzar's inscriptions and the Tintir list are silent about any garden; and the lost lines of the Babylonian Chronicle might have contained the version of Babylon's capture given by Herodotus (B-R n. 238: "Dalley 1994, 50f."). fn 39 (Perrot & Chipiez; König on Ctesias' Persika), fn 40 George, fn 41 Dandamaev on royal paradeisoi in Babylonia belong to this section on gardens at Babylon.

--- p. 51 (Curtius Rufus; 'hanging'; the Ashurbanipal relief; the prism text begins) ---
* VERBATIM (B-R n. 85, p. 51): "... since the description of the builder given by Q. Curtius Rufus, as 'a king of Assyria, reigning in Babylon', fits Sennacherib, a new location of the Hanging Gardens is required".
* VERBATIM (B-R n. 146, p. 51): the gardens were "hanging gardens in the sense of being suspended upon vaults".
* Fig. 1 (BM 124939, the garden panel from Ashurbanipal's North Palace): Dalley reads it as showing Sennacherib's garden and its aqueduct, and as matching Diodorus' comparison of the garden with a theatre (B-R n. 86: "Dalley 1994, 51 mit Fig. 1 und 54"; n. 143: "51f. mit Fig. 1").
* pp. 51-53: the passage of Sennacherib's prism inscription describing the new water-raising device is quoted and re-interpreted (B-R n. 160: "Dalley 1994, 51-53"; fn 42 Luckenbill, Annals of Sennacherib 110-111 and Heidel, Sumer 9, the octagonal prism; fn 43 on the disjointed sequence of the inscription: "VI.89 ff. describes new castings of the 'big trees' and palms, of lion colossi, bull colossi and sphinxes ... then he goes on to tell of setting up the 'trees' and to describe the gardens"; fn 44: "This crucial word was damaged on the source used by Luckenbill, and was therefore omitted in his translations; the duplicate source published by Heidel gave a clear text. Note that the word for copper is often used interchangeably with the word for bronze"). Her translation of the passage (as given in Dalley & Oleson 2003, 7-8, and the 2013 book, Appendix) runs: "In order to draw water up all day long I had ropes, bronze wires and bronze chains made, and instead of shadufs I set up the great cylinders [gišmahhu] and alamittu palms over cisterns."

--- pp. 52-53 (alamittu and gišmahhu = the screw) ---
* p. 52: the two terms alamittu and gišmahhu are understood as the 'screw/spiral' and the 'cylinder' of an Archimedean water screw cast in bronze (B-R n. 161: "Dalley 1994, 52"; fn 45 al-Rawi & Roaf: gišmahhu = cylinder in Old Babylonian mathematical problems, "I am grateful to Eleanor Robson for pointing this out"; fn 46 CAD A/1 333: alamittu identified from Löw's Flora der Juden as a palm whose trunk shows a spiral pattern — "The Botanical Gardens in Oxford contain mature examples of the tree").
* p. 53: the spiral as a familiar Mesopotamian form (fn 47 the spiral mud-brick columns at Tell al-Rimah; fn 48 Ur; fn 49 Basmusian/Shemshara; fn 50 Weiss; fn 51 Larsa; fn 52 Carter on Rimah's sculptured palm-trunk columns); the Classical descriptions, above all Strabo's and Philo's, are said to reveal the screw (B-R n. 162: "Dalley 1994, 53"). fn 53: Sennacherib "proudly described how cleverly he cast gigantic figures in copper using new technology" (Dalley 1988 on bronzeworking) and his qanat for Arbela "may also be described with a new, technical use of vocabulary".

--- p. 54 (matching the Classical descriptions with Nineveh) ---
* Diodorus' comparison of the garden to a theatre auditorium fits the relief (Fig. 1), and the dimensions of 4 x 4 plethra given by Diodorus and Strabo fit Sennacherib's park on Kuyunjik (B-R n. 86, 88, p. 54). fn 54-58: Esarhaddon's inscriptions; Nebuchadnezzar's inscriptions (Langdon) as the contrast; Russell, Sennacherib's Palace without Rival, fig. 44 (the South-West Palace plan); Durand on the organisation of the Mari palace (the garden beside the palace); Stevenson's 1992 Iraq proposal for irrigating the gardens at Babylon (rejected).

--- pp. 55-56 (Berossus, Josephus, Abydenus, Alexander Polyhistor; the sources still available) ---
* p. 55: the Berossus fragments preserved by Josephus and Eusebius are argued to be corrupt (B-R n. 175); fn 59-62: Burstein's side-by-side edition of the Berossus fragments, Schnabel, Syncellus, and the corrupted queen's name "Amakales" (fn 62: "Note that the name is mistranscribed Amakales by Burstein").
* pp. 55f.: Abydenus' report (via Eusebius) of "Nebuchadnezzar's" waterworks can go back to Berossus but with a corruption of the name: Sennacherib must originally have been meant (B-R n. 83). Dalley assumes that Sennacherib's inscriptions remained available as sources into the Seleucid period (B-R n. 121: "Dalley 1994, 55f.").
* p. 56: Josephus himself or his presumed source Alexander Polyhistor wrongly attributed a tradition about the Hanging Gardens to Berossus, who therefore did not himself report the gardens or name Nebuchadnezzar as builder (B-R n. 82). Nineveh was not completely destroyed in 612 (fn 69 Dalley, "Nineveh after 612 B.C."; fn 70 Na'aman; fn 71 MacGinnis), the Assyrian reliefs remained visible, and native "guides" showed them to visitors into Hellenistic times (B-R n. 197, p. 56). The surviving accounts also transferred to Nineveh the historical circumstances of the conquests of Babylon by Sennacherib and Ashurbanipal (B-R n. 233, p. 56; fn 72 Steiner & Nims, the Aramaic tale of the two brothers Assurbanipal and Shamash-shum-ukin). fn 63-68 belong here: the rivers "bulbulliya" and "tebilti" in Sennacherib's texts are epithets ("flood-prone", "flooder"), not names (fn 63); the mechanical gates of "Babylon" attributed to Semiramis by Diodorus II.8.7 may be "misunderstanding and corruption" of Sennacherib's gate passage, and "Sipparians" a corruption of Shapparishu in the Bavian inscription (fn 64); Eusebius' Armenian Chronicle, Cory's Ancient Fragments, Karst, Jacoby (fn 65); Helm; Moses Khorenats'i (fn 66-67); Ahiqar (fn 68).
* pp. 56f., VERBATIM (B-R n. 90): the divergences between the Classical descriptions show "that the gardens were still extant in the Parthian period"; Curtius' statement that the structure had survived the ravages of time is taken as evidence that the garden — at Nineveh — still existed in his day (B-R n. 90, 95).

--- pp. 57-58 (reconstruction and conclusion) ---
* pp. 57f. with Fig. 3: "Reconstruction drawing of Sennacherib's palace garden at Nineveh incorporating new evidence" (B-R n. 147), drawn by Terry Ball of English Heritage (fn 75). fn 73: the prism names a "garden gate" ("'Igi-sig-sig the god who makes orchards fruitful' (is the name of) the mušlalu of the garden") in a sequence of palace gates, quoting Campbell Thompson, Iraq 7 (1940) 90; fn 74: BM Or. Dr. V.1 (British Museum Original Drawing) reproduced in SAA III (1989) fig. 6.
* p. 58, VERBATIM (B-R n. 240, their italics removed): "Sennacherib built the gardens, and a contemporary sculpture as well as an inscription confirm the truth of later accounts". B-R n. 239 also cite the title itself as the article's summary claim: the wrongly criticised Classical texts and the cuneiform sources yield a harmonious picture once the gardens are placed at Nineveh.

--- Summary of the thesis as read by the critics ---
* Bagg 2014 (BiOr 71, col. 490), on the book but equally on the article: "Dalley's chain of thought can be summarized as follows: 1) the Classical sources mention a Hanging Garden in Babylon, which was watered by screws and built by Nebuchadnezzar; 2) such a World Wonder has neither been found in Babylon nor is it mentioned in Nebuchadnezzar's inscriptions; 3) Sennacherib built a royal park in Nineveh watered by screws which he had invented; 4) the Classical sources confuse Babylon with Nineveh. Ergo: The Hanging Garden was Sennacherib's palace garden in Nineveh."
* Bagg 2000 (Assyrische Wasserbauten, 201, quoted by B-R n. 165): "Die einzige konkrete Verbindung zwischen den klassischen und assyrischen Quellen, die S. Dalley feststellen kann — und eigentlich ihr einziger Beweis —, bezieht sich auf die in beiden Quellen erwähnten Bewässerungsmaschinen."
* RINAP 3/1 (Grayson & Novotny 2012), introduction n. 44: "For the opinion that Sennacherib's 'replica of Mount Amanus' was the origin of the 'Hanging Gardens of Babylon' story, see Dalley, Iraq 56 (1994) pp. 45-58; and Dalley, HSAO 6 pp. 19-24 ... For some critical reviews of Dalley's proposal, see Reade, Iraq 62 (2000) pp. 195-217; and Bichler and Rollinger, Studies Schretter pp. 153-218."
