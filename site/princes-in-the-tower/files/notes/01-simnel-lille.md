# 01 — The 1487 rising ("Lambert Simnel") and the Lille receipt

Research notes. Scope: what the 1487 "Dublin king" was called, whom he claimed to be, and what the Lille receipt used by Philippa Langley's Missing Princes Project (book: *The Princes in the Tower: Solving History's Greatest Cold Case*, History Press, Nov 2023; revised edn *How History's Greatest Cold Case Was Solved*, 29 May 2025; Channel 4, *The Princes in the Tower: The New Evidence*, 18 Nov 2023) can and cannot bear.

Evidence tags: **[Verified]** = I read the passage in the cited edition/URL and quote it from there. **[Unverified]** = taken from a secondary source or a summary; original not seen. **[Partisan note]** flags Ricardian (R) vs traditionalist/academic (T) alignment where it bears on reliability. Primary sources are quoted in the original language where wording matters, with my own translation unless stated.

## 1. The Lille document

### 1.1 Reference, date, language
- Archive reference as published by Langley: "Lille Receipt – Edward V: Proof of Life, 16 December 1487. Archives Départementales du Nord, Lille: ADN B 3521/124564" (caption on https://www.revealingrichardiii.com/solved.html). **[Verified as Langley's citation]** The form means série B (Chambre des comptes de Lille), liasse B 3521, pièce no. 124564. B 3521 belongs to the artillery/"acquits" bundles of Maximilian's administration described in *Inventaire sommaire des Archives départementales antérieures à 1790, Nord, Série B*, tome VIII (ed. Jules Finot, Lille 1895), pp. 265–266 — see 1.4.
- Date of the document: 16 December 1487 (Langley; Phillips; History Press). The pikes are said in the document to have been bought/collected "in June" 1487 (Langley: "The weapons had been collected by Maximilian in June of that year"; Phillips: "The document says that the pikes were bought in June 1487"). **[Verified as reported; original not seen]**
- Language: French (Burgundian chancery French) — inferred from the printed abstract tradition (Finot) and the names of the signatories; Langley prints only an English translation (2023, Appendix 2, pp. 309–310, per Phillips n. 72). **No transcription of the French text is published online that I could find.** So the often-repeated formula "filz du roy Edouard" is *not* verifiable from any published transcription; every quotation in circulation is Langley's English.

### 1.2 What it literally says (as published or quoted)
The English wording in circulation, all deriving from Langley's translation:
- Langley's site: the pikes were "'to serve her nephew – son of King Edward, late her brother (may God save his soul), [who was] expelled from his dominion.'" **[Verified quotation of Langley]**
- History Hit (Amy Irvine, 4 Dec 2023, https://www.historyhit.com/the-princes-in-the-tower-solving-historys-greatest-cold-case/), quoting the book: the pikes were "to lead across the sea with a specialist a German mercenary, who the Madam the Dowager sent at that time, together with several captains of war from England, to serve her nephew, son of King Edward, late her brother, who was expelled from his dominion". **[Verified quotation of Irvine]**
- Matt Lewis, "The Lambert Simnel Affair" (Substack, 9 Jan 2025): "…to take and lead across the sea, whom Madam the Dowager sent at that time, together with several captains of war from England, to serve her nephew – son of King Edward, late her brother". **[Verified quotation of Lewis]**
- Gareth Streeter (Royal History Geeks, 21 Dec 2023): 400 pikes to be sent by "lord Martin de Zwarte, a knight from Germany" across the sea by "Madam the Dowager" for "her nephew – son of King Edward, late her brother". **[Verified quotation of Streeter]**

So the document (in translation) contains: (i) 400 pikes; (ii) Martin Schwartz ("Martin de Zwarte, knight from Germany"), sent by "Madame la Douairière" (Margaret of York) "together with several captains of war from England"; (iii) purpose "to serve her nephew, son of King Edward, late her brother (God save his soul), [who was] expelled from his dominion/lordship". It does **not** name the nephew "Edward", does **not** give a regnal number, and does **not** mention Ireland (nothing in any published quotation refers to Ireland or Dublin).

### 1.3 Who paid, for what, to whom — the structure of the document
From Chris Phillips's analysis of Langley's published translation ("Who did 'Lambert Simnel' claim to be?", 27 Apr 2024, pp. 12–14, https://www.medievalgenealogy.org.uk/families/LambertSimnel.pdf) **[Verified read]**:
- Purchase of 400 pikes "bought from Jean de Finet (or de Smet) of Mechelin by the order of the king [Maximilian] to be distributed to Martin Schwartz's troops, sent by Margaret in the service of her nephew".
- Two parts. Part 1 is the merchant's receipt for payment, "signed at his request (presumably because he was unable to sign for himself) by Florens Hauweel, the secretary of Lienart de La Court (lieutenant of the master of the artillery of the King of the Romans) and of Andrieu Schaffer (controller of the same). This is the part that specifies the identity of the pretender and the intended use of the pikes." Part 2 "certifies only the sale and receipt of the pikes and their delivery to the Lord of Walhain [Jean de Berghes, seigneur de Walhain, Maximilian's chamberlain], to be distributed in the manner specified in the first part … This is the part signed by de La Court and Schaffer."
- Langley's characterisation (2023, p. 177, per Phillips): the document was "drawn up, inspected and signed by three leading officials at the Burgundian court", Hauweel being "one of King Maximilian's own secretaries". Phillips: in Antonius Sanderus's list of the ducal household under Philip the Fair, Hauweel "occurs only as the 14th name in a section containing 21 secretaries", i.e. "a relatively junior member of the ducal secretariat" (Phillips p. 13, citing Sanderus, *Chorographia Sacra Brabantiae*, 1659–60, "Status Aulicus", p. 14). **[Unverified: Sanderus not checked]**
- Langley's site adds the gloss "expelled from his dominion (to the Channel Islands)" — the Channel Islands are her theory (Edward V hidden in Guernsey/Coldridge), not in the document.

### 1.4 Prior publication — not a 2020 "discovery"
- Phillips (p. 12): "It is described as a discovery made by Albert Jan de Rooij in 2020, but in fact an abstract of it - including the description of the pretender given verbatim - was published as long ago as 1895, and it has also been referred to in the modern literature on the Battle of Stoke", citing "Finot (1895), pp. 265, 266; Bletzer (2013), p. 113 and note xiii" — i.e. Jules Finot, *Inventaire sommaire … Nord, Série B*, tome VIII (1895), under B 3521; and Michael Bletzer, "'Excellent knights' and 'worthy soldiers': Habsburg captains at Bosworth and Stoke Field", in *Report on Military History for the Bosworth Battlefield Association*, ed. A. Curry and J. Dickinson (2013), pp. 111–118. Phillips: "Bletzer's interest was in military history, and he quoted the description of the pikes, but not the description of the pretender." **[Verified as Phillips's statement; Finot tome VIII and Bletzer not accessed — tome VIII is not among the Google/archive.org volumes I could find (tomes I, II, IV, V, VI are online); ResearchGate blocked the Bletzer PDF]**
- The surname of the merchant: "de Finet by Finot and by Bletzer, but … de Smet by Nijman-Bliekendaal and Langley" (Phillips n. 74).
- Independent corroboration that "de Smet" is the right reading and that he was a routine supplier: *Inventaire sommaire … Nord, Série B*, tome IV (ed. C. Dehaisnes, 1881), https://archive.org/details/inventairesomma02finogoog **[Verified]**:
  - B 2129 (1 Jan–31 Dec 1483, acquits of the recette générale), p. 265: quittance "de Jean De Smet, demeurant à Malines, qui a fourni « sept vingts dix picques de bois de fresne chascune de xxiiii piez ou environ de long, ferrées de petiz fers tous d'achier à la faison d'Allemaigne »" (150 ash-wood pikes c. 24 ft long, ironed with small all-steel heads "in the German fashion").
  - B 2143 (1 Jan 1491–10 Jan 1492): quittances "d'Engelbert, comte de Nassau, au sujet des hommes d'armes qui ont servi à Dam et à Ardenbourg, de Jean de Smet, fustaillier de Malines, pour piques fournies aux mêmes hommes d'armes".
  So a Mechelen wood-turner named Jean de Smet was selling German-style pikes to Maximilian's war treasury before and after 1487. The 1487 receipt is one of a class of routine artillery acquits.

### 1.5 The signatories and the paper trail (as reported)
Lienart (Léonard) de La Court, lieutenant of the master of the artillery; Andrieu Schaffer, controller of the artillery; Florens Hauweel, their secretary, signing for the illiterate merchant; delivery to Jean de Berghes, lord of Walhain (Maximilian's chamberlain, cf. tome IV: "Jean de Berghes, seigneur de Walhaing, chambellan"). **[Names per Phillips/Langley; Walhain's office verified in tome IV]**

### 1.6 Doubts about the reading and its evidential weight
1. **Chronology inside the document.** Phillips p. 13: "The document says that the pikes were bought in June 1487 for distribution to Martin Schwartz's troops, and were delivered to be so distributed. But on the English evidence, those troops had left Flanders by late April, more than a month earlier [Henry VII to Ormond, Kenilworth 13 May 1487: 'our rebels landed the fifth day of this month in our land of Ireland' — Pollard I no. 30, **Verified**]. Even the later date of 15 May used by Langley would make the purchase of the pikes more than two weeks too late for them to be distributed to Schwartz's troops. The suggestion of Langley's colleague Zoë Maula is that the pikes may have been diverted for use in Brittany instead [Langley 2023 pp. 186–7]. So the document's account of what had happened to the pikes must be inaccurate … If the officials signed off an obviously false statement about the former, what reliance can be placed on what the document says about the latter?"
2. **Who wrote the identification.** Only Part 1 (drafted by the secretary Hauweel) describes the nephew; the two artillery officers signed Part 2, which does not repeat it (Phillips p. 13).
3. **Uniqueness.** "This makes it unique among contemporary references to the pretender, in the claim that he was a son of Edward IV" (Phillips p. 13). Every other Burgundian-territory record — Molinet, the Voorne estate accounts, the Mechelen city accounts, Adrien de But — calls him the son of the Duke of Clarence (section 2.4).
4. **"Son of King Edward" would be a natural slip** for a clerk who knew Margaret was Edward IV's sister and that the claimant was "her nephew" and "rightful heir" (Phillips p. 14; Streeter: "a simple misunderstanding by an administrator"; Amin 2025: pretenders were routinely upgraded — French records called Henry Tudor "the son of Henry VI").
5. **No name, no regnal number, no Ireland** in the published wording; "expelled from his dominion" fits any dispossessed Yorkist claimant (Warwick was also, on the Yorkist view, the disinherited heir).
6. **Dutch Research Group's own caution**: Zoë Maula, Jean Roefstra and William Wiss, *Ricardian Bulletin*, June 2024, p. 4: "the finds made by the DRG are in our own opinion open to various interpretations and do not constitute irrefutable proof without other genuine and undoubted sources to back up what these documents are telling us – or what we (wish to) believe they are telling us." **[Unverified original; quoted by Dan Moorhouse, thewarsoftheroses.co.uk, 21 Jul 2024]**
7. What is *not* in doubt: authenticity and date. Nobody (Hicks, Phillips, Amin, Streeter, Pilling) disputes that the receipt is a genuine 1487 Burgundian accounting document; the dispute is about what the clerk's phrase proves.

## 2. What the sources call the 1487 claimant, and whom they say he claimed to be

### 2.1 English official/record sources

**(a) Act of Attainder of John de la Pole, earl of Lincoln, and adherents, Parliament of Nov 1487.** *Rotuli Parliamentorum* VI, p. 397 (1767 printing, https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_rotuli-parliamentorum-u_great-britain-parliamen_1767_6; also Pollard, *Reign of Henry VII from Contemporary Sources* I (1913), no. 35; PROME on BHO is paywalled). **[Verified from the 1767 printing's OCR; day of month garbled in OCR, 24 May per Pollard/Phillips]**
> "…the [xxiiij] day of May last passed, at the Cite of Develyn, contrarie to his homage and faith, trouth and allegiaunce, trayterously renounced, revoked and disclaymed his owne said most naturall Sovereygne Leige Lord the Kyng, and caused oone Lambert Symnell, a child of x yere of age, sonne to Thomas Symnell, late of Oxforde Joynoure, to be proclamed, erecte and reputed as Kyng of this Realme, and to hym did feith and homage, to the grete dishonour and despite of all this Realme; and frome thens … arived with a greate Navie in Furnes in Lancashire, the iiij day of June last past, accompanyed with a greate multytude of Straungers … to the nomber of viij M persones … to they come to Stoke in the Countie of Notingham; where, the xvj day of June last … levied warre ayenst the persone of his Sovereygne and naturall Leige Lorde".
Note: the Act names the boy and his father, gives his age as ten, and says he was "proclamed … as Kyng" — it does **not** say whom he claimed to be (Phillips n. 23).

**(b) York House Books (York Civic Records II, ed. A. Raine, YASRS 103, 1941, pp. 13–14, 20–23; https://archive.org/details/YASRS103).** **[Verified]**
- Henry VII to York, Coventry 4 May 1487: "we have certain knowleige in sundry wise that our rebelles bene departid out of Flaundres, and goon westwardes" (p. 13) — no identity stated.
- Heading of the rebels' letter (fo. 97): "Copie of a letter direct to the Maire, etc. from the lords of Lincoln, Lovell and othre, late landed in Fourneys in the name of ther King calling hymself King Edward the V." — **Raine's "V" is a misprint**: Pollard I no. 33 (1913) heads it "A letter of the Earl of Lincoln, styling Simnel Edward VI" and prints "in the name of that king calling himself king Edward VI"; Attreed, *York House Books* II (1991), p. 570 reads "Edward the vjt" (Phillips n. 30: Raine "made the crucial mistake of mistranscribing Edward VI as Edward V"). **[Pollard verified; Attreed via Phillips]**
- The letter itself carries no name or number: "By the King. To our trusty and wellbiloved the Maiour, his brethren and comunaltye of our Citie of York. Trusty and wellbiloved we grete you wele, and for somoch as we beene comen within this oure realme, not oonlly, by goddes grace, to atteyne oure right of the same, but also for the relief and well of our said realme … Yevene undre our signet at Masham the viij day of Juyn." The city replied that "he which the said lords callid the King" would not be admitted.
- 12 June: the Lords Scrope "cam on horsbak to Bowthom Barre, and ther cried King Edward, and made a salt at the yates" (p. 22).
- Stoke: "the child which they callid ther King was takyn and broght unto the Kinges grace" (p. 23). Editor's note p. 23 n. 1: the House Books "were written up some time after the events".
Implication: the York clerk understood the pretender to be "Edward the sixth", i.e. a different Edward from Edward V — but this is the copyist's heading, not the rebels' text (Phillips p. 5).

**(c) Convocation of Canterbury, St Paul's, February 1487** (Morton's register; Wilkins, *Concilia* iii. 618; Pollard III no. 84, pp. 246–249; Harper-Bill, *Register of John Morton* I (1987), pp. 25–26). **[Verified in Pollard III, https://archive.org/details/reignofhenryviif03polluoft]**
> "productus fuit quidam dominus Willielmus Symonds, presbyter, xxviii. annorum aetatis, ut asseruit, qui ibidem in praesentia dictorum dominorum ac praelatorum et cleri, necnon majoris, aldermannorum, et vicecomitum civitatis London, publice fatebatur et confessus est, quod ipse filium cujusdam [ ] orginmaker universitatis Oxon. ad partes Hiberniae abduxit et transvexit. Qui quidem filius pro comite Warwici ibidem reputabatur, et quod ipse postea erat cum domino Lovell in Furnefotts [MS: ffurnesfelles]".
(Translation: a certain Sir William Symonds, priest, aged 28 as he asserted, was produced, who … publicly acknowledged and confessed that he had carried off and conveyed to Ireland the son of a certain [blank] organ-maker of the University of Oxford; which son was there reputed to be the Earl of Warwick; and that he himself was afterwards with Lord Lovell in Furness Fells.) Pollard III p. 255 n.: Wilkins "omits some interesting passages telling how the Earl of Derby produced the Earl of Warwick before Convocation, the mayor and sheriffs of London and others being present, and how the archbishop took him back to Lambeth." Phillips dates Symonds's confession 17 Feb and Warwick's appearance 19/20 Feb (Harper-Bill 1987 pp. 25–26).

**(d) Henry VII to Innocent VIII, Kenilworth, 5 July 1487** (Gairdner, *Letters and Papers illustrative of the reigns of Richard III and Henry VII*, Rolls Series, I (1861), pp. 95–96; https://archive.org/details/letterspapersil01gair). **[Verified, Latin]**
> "Cum nonnulli ex praelatis Hiberniae, archiepiscopus scilicet Dublinensis, archiepiscopus Armachanensis et episcopi Medensis et Darensis, tam in nostri dominii quam censurarum ecclesiasticarum contemptum, rebellibus hostibusque nostris opem et juvamen impenderint, ac spurium quemdam puerum, quem victoria potiti in manibus nostris habemus, ad rebellium ipsorum et hostium nostrorum confingentium puerum ipsum ducis quondam Clarentiae filium esse, in regem Angliae coronarunt, ad grave nostrum et totius regni nostri praejudicium, vestram Sanctitatem humillime imploramus ut praefatos praelatos in censuras incursos ecclesiasticas postulare velit, atque in eos de jure procedere."
(Since certain prelates of Ireland, namely the archbishop of Dublin, the archbishop of Armagh and the bishops of Meath and [Kildare/Derry], in contempt of our lordship and of ecclesiastical censures, gave help and aid to our rebels and enemies, and crowned as king of England a certain bastard boy — whom, having won the victory, we hold in our hands — our rebels and enemies pretending that this boy was the son of the late Duke of Clarence …, we humbly beg your Holiness to declare the said prelates fallen under censure and proceed against them.) Phillips n. 43: it would be "demonstrably false, but also completely unnecessary" for Henry to lie to the Pope about the claim, since the Pope actually ordered an investigation.

**(e) Innocent VIII's mandate, 5 Jan 1488** (Calendar of Papal Registers XIV, p. 307, https://www.british-history.ac.uk/cal-papal-registers/brit-ie/vol14/pp305-309). **[Verified, calendar English]** The Pope "has recently learned that Octavian and Walter, archbishops of Armagh and Dublin, John bishop of Meath, and the present bishop of Kildare … adhered to and aided and abetted the enemies and rebels of the said king, and even *de facto* set up and crowned as king, falsely alleging him to be a son of the late duke of Clarence, a boy of illegitimate birth".

**(f) Henry VII to Sir Gilbert Talbot, 20 July 1493** (Halliwell, *Letters of the Kings of England* i. 172–3; Pollard I no. 67, pp. 93–94). **[Verified in Pollard]** Margaret of Burgundy "as she showed lately in sending hither of a feigned boy, surmising him to have been the son of the Duke of Clarence, and causeth him to be accompanied with the Earl of Lincoln, the Lord Lovel, and with great multitude of Irishmen and Almains"; Warbeck "at first into Ireland called himself the bastard son of King Richard; after that the son of the said Duke of Clarence; and now the second son of our father, King Edward the IVth".

**(g) Warbeck's confession, 1497** (Pollard I pp. 184–5): at Cork "they of the Towne … threped vpon me that I shuld be the Duke of Clarence sone, that was before tyme at Develyn." **[Verified in Pollard]** (Tudor-controlled text; but it shows the official memory of the Dublin king as "Clarence's son".)

**(h) Milanese report, Sept 1497** (Pollard I pp. 164–5, Milanese calendar): the youth taken in 1487 "By the royal clemency … is living in the Tower of London, under the very slightest restraint. They say that his Majesty, out of respect for the sacred unction, wants to make a priest of him." (Pollard's note: "Apparently an allusion to Simnel's unction at his coronation at Dublin"; the report seems to conflate Simnel with Warwick.) **[Verified in Pollard]**

**(i) De Puebla to Ferdinand and Isabella, Jan 1500** (Spanish Calendar i. 249; Pollard I no. 145): "now that Perkin and the son of the Duke of Clarence have been executed, there does not remain 'a drop of doubtful Royal blood'". **[Verified in Pollard]**

### 2.2 English narrative sources

**(a) The Heralds' Memoir** (BL Cotton Julius B XII; printed Leland/Hearne, *Collectanea* IV (1770), pp. 209, 214, https://archive.org/details/gri_33125011195340; ed. Emma Cavell, *The Heralds' Memoir 1486–1490* (2009), pp. 108–9, 117). **[Verified in Hearne's print]**
- Feb 1487: "at that Counseill was th Erle of Lyncolln, whiche incontynently after the saide Council departede the Land, and went into Flaunders to the Lorde Lovell, and accompanyed hymself with the Kings Rebells and Enemyes, noysing in that Countrey, that th Erle of Warwik shulde be in Irelande, whiche himselfe knew, and daily spake with him at Shene afor his Departing" (p. 209).
- Stoke: "And ther was taken the Lad that his Rebells callede King Edwarde, whos Name was indede Lambert, by a Vaylent and a gentil Esquier of the King's Howse, called Robert Bellingham" (p. 214).
- **Textual point**: Hearne's print reads "Lambert", but Michael Bennett's examination of the manuscript found the herald wrote "whose name was indeed **John**" (Smith 1996, p. 514; Cavell 2001 p. 288 / 2009 p. 117 per Phillips n. 10, 28). **[Bennett/Cavell readings unverified by me]** Smith: "A 'King Edward' impersonated by a John seems to be a spontaneous invention recorded shortly afterwards." The herald thus gives *no regnal number* ("King Edwarde") and a name that is not Lambert.

**(b) Bernard André, *Vita Henrici Septimi*** (written c. 1500–02; ed. Gairdner, *Memorials of King Henry the Seventh*, Rolls Series 10, 1858, pp. 49–52, https://archive.org/details/historiaregishen00gair; online ed. D. F. Sutton 2010). **[Verified, Latin]** Chapter "De Conjuratione Hibernica", A.D. 1487:
> "Crudescente iterum filiorum Eduardi regis diro funere, ecce aliud novum facinus seditiosi homines excogitarunt; quippe ut fictionem suam mendacio velarent quemdam vulgo natum, puerum, sive pistoris, sive sutoris, filium Eduardi Quarti scelerata mente jactaverunt. … Sic, igitur, excogitata inter ipsos fallacia, Eduardi secundum filium in Hibernia regem coronatum fama retulit. Cumque ad regem talis rumor delatus esset; ipse ut est sagacissimus ab illis percontatur omnia; quomodo videlicet et a quibus illuc delatus sit, ubi educatus, ubi tamdiu moratus, quos haberet amicos … Nuncii missi pro rerum varietate varii, postremo * * * * qui se illum si talis esset facile cogniturum asserebat, transmissus est. Ille autem arte mala jam instructus ab his qui Eduardi tempora notaverant ad omnia caduciferi interrogata promptissime respondebat. Tandem, ne longum faciam, falsa suggerentium instructione a plurimis eisdemque prudentibus viris Eduardi filius credebatur, adeoque firmiter tenebatur ut plerique mortem oppetere pro illo minime dubitarent. … ut comes Linconiensis * * * idem credere non ambigeret. Et quoniam ex ipsius Eduardi stirpe ductum habebat, domina Margareta olim Karoli novissimi ducis Burgondionum uxor Eduardique germana illum per litteras ad se vocavit; qui furtim hinc fugiens ad illam, paucis tantae proditionis consciis, celeriter est profectus." (pp. 49–50; asterisks = blanks in the MS)
Translation: "The dire death of King Edward's sons breaking out afresh, behold, seditious men devised another new crime; for to cloak their fiction with a lie they wickedly put about that a certain low-born boy, the son of a baker or of a cobbler, was the son of Edward IV. … So, the deceit having been contrived among them, rumour reported that Edward's *second son* had been crowned king in Ireland. When such a rumour reached the king, he … enquires everything: how and by whom he had been brought there, where brought up, where he had stayed so long, what friends he had … Various messengers were sent, and finally [a herald] was sent over who said he would easily recognise him if he were such. But the boy, already schooled in evil art by those who had noted the times of Edward, answered all the herald's questions most readily. In the end … through the false instruction of his prompters he was believed by very many, and prudent men too, to be Edward's son, and was held so firmly that most did not hesitate to die for him … so that the Earl of Lincoln did not hesitate to believe the same. And because he traced his descent from Edward's stock, Lady Margaret, formerly wife of Charles, last duke of Burgundy, and Edward's sister, summoned him to her by letter; and he, fleeing secretly from here to her, with few privy to so great a treason, quickly departed."
After Stoke (p. 52): "Ibi nebulonum ille regulus in Hibernia ut ante dixi coronatus misellus bello capitur; qui interrogatus qua audacia tantum facinus vapulo facere ausus esset, a quibusdam suae sortis flagitiosis hominibus se fuisse coactum non negavit. Deinde super generis ac parentum conditione interrogatus, viles omnino personas, vilibusque officiis, nec in hac historia inseri dignis, omnes fuisse confessus est." ("There that wretched kinglet of rogues, crowned in Ireland as I said, is captured in battle; asked with what boldness he, a whipping-boy, had dared such a crime, he did not deny that he had been forced by certain villainous men of his own sort. Then asked about his family and parents, he confessed they were all wholly base persons, of base occupations, not worthy of insertion in this history.") Henry's set speech (p. 51) calls Margaret a "levis ac procacis mulierculae" who "sanguinem suum a Ricardo fratre suo extinctum non ignorat" ("is not unaware that her blood was extinguished by her brother Richard") — André's Henry thus treats Edward IV's sons as dead.
Assessment: André is the only early narrative to say the boy was passed off as a son of Edward IV — but he says the *second* son (i.e. Richard of York), never Edward V; he never names Lambert; and he misplaces Henry's enquiry after the coronation (Phillips pp. 6–7; Smith 1996 pp. 498–9). Langley (2023, p. 179) cites André's "son of Edward IV" but omits "secundum filium" (Phillips n. 39). André was Henry VII's court poet/tutor — a Tudor source, writing 13+ years later, with blanks in the MS where names should be.

**(c) Polydore Vergil, *Anglica Historia*, Book XXVI** (MS 1512–13, ed. D. Hay, Camden 3rd ser. 74, 1950, pp. 12–25; printed 1534/1546/1555; 1555 Latin and English at https://philological.cal.bham.ac.uk/polverg/26lat.html and 26eng.html). **[Verified, 1555 text]**
> "Ricardus Simondus presbyter, homo sordido loco natus … discipulum sibi elegit Lambertum Symenellum puerum non malo usquequaque ingenio … Cuius consilio materiam dedit, quod in vulgus fama valeret filios Edouardi regis aliquo terrarum secreto migrasse atque ita superstites esse, ac Edouardum Varvici comitem ducis Clarentiae filium vel necatum vel brevi necandum. … Haud ita multo post fama passim dissipavit Edouardum comitem Varvici in carcere interiisse. Quod ubi Simondus rescivit … mutat adulescentulo nomen vocatque eum Edouardum, quod illud nomen filio ducis Clarentiae fuisset, qui et id aetatis puer erat, ut alter alteri annis non praestaret, ac protinus cum eo in Hyberniam traiicit, ibique secreto convenit nonnullos Hybernorum principes … narrat se servasse filium ducis Clarentiae a morte ac duxisse in eam terram quam audierat semper unice amasse nomen et genus Edouardi regis."
(The priest Richard Simons … chose as his pupil the boy Lambert Simnel …; material for his plan was the popular rumour that King Edward's sons had secretly gone abroad and survived, and that Edward, Earl of Warwick, Clarence's son, was killed or soon to be killed … Not much later rumour spread that Warwick had died in prison. When Simons learnt this … he changes the youth's name and calls him Edward, because that was the name of the Duke of Clarence's son, who was a boy of the same age, so that neither exceeded the other in years, and at once crosses with him to Ireland … tells [the Irish lords] that he had saved the Duke of Clarence's son from death.)
- Warwick shown: "Rex, dimisso concilio, Londinum venit, ac insequenti die dominico educi per mediam urbem ex turri iussit Edouardum filium ducis Clarentiae ad templum divi Pauli. Hic adolescens, ut edoctus erat, sese omnibus ostendens interfuit in supplicatione in caeterisque sacris, ac simul collocutus est cum multis principibus et praesertim cum iis qui coniurationis participes habebantur" (the king … on the following Sunday ordered Edward, son of the Duke of Clarence, to be led out of the Tower through the middle of the city to St Paul's; this adolescent, as instructed, showing himself to all … conversed with many lords, especially those thought party to the conspiracy).
- Dublin: "Dublini Lambertum puerum perinde quasi regia stirpe ortum more maiorum regem creandum curant".
- Stoke and after: "Lambertus vero puer rex commentitus cum Ricardo suo praeceptore capitur, sed vitae utriusque parcitur, quod ille innocens nihil per aetatem peccasset … Vivit adhuc Lambertus ex rege accipitrum domitor factus postquam aliquantisper in coquina regia veru verterat aliaque viliora munera obiverat." (The boy Lambert, the counterfeit king, is captured with his tutor Richard, but both lives are spared, because the former, innocent, had by reason of his age done no wrong … Lambert is still alive, made the king's falconer after he had for a while turned the spit in the royal kitchen and performed other base tasks.)
- Warham's embassy 1493 (1555 addition): Margaret "quod monstra pareret, quae, aetate iam decursa intra paucos annos duos Lambertum … et Petrum non octavo nonove, ut fit naturaliter, sed centesimo octogesimo mense enixa esset. Tot enim menses conficiunt annos circiter quindecim, quot quidem annos ambo pueri ante habuerant, quam illa eos quasi e partu in lucem edidisset" (that she bore monsters, having in her old age within a few years given birth to Lambert and Peter not in the eighth or ninth month but in the 180th; for so many months make about fifteen years, the age both boys had before she brought them forth).
- Vergil (1555) also says Warwick was "decem et quinque annos natum" (fifteen) in 1485 — wrong (he was ten).
- MS vs print: in the 1512–13 MS Vergil simply says Simonds changed the boy's name to Edward and took him to Ireland saying he had saved Warwick; the printed editions added the rumour about Edward IV's sons surviving and the "same age" detail (Phillips p. 4, citing Hay 1950). **[Hay not seen]**

**(d) London civic annals.** (i) "Historical notes of a London citizen, 1483–1488" (ed. R. F. Green, *EHR* 96 (1981), pp. 585–9): "a child was carried into Ireland saying he was the Duke of Clarence's son, 'and was nat'"; after Stoke "the Duke of Clarence's son and the other child that was in Ireland were shown openly at St Paul's in London on Relic Sunday, 8 July 1487" (Phillips p. 6). **[Unverified original]** (ii) Cotton Vitellius A XVI (Kingsford, *Chronicles of London*, 1905, p. 194; https://archive.org/details/chroniclesoflond00kinguoft) **[Verified]**: "this yere was Stoke feeld, wher by the kynges powre was slayne therle of Lyncolne, Maxten Swart, a Ducheman, and moche of the people that came w' theym" — no mention of the boy at all; likewise the Harley 541 and Julius B I entries (Kingsford pp. 278, 323 n.). Silence in London chronicles is itself notable.

### 2.3 Irish sources

**(a) Annals of Ulster** (ed. B. Mac Carthy, III (1895), pp. 298–9, 314–17; CELT text https://celt.ucc.ie/published/T100001C/). **[Verified, Mac Carthy's translation via CELT]**
- U1485.22: "The king of the Saxons, namely, king Richard III., was slain in battle and 1500 were slain in that battle and the son of the Welshman, he by whom the battle was given, was made king. And there lived not of the race of the blood royal that time but one young man, who came, on being exiled the year after, to Ireland."
- U1487.12: "A great fleet of Saxons came to Ireland this year to meet the son of the Duke of York, who was exiled at that time and living with the Earl of Kildare, namely, Gerald, son of Earl Thomas. And there lived not of the race of the blood royal that time but that son of the Duke and he was proclaimed king on the Sunday of the Holy Ghost in the town of Ath-cliath [Dublin] that time. And he went east with the fleet and many of the Irish went with him east, under the brother of the Earl of Kildare, namely, Thomas, son of the Earl and under Edward Plunket".
- U1487.24: "Battle was given between the two kings that were in Saxon-land this time—that is, the king that was a Welshman and the young man whom we mentioned before, who was called king in the town of Ath-cliath—and the battle went against that young man."
Readings: Smith 1996 (R) — "'son of the duke of York' … would best fit Edward V, since he is described as a young man and in exile"; Langley (R) likewise. Phillips (pp. 8, n. 47): "an impossible" identification — he cannot be a son of Richard, Duke of York (d. 1460) if a boy, nor of Richard of Shrewsbury; Edward IV is called "king" elsewhere in the same annals (1461, 1503); "the most natural interpretation seems to be that the writer thought the pretender was not a boy, but was a younger brother of Edward IV". Lewis (2018) canvasses "Duke of York" = Edward IV. The annal's coronation date (Whitsunday, 3 June) conflicts with the attainder (24 May); Randolph Jones argues Sunday 27 May.

**(b) Irish government records in the pretender's name** (all via Randolph Jones and Phillips pp. 8–10; originals destroyed 1922 or in private hands). **[Unverified originals]**
- 1494 Poynings parliament annulled "All records, processes, stiles, pardons, liveries, acts, and ordinances of Council, and all other acts done in the 'Laddes' name" (Gilbert 1865 p. 606 n. 3) — the boy is "the lad", unnamed.
- Red Book of the Irish Exchequer memorandum: a coronation at Dublin on 27 May, the king's name erased (Jones 2014b, pp. 207–9).
- Exchequer Memoranda Roll 2 Hen VII (abstracts by W. Lynch and J. R. Hutchinson, made independently before 1922): a writ dated "May, 1 Edward VI" (Jones 2014a, pp. 43–5). Langley (2023 pp. 425–7 nn. 25) suggests the regnal number could be a later addition; Phillips n. 52 rejects this.
- Original letters patent appointing Peter Butler sheriff of Kilkenny, dated 13 August "in the first year of our reign", in the name of "Edward … King of England, France and Ireland" (not "Lord of Ireland"), attested by Kildare as "Lieutenant" (not Deputy). Ormond Deeds; image at the Virtual Record Treasury of Ireland (captioned "Edward VI", though the document itself carries no numeral — Phillips n. 54). Smith 1996 (p. 516): "its seal appears to be that of Edward V … The most unforced interpretation … would seem to be that it was issued under Edward V in 1486." Langley's site: "A letter patent in the National Library of Ireland, dated 13 August 1486, is now attributed to Edward V (aged 15) and the first year of his reign … This grant may also be dated to 13 August 1487, when Edward V was 16." Jones (2023, "Was the Dublin king King Edward V or King Edward VI?", *Ricardian Bulletin* March 2023, pp. 57–60) and Phillips (pp. 9–10): 1486 is impossible because Irish chancery documents continued in Henry VII's name to 1 May 1487; and "If the document had been issued in August 1487 in the name of someone claiming to be Edward V, it would have been dated as of his fifth regnal year, not his first" (regnal years run from accession, as with Henry VI's readeption and Edward IV's restoration).
- Irish coins: legend "Edwardus Rex Anglie Francie / Et Rex Hybernie" or anonymous "Rex Anglie Francie et Rex Hybernie" (Carlyon-Britton 1941; Dolley 1969; Ashdown-Hill, *Ricardian* 19 (2009), 63–83) — the name Edward, no number.

**(c) Book of Howth** (Lambeth Carew MS; *Calendar of Carew MSS* v (1871), pp. 189–90; mid-16th-c. compilation largely dependent on Hall — Jones, "The Book of Howth's account of the Lambert Simnel conspiracy: an eyewitness account?", *Medieval Dublin* XVII (2019)). **[Unverified: volume v not online; quotations via Amin/Smith/Phillips]** "there in Dublin in Ireland they proclaimed this child King of England, being borne and sitting upon Darsey's shoulders to be seen of all men"; later at Greenwich Henry VII had "King Lambarte Symenell" serve wine to the Irish lords, and only Howth would drink, "declaring that Lambert Simnel was a poor innocent" (Smith 1996 pp. 515–16). Smith (R) reads the banquet as a staged acknowledgement; Jones (2019) reads it as Henry exhibiting both the ex-pretender and the real Warwick. Waterford civic tradition (quoted by Nathen Amin, "The Crowning of King Edward – or is that King Lambert?", Substack): "one Lambert, a boy, an organ-maker's son, was crowned at Dublin King of England and Lord of Ireland … The crown they took off the head of the image of our Lady of Damascus, and clapped it on the boy's head" **[Unverified; a later Tudor civic record]**.

**(d) Sir Richard Edgecombe's voyage, 1488** (Harris, *Hibernica* (1770), pp. 59–77, https://archive.org/details/hibernicaorsome00irelgoog). **[Verified]** The 1488 narrative records the new oaths ("I Gerald, Erle of Kildare, promit and othe me, that from hensefourth I shall be true faithfull and obeisaunt Liegeman and Subget untoo … Kyng Henry VIIth") but does not itself name or describe the pretender; the "Lambert Simnell" identification in that volume is Harris's 18th-century headnote and footnotes.

### 2.4 Burgundian/Habsburg and other Continental sources

**(a) Jean Molinet, *Chroniques*, ch. CLVIII** (ed. Buchon, 1828, III, pp. 151–156, https://archive.org/details/chroniquesdejea00buchgoog; ed. Doutrepont–Jodogne, 1935, I, pp. 562–5). Written c. 1490 by the official Burgundian historiographer. **[Verified, Buchon text]**
> Rubric: "Le couronnement du fils de Clarence en Irlande. Son emprisonnement en Engleterre, et la mort du seigneur Martin Zwatre."
> "Toutesfois ung rainceau extraict d'estoc de royale géniture, s'estoit nourri entre les fertils et seigneurieux arbrisseaux d'Irlande, lequel, quand il ot esté paré, flouri et eslevé en force, a voulu réparer et remettre en honneur resplendissant l'arche glorieuse et triumphante maison d'Iorcq, dont il est issu. Ce très noble rainceau est Edouard, fils du duc de Clarence, lequel, par le conseil et meure délibération des nobles d'Irlande, et en faveur de plusieurs barons d'Angleterre, ses bienveuillans, fut délibéré de soi couronner roy, et d'expulser de son royal trosne le comte de Richemont … fit advertir de son emprinse, très haulte et puissante dame, madame Marguerite d'Iorcq, ducesse de Bourgogne, sa tante … Ceste très haulte et puissante dame, diligenta tellement pour son nepveu, vers la sérénité de son beau-fils, roy des Romains, qu'elle esleva une bande d'Allemans en nombre de quinze à seize cents, desquels estoit principal conducteur et capitaine, sire Martin Zwatre. … ils se partirent de Hollande, et arrivèrent en Irlande, où ils trouvèrent le duc de Clarence, ensemble les comtes de Lynconne et de Guldar, et des nobles du pays, qui, du grément de tout le peuple, le firent couronner roy d'Angleterre par deux archevesques et douze évesques, puis le menèrent à main armée au pays du nort en Angleterre, et descendirent en une havre nommé le Four" (pp. 152–3).
> London rejoices: "crièrent à haulte voix, comme tous resjouis: Vive Werwicq au roy Edouard!" (p. 154). After Stoke: "Et le roy Edouard fut prins et mené prisonnier en la ville de Nieuwercq" (p. 156).
Translation: "…a shoot drawn from the stock of royal begetting had been nurtured among the fertile and lordly shrubs of Ireland, which, when it had been adorned, flowered and raised up in strength, wished to repair … the glorious house of York from which it issued. This most noble shoot is Edward, son of the Duke of Clarence, who by the counsel of the nobles of Ireland and with the favour of several English barons resolved to have himself crowned king … She [Margaret], his aunt, worked so hard for her nephew with her son-in-law the King of the Romans that she raised a band of 1,500–1,600 Germans under Sir Martin Schwartz … they found the Duke of Clarence, with the earls of Lincoln and Kildare … who had him crowned king of England by two archbishops and twelve bishops … 'Long live Warwick, to King Edward!' … And King Edward was taken and led prisoner to Newark." Molinet believed the claimant genuinely was Warwick ("the duke of Clarence", i.e. Clarence's heir) and never names Lambert.

## 3. The age and identity problem

- **Ages in 1487**: Edward V, b. 2 Nov 1470 — 16 in May–June 1487, 17 by 16 Dec 1487 (hence Langley's "Proof of Life (aged 17)"). Edward, Earl of Warwick, b. 25 Feb 1475 — 12. "Lambert Simnel" — "a child of x yere of age" (Attainder, Nov 1487) **[Verified]**. Vergil: the same age as Warwick ("ut alter alteri annis non praestaret"), a "puer", later revised in places to "adulescentulus"; Vergil's Warham speech implies both pretenders were "about fifteen" when Margaret "gave birth" to them; but Vergil also makes Warwick 15 in 1485 (Phillips n. 23; Smith 1996 pp. 512–13: "Adolescence normally covers the ages of thirteen to eighteen … The description could be applied to a lad of sixteen or seventeen, but hardly to the ten-year-old specified in the act of attainder. With some latitude 'adolescent' might fit the real Warwick, who was twelve in 1487."). Molinet: "paré, flouri et eslevé en force" — Bennett (1987 p. 130) renders "full grown, in the flower of manhood, and raised up in force"; Phillips (n. 23) says the botanical metaphor "might not imply that he was fully grown". Adrien de But: "juvenis". Annals of Ulster: "young man". André: "puerum".
- **Warwick displayed**: Vergil — led from the Tower through London to St Paul's on the Sunday after the council was dismissed; Convocation register (Pollard III p. 255 n.) — the Earl of Derby produced Warwick before Convocation with the mayor and sheriffs present, c. 19–20 Feb 1487; London citizen's notes — shown again with "the other child that was in Ireland" on 8 July 1487 (Phillips p. 6). The Heralds' Memoir has Lincoln claiming Warwick "shulde be in Irelande" while "himselfe knew, and daily spake with him at Shene" — the Tudor riposte.
- **Simnel's later life**: Vergil — kitchen spit-turner, then the king's falconer ("accipitrum domitor"), "vivit adhuc" (MS written 1512–13, so alive then; the "died 1525/1534" dates are inferences). Book of Howth — serves wine to the Irish lords at Greenwich. Attended Sir Thomas Lovell's funeral, 1524 (L&P Henry VIII iv/1 p. 150, via Phillips n. 20) **[Unverified]**. Milanese envoy 1497 confuses him with the anointed youth "living in the Tower".
- **The dilemma stated by both sides**: a ten-year-old (Attainder) could hardly be the "young man"/"juvenis" who was crowned and led an army; Ricardians (Smith, Langley, Lewis) infer the crowned youth was older — i.e. Edward V (16) — and that "Lambert Simnel, aged ten" is a substitute; traditionalists reply that ages in these texts are loose (Vergil gets Warwick's own age wrong by five years), that a 12-year-old "Warwick" fits "puer/adolescens", and that André's Henry personally interrogated the captive and found a "vapulo" of base parentage. Smith (p. 514) also notes the Attainder's "joiner" versus Convocation's "organ-maker" (Pollard III p. 247 n. 1: Wilkins prints "orginmakes"; the Attainder says "joynoure").
- **What "Edward VI" implies**: a sixth Edward is by definition *not* Edward V. The number appears (i) in the York clerk's heading (Attreed's reading "vjt"; Raine misprinted "V"); (ii) in two independent 19th/20th-c. abstracts of a lost Irish Exchequer roll ("1 Edward VI"); and (iii) implicitly in the Butler patent's "first year of our reign" (13 Aug), which on Jones's and Phillips's argument cannot be Edward V's (whose first year was 1483–4). It does *not* appear in the letter text, on the coins, in the Heralds' Memoir, in Molinet, or in any Irish narrative; Smith (p. 516): "There seems to be no extant contemporary Irish evidence … for 'Edward VI'". Amin (2025): "the regnal number remains unclear". Phillips's position: the two Irish administrative traces are "inconsistent with the theory that the pretender claimed to be Edward V" (p. 10). Langley's position: the Butler patent dates from 13 Aug 1486 (Edward V's "first year" being reckoned from a 1486 Irish recognition), or from 1487 "in order to denote the first year of his reign" after the Dublin coronation.

## 4. Scholarly treatments and the 2023–2026 responses

### 4.1 Pre-2023
- **Michael Bennett, *Lambert Simnel and the Battle of Stoke* (Alan Sutton/St Martin's, 1987; reissued History Press 2023)** (T, academic). Standard monograph; accepts the boy as an impostor Warwick; prints translations of Molinet (pp. 129–31) and of Vergil's MS (pp. 133–8); examined Cotton Julius B XII and found the herald wrote "John". **[Content via Phillips and Smith; book not accessible — archive.org copy is lending-only]**
- **Gordon Smith, "Lambert Simnel and the King from Dublin", *The Ricardian* 10, no. 135 (Dec 1996), pp. 498–536** (R; PDF at https://richardiii.net/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/10-135-Lambert-Simnel-and-the-King-from-Dublin.pdf). **[Verified read]** Thesis: "three chronicles written within a generation of the crowning of 1487 give three different identities for the king from Dublin: Molinet's Warwick, André's false York, and Vergil's false Warwick" (p. 499); the herald's "John"; York's "Edward the sixth"; the Butler patent seal "appears to be that of Edward V" and dates from 1486; the Annals of Ulster "best fit Edward V"; "Lambert Simnel" is probably a government pseudonym; concludes the Dublin king may have been Edward V, "only a possibility". This is the seed of Langley's Simnel argument.
- **John Ashdown-Hill, "Coins attributed to the Yorkist pretenders", *Ricardian* 19 (2009), 63–83; *The Dublin King* (History Press, 2015)** (R). Argues the Dublin king may genuinely have been Clarence's son/heir and proposes DNA testing; identifies de But's "Earl of Suffolk" with Edmund de la Pole (Phillips n. 63 objects: de But died 1488). **[Book not accessible; via Phillips and publisher blurb]**
- **Randolph Jones** (independent; *Ricardian Bulletin* Sept 2014, March 2021, March 2023; *Medieval Dublin* XV (2014) and XVII (2019)): the Irish "Edward VI" evidence, the coronation date 27 May, the Butler patent, the Book of Howth. **[Via Phillips]**
- **Nathen Amin, *Henry VII and the Tudor Pretenders: Simnel, Warbeck and Warwick* (Amberley, 2020)** (T). **[Not accessed]** His 2025 view (Renaissance English History Podcast, Apr 2025, https://www.englandcast.com/2025/04/princes-in-the-tower-3/): the receipt is "completely authentic" and "hugely compelling" if taken alone, but "could be explained away very simply" — pretenders were routinely misidentified/upgraded (French officials called Henry Tudor "the son of Henry VI"); contemporary sources "consistently refer to conspiracies favoring 'Edward of Warwick'"; Henry parading the real Warwick "contradicts theories the imposter represented a Princes survivor"; the Irish backed Clarence's line because Clarence was born in Dublin. His Substack ("The Crowning of King Edward – or is that King Lambert?") collects the Howth/Waterford/coin evidence and concludes: "I do believe the Irish were firm in their conviction they were truly crowning a Yorkist prince", while the regnal number "remains unclear".

### 4.2 Langley's case (R) and the published responses
- **Langley 2023/2025** (with Nathalie Nijman-Bliekendaal): the receipt is "Proof of Life"; "Four of the receipts details confirm the weapons were for Edward V. He was the nephew of Margaret of Burgundy, the son of King Edward (IV), the right age to lead an army and fight in battle (16), and had been 'expelled from his dominion' (to the Channel Islands). The Lille receipt also suggests that Edward V was alive, or thought to be alive, in December 1487 (age 17)"; Henry "reverse-engineer[ed]" the impostor story: "Edward V became a 10 year-old boy called 'Lambert Simnel', the son of a joiner, tailor, barber, baker, organ-maker or shoemaker" (solved.html). Her handling of the Clarence-son sources: Henry "successfully muddied the waters" / the description was used in Flanders "to protect Edward's identity" / "simply … a misnomer that took hold in common parlance" (2023 pp. 293, 422, 424, per Phillips n. 71).
- **Strongest published defence**: Matt Lewis (R; Richard III Society), "The Lambert Simnel Affair", 9 Jan 2025 — the receipt "has no propaganda value, so little motive to lie"; the Heralds' Memoir says only "King Edwarde", the numbering being added "when the official story had taken shape"; a 16-year-old commander is unremarkable (Black Prince, Henry V, Edward IV); conclusion: the affair was "an uprising in favour of one of the Princes in the Tower, most likely a 16-year-old Edward V", though this "remains beyond proof". **[Verified read]**

### 4.3 Where the weight lies
- Against the Edward V reading: Convocation record + Heralds' Memoir + Henry's letter to the Pope + papal mandate + London notes + York "vjt" + Irish regnal-year traces + Molinet + de But + Voorne + Mechelen + Weinreich + Vergil (+ Henry to Talbot 1493; Warbeck's 1497 confession). Only André (and he says *second* son) and the Lille clerk say "son of Edward IV".
- For it: the Lille receipt (a private, non-propaganda Habsburg record); the Annals of Ulster's "son of the Duke of York" and "young man"; the age implausibility of a ten-year-old warrior-king; the herald's "King Edwarde" without number; the Society/Smith reading of the Butler patent seal.

## (a) Ten-line summary: what the Lille receipt can and cannot bear
1. It is a genuine Habsburg artillery acquit, ADN Lille B 3521 no. 124564, dated 16 Dec 1487: a Mechelen wood-turner (de Smet/"de Finet") paid for 400 pikes for Martin Schwartz's force, sent by "Madame la Douairière" for "her nephew, son of King Edward, late her brother, expelled from his dominion".
2. It proves that in Dec 1487 a junior secretary in Maximilian's artillery office described Margaret's protégé as a son of Edward IV — nothing more; no name, no "Edward V", no regnal number, no Ireland.
3. It was abstracted in print in 1895 (Finot, *Inventaire sommaire* VIII, pp. 265–6) and used by Bletzer in 2013; "discovered 2020" is misleading.
4. Its own account of the pikes' use is chronologically impossible (bought "June" 1487 for troops that sailed by early May), which weakens its reliability on incidental matters like identity.
5. The identifying phrase sits only in the merchant's receipt drafted by the secretary Hauweel, not in the certificate signed by the two artillery officers.
6. Every other Burgundian-side record — Molinet, de But (three entries), the Voorne and Mechelen accounts, Weinreich — calls him the son of Clarence/Warwick; so does Henry VII to the Pope, the papal mandate, the Convocation confession, the Heralds' Memoir and Vergil.
7. Ireland's surviving traces ("1 Edward VI"; Butler patent "first year", 13 Aug) point to a *sixth* Edward, unless one accepts Langley's 1486 dating of the patent.
8. The Annals of Ulster ("son of the Duke of York", "young man") and André ("filium Eduardi Quarti … secundum filium") are the only early texts pointing to a son of Edward IV — and André says Richard, not Edward.
9. The real anomaly is age: the Attainder's ten-year-old versus the "young man"/"juvenis"/"adolescens" of Irish, Flemish and (revised) Vergilian texts; this is a genuine problem for the official story, but it does not identify the youth as Edward V rather than a 12-year-old "Warwick" or an older substitute.
10. Net: the receipt is admissible evidence that *someone at Maximilian's court thought* the 1487 claimant was Edward IV's son; it cannot bear the weight of "proof of life" for Edward V, and the published critiques (Phillips 2024, Hicks 2024, Barnfield 2024, Amin 2025) have not been answered on the Irish regnal-year point or on Molinet/de But.
