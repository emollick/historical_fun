Princes in the Tower

Did the Princes in the Tower Survive?

On 21 June 1483 a servant of the Lord Chancellor wrote to a friend that the nine-year-old Duke of York was in the Tower with his brother, “blessid be Jhesus, mery”. No one recorded a dated sighting of either boy again. In 2023 a research project led by the writer Philippa Langley said four documents show that they lived. Here are those documents, and the sources for the murder, in their own words.

The mystery

In the spring of 1483 England had a boy king. Edward IV, the first king of the house of York, died on 9 April, leaving two sons: Edward, Prince of Wales, who was twelve and became King Edward V, and Richard, Duke of York, who was nine. The boys’ uncle Richard, Duke of Gloucester, the dead king’s younger brother, was named Protector, to govern for the boy king. On 30 April, at Stony Stratford on the road to London, he took charge of the young king from the escort provided by the boy’s mother’s family and brought him to the capital, where he was lodged in the Tower of London, then a royal residence as well as a fortress and a prison, and the usual place for a king to wait for his coronation.

The boys’ mother, Queen Elizabeth Woodville, took her other children into sanctuary at Westminster Abbey, where by law no one could lay hands on them. On 16 June, with armed men filling Westminster, the younger boy was handed over to the Cardinal Archbishop of Canterbury and taken to join his brother in the Tower. Five days later Simon Stallworth, a servant of the Lord Chancellor, wrote to an Oxfordshire knight, Sir William Stonor, that the Duke of York was in the Tower with his brother, “blessid be Jhesus, mery”. In the same week Lord Hastings, the late king’s chamberlain, had been beheaded on 13 June; on 22 June the boys were publicly declared illegitimate, on the ground that their father’s marriage to their mother had not been valid, which put them out of the succession; and on 26 June Gloucester accepted the crown. He was crowned Richard III on 6 July.

From that point the boys were seen less often. Dominic Mancini, an Italian visitor who was in London until July and wrote up what he had seen for a French patron before the year was out, says that after Hastings’ death the boys’ attendants were dismissed, that the brothers were moved into the inner apartments of the Tower, and that they “began to be seen more rarely behind the bars and windows, till at length they ceased to appear altogether”. The young king’s physician, John Argentine, told Mancini that the boy went to confession every day because he believed he was going to die. A London chronicle records the boys shooting and playing in the Tower garden at some point in the mayoral year that ended in October 1483. No one recorded a dated sighting of either boy again. In the south of England men began to plot to free them. By October the plotters had heard that the boys were dead, and rose instead for Henry Tudor, an exile in Brittany with a distant claim to the throne, who swore on Christmas Day 1483 to marry the boys’ eldest sister, Elizabeth of York. In January 1484 the Chancellor of France told the Estates General, the assembly of the French realm, meeting at Tours, that Edward IV’s children had been murdered and the crown given to their murderer. Richard III never answered the charge and never showed the boys, and in March 1484 he gave their mother a public oath for the safety of her daughters that did not mention her sons. He was killed at Bosworth in August 1485 by Henry Tudor’s army. Henry became Henry VII and married Elizabeth of York.

Why it is a mystery

Nothing after the summer of 1483 is certain. No one who wrote at the time claims to know whether the boys died, or when, or how. Richard III never said. Henry VII’s first parliament condemned Richard by act of attainder, a parliamentary sentence, for “shedding of Infants blood”, but named no victims; Henry never produced bodies, held no inquest, and never published the confession that Sir James Tyrell, a knight who had served Richard III, is supposed to have made before his execution in May 1502. No text of any confession survives. The first full account of a murder was written about thirty years after the event by Thomas More, the lawyer who later became Henry VIII’s Lord Chancellor. It names Tyrell as the organiser and two men, Miles Forest and John Dighton, as the killers; it says the bodies were buried at the foot of a stair and then moved by a priest who died without telling anyone where; and it admits that “some remain yet in doubt” whether the boys died in Richard’s reign at all.

In 1674 workmen taking down a staircase in the White Tower, the central keep of the Tower of London, found the bones of two children about ten feet down. Charles II had them placed in an urn in Westminster Abbey. They were examined once, in 1933, by the Abbey’s archivist (its Keeper of the Muniments) and a professor of anatomy, who worked on the assumption that More’s story was true. They have never been dated, sexed or tested for DNA, and the Abbey has declined requests to test them in 1993, 1995, 2013 and 2018. There is therefore no body, no confession, no witness and no contemporary record of a death. The evidence consists of the disappearance and the conduct of those around the boys.

The two sides

Most historians of the period conclude that both boys died in the Tower in 1483, most probably on Richard’s orders, because that is what the conduct of everyone around them implies. This is the position of the standard academic studies of the reign, by Charles Ross (1981), Rosemary Horrox (1989), A. J. Pollard (1991), Michael Hicks (2003, and again in a 2024 article) and Sean Cunningham (2003), and of the recent biographies by David Horspool (2015) and Chris Skidmore (2017).

Against them stand Richard III’s defenders, known as Ricardians and organised since 1924 in the Richard III Society. They point out that no death is recorded, that the fullest accounts of a murder were written under the dynasty that replaced Richard, and that the two young men who claimed the Yorkist crown in Henry VII’s reign might have been the princes themselves. The first was a boy crowned “King Edward” at Christ Church, Dublin, on 24 May 1487, with an army of German mercenaries under Martin Schwartz sent by the boys’ aunt Margaret, Edward IV’s sister and the widowed Duchess of Burgundy. He was captured when that army was destroyed at Stoke, near Newark, on 16 June. Henry’s government said he was Lambert Simnel, an Oxford organ-maker’s son coached to pass as the boys’ cousin Edward, Earl of Warwick, the son of their uncle George, Duke of Clarence, whom Henry kept in the Tower. The second was a young man who appeared at Cork in 1491 as Richard, Duke of York, and was received under that name by Margaret in 1492, by Maximilian, the Habsburg King of the Romans (the elected German king) who governed the Low Countries, in 1493, and by James IV of Scotland in 1495. He was captured in 1497, confessed to being Piers Werbecque, called Perkin Warbeck, born at Tournai, a town in what is now Belgium, and was hanged in November 1499.

In 2023 Philippa Langley, the writer who led the search that found Richard III’s grave under a car park in Leicester in 2012, and her Missing Princes Project put four documents forward as evidence that both young men were who they said they were: a receipt in the accounts of Maximilian’s artillery, kept at Lille in northern France and dated December 1487, which calls the Dublin king “son of King Edward”; a first-person account of Richard of York’s escape from the Tower, in a manuscript of about 1500 in the provincial archive of Gelderland at Arnhem in the Netherlands; a pledge by “Richard of England” to Albert, Duke of Saxony, Maximilian’s commander in the Low Countries, dated at Dresden in October 1493; and a letter of Maximilian’s from the same winter naming three bodily marks by which the claimant could be known. Nobody on either side disputes that these documents are genuine. The dispute is over what the documents prove.

How the case was judged

Everything used here is in print or online. On the survival side: the four documents above and the records around them, that is the Irish grants and coins of 1487, the accounts of Margaret’s estates and towns, the chronicles of the Burgundian court, the claimant’s own letters of 1493 and 1497, the depositions about him taken at Setúbal, near Lisbon, in 1496, and the town records of Tournai. On the murder side: every source from Stallworth’s letter of June 1483 to More and the bones. Each document is quoted in its own language, Latin, French, Middle English, Irish or Dutch, with a translation, wherever an edition could be reached. Mancini’s Latin, the Dutch of the Arnhem manuscript and the Great Chronicle of London, a city chronicle written up about 1512, could not be, and are marked where they occur. Nothing was seen in manuscript.

One question was asked of each document. For the survival documents: does this show a prince alive, or only that a claimant, or someone paying for one, said so? For the murder sources: does the writer claim to know of a death, report a rumour, or draw an inference, and whose side was he on? Each carries a tag saying which. Modern scholarship is named as such, and partisanship, Ricardian or traditionalist (holding Richard guilty), is flagged where it affects reliability. The weighing at the end is written out as a ledger. The evidence is sorted into five clusters; each cluster is given a number for how likely that body of evidence would be if each of five outcomes were true; and the numbers are combined in the standard way for weighing evidence (Bayes’s rule) from a stated starting weight for each outcome. Each number is a judgment about the evidence, and another reader might set it differently. Their job is to make the weighing checkable and to show how far the verdict moves when any one of them changes. The result is that both boys most probably died in the Tower in 1483, in the late summer or autumn, while in Richard III’s custody: about 85 chances in 100. The chance that one or both lived on, as the Dublin king, as the 1490s claimant or as someone unrecorded, comes out at about 6 in 100, and nearly all of that is an allowance for evidence not yet found. The rest, about 9 in 100, covers a death in 1484 or 1485 still in Richard’s keeping, a death under Henry VII, or a death abroad as children. The section headed “Weighing it” shows how far these figures move when any one judgment changes.

The reasons, in short, are these. The survival case rests on documents that record what a claimant and his backers said. Read in their own archives, they contain nothing a pretender’s court would not also have produced. The strongest of them, the Lille receipt of December 1487, is contradicted by every other contemporary record from Ireland, England and Burgundy that says whom the boy claimed to be (two later or ambiguous texts are discussed below). It is also contradicted by the dates on the boy’s own documents, an argument worked out by Randolph Jones, who has published on the Irish records in The Ricardian, the Richard III Society’s journal, and restated in 2024 by Chris Phillips, an independent researcher. The survival side has not answered it. The murder case is not proof either. No contemporary claims to know how the boys died, and no later narrative is more than report. But the boys vanished from view in the summer of 1483; London believed them dead by the autumn; their mother’s allies acted on that belief; and Richard, who could have ended the rumour by producing two living nephews, did not do so in the two years of his reign. His failure to produce the boys is the strongest evidence that they were dead.

The witnesses

A few sources recur in the tables and are cited there by short names. Mancini has been introduced. “Crowland” is the Crowland chronicle, an anonymous continuation of the history of Crowland Abbey in Lincolnshire, written in ten days in April 1486 by a man who describes himself as a doctor of canon law and a councillor of Edward IV; it is the best-placed English account of the reign. Polydore Vergil was an Italian scholar who wrote a history of England at Henry VII’s encouragement, drafted about 1512 and printed in 1534. The Great Chronicle of London and Robert Fabyan’s New Chronicles are London civic chronicles written up about 1512 and 1516. Titulus Regius is the act of Richard III’s parliament of 1484 that set out his title to the crown, and “Rot. Parl.” is the Rolls of Parliament, the official record, in the printing of 1767. Harleian MS 433 in the British Library is Richard III’s own register of grants and letters. Masselin’s Journal is the record kept by Jehan Masselin, one of the deputies, of the French Estates General at Tours in 1484. Morton’s register is the record of John Morton, Archbishop of Canterbury, and includes the proceedings of Convocation, the assembly of the clergy. The Heralds’ Memoir is an account of Henry VII’s early years kept by the royal officers of arms. Jean Molinet was the official historian of the Burgundian court. “Gairdner, Letters and Papers” is James Gairdner’s 1861 edition of the state letters of Richard III’s and Henry VII’s reigns; the Ormond Deeds are the charters of the Butler earls of Ormond in Ireland, edited by Edmund Curtis; “ADN Lille” is the departmental archive of the Nord at Lille; “CSP Spain”, “CSP Venice” and “CSP Milan” are the Calendars of State Papers, English summaries of the despatches of the Spanish, Venetian and Milanese envoys. Ann Wroe’s Perkin (2003) is the fullest modern study of the 1490s claimant. Tim Thornton is a historian whose articles of 2021 and 2024 trace More’s informants and the afterlife of Edward V’s belongings. “TNA PROB 11” is the register of wills at the National Archives. Tanner and Wright’s article of 1935 is the report of the 1933 examination of the bones, and P. W. Hammond’s of 1976, in The Ricardian, is a review of it.

The survival case, document by document

Philippa Langley’s The Princes in the Tower: Solving History’s Greatest Cold Case (2023; revised 2025) and the Channel 4 television film of November 2023 rest on four named documents: a receipt for pikes at Lille (December 1487), a first-person narrative of Richard of York’s escape in the Gelderland provincial archive (Gelders Archief) at Arnhem, a pledge by “Richard of England” to Albert of Saxony at Dresden (October 1493), and a letter of Maximilian’s (November–December 1493) naming three bodily marks by which the claimant could be known. Around these sit Irish letters patent of 1487, a life of Henry VII written about 1500 by Bernard André, the blind French poet who served as the king’s court historian, and arguments from how Elizabeth Woodville and Henry VII behaved. Their authenticity is not in dispute. What follows asks what each of them proves.

What it can bear. It shows that in December 1487 someone in Maximilian’s artillery office described Margaret of York’s protégé of that spring as a son of her brother King Edward. It names no Edward V, carries no regnal number and mentions no Ireland. Its own account of the pikes cannot stand as abstracted: bought “in June 1487” for a force that Henry VII already knew had landed in Ireland on 5 May (Henry VII’s letter to the Earl of Ormond, an Irish magnate loyal to him, printed in J. The identifying words stand in the merchant’s receipt drafted by the secretary Florens Hauweel; the certificate signed by the two artillery officers attests the sale and delivery, not the description (Phillips 2024, pp. 13–14). It is evidence of what one clerk believed, not of the boy’s identity.

The regnal year

The argument is Randolph Jones’s, worked out from the Irish records in three articles in The Ricardian (2014, 2021, 2023) and restated by Chris Phillips (2024, pp. 9–10). The printed records bear it out. Regnal years, the years of a king’s reign by which documents were dated, were counted in England and Ireland from accession, not coronation. Edward V’s first year ran from 9 April 1483 to 8 April 1484; his fifth would have run from 9 April 1487. A document issued on 13 August 1487 in Edward V’s name would be dated anno regni nostri quinto. The Butler patent (the grant of 13 August in the table) is dated in the first year, and the lost exchequer roll dated a writ of May 1487 to “1 Edward VI”. Langley’s answers are that the patent belongs to 1486, or that the count restarted at the Dublin coronation. The first is impossible: the Irish chancery was still issuing in Henry VII’s name on 1 May 1487 (Jones 2021, p. 49). The second has no precedent. Henry VI, the king of the rival house of Lancaster restored for six months in 1470, dated by “the forty-ninth year of our reign and the first of our readeption”; Edward IV, restored in 1471, continued his count; a regnal year that could be repeated would have been useless for dating (Jones 2023; Phillips 2024, pp. 9–10). Gordon Smith, the Richard III Society writer who first argued in 1996 that the Dublin king was Edward V, read the same patent and had to date it to 1486 to make it fit (Smith 1996, p. 516); that dating cannot stand.

The result is the same in all three jurisdictions. In England the boy was an organ-maker’s son taken for Warwick, and Henry VII said so in a private letter to the Pope whose purpose was to get four Irish bishops prosecuted, in a country where everyone knew whom they had crowned. In Ireland he was a new King Edward, in his first year, styling himself King rather than Lord of Ireland, the title English kings then used there. In the Burgundian Netherlands the duchess’s own estate paid for men “to support the Duke of Clarence, her brother’s son”, Mechelen gave him wine as “the son of Clarence”, and the court historiographer wrote him up as Edward, son of Clarence. Langley’s reading requires every one of these records to be mistaken and the artillery clerk alone to be correct.

André, the Annals, and the age of the boy

Two texts are usually cited beside the receipt. Bernard André, Henry VII’s blind court poet, writing around 1500 with blanks left where names should go, says the boy was put about as a son of Edward IV; but he says “Edward’s second son”, that is Richard of York, never Edward V, and his own Henry describes Margaret as one who “is not unaware that her blood was extinguished by her brother Richard” (sanguinem suum a Ricardo fratre suo extinctum non ignorat). Langley quotes the first phrase and omits the second (Phillips 2024, n. 39). The likeliest explanation is that André, thirteen years on, ran the 1487 boy together with the 1490s claimant, who did claim to be Richard of York and was likewise first recognised in Ireland and then taken up by Margaret. The Annals of Ulster call the Dublin king “the son of the Duke of York” and, under 1485, the one surviving “young man of the race of the blood royal”, exiled to Ireland the next year. Read literally the phrase fits no one: Richard, Duke of York (the boys’ grandfather, killed in 1460) had no son young enough, and Richard of Shrewsbury (the younger prince himself, named from his birthplace) had none; read as “grandson of the Duke of York” it fits Clarence’s son as well as Edward IV’s; and the same annals call Edward IV “king” under 1461 and 1503 (Phillips 2024, n. 47). The entry shows only that a Gaelic annalist far from Dublin described the boy loosely; it does not show that the boy was Edward V.

One point remains unresolved: the boy’s age. The attainder makes Lambert Simnel “a child of x yere of age”; Vergil makes him the same age as Warwick, twelve; Molinet, de But and the Annals describe a youth, and the person crowned and put at the head of an army was a youth. Ricardian writers infer that the crowned figure was older than the official ten-year-old, and therefore Edward V (sixteen). The inference does not follow. Ages in these texts are loose (Vergil misdates Warwick’s own age by five years), a twelve-year-old “Warwick” fits the Latin words used, puer (boy) and adolescens (youth), and if the government did understate the age of a substitute, that is a separate question from the identity the boy claimed, on which every record but one agrees.

Finding on 1487. The Dublin king claimed to be Edward, Earl of Warwick, or at any rate a new King Edward, not Edward V: about 95 per cent, as a judgment on the records above. The Lille receipt is best read as a clerk’s assumption that Margaret’s nephew was her brother the king’s son. Chris Phillips reached the same conclusion in 2024, in a note written with four members of the Dutch research group of Langley’s own project, and so did Michael Hicks in Historical Research the same year.

Elizabeth Woodville and Henry VII’s silence

Two arguments from conduct run through the survival literature. Elizabeth Woodville left sanctuary on 1 March 1484 under Richard’s public oath that her daughters would be kept “in surety of their lives” and married to gentlemen born (BL Harleian MS 433); a mother, it is said, would not have dealt with her sons’ murderer. Then on 12 February 1487, weeks before the Simnel plot surfaced, Henry VII moved her to Bermondsey Abbey, a religious house across the river from the Tower, and transferred her dower (the lands assigned to her as a widow) to her daughter, and her son by her first marriage, the Marquess of Dorset, was arrested; Vergil hints at punishment for her 1484 dealings with Richard, and Ricardians read it as punishment for backing a living son. Neither argument is strong. The 1484 oath is stronger evidence for the other side: it provides for daughters only, in March 1484, and a queen with living sons in the king’s custody would have asked for them. Her retirement in 1487 coincides with Henry’s suspicion of the whole Woodville connection at the moment a Yorkist rising was gathering, which would follow whether the figurehead was Warwick or anyone else; and in November 1487 Henry was still considering her as a bride for James III of Scotland, which he would not have done had he believed her to be plotting with a surviving rival king.

Henry VII’s first parliament attainted Richard for “shedding of Infants blood” (Rot. Parl. VI, 276) but named no victims; he produced no bodies, held no inquest, published no confession in 1502, and the first full account is More’s. The silence is consistent with a king who believed the boys dead but could not prove it, and Henry did say what he believed: to Sir Gilbert Talbot, one of his captains, in July 1493 the new claimant was a “feigned” boy who had tried three identities in turn, and André’s Henry treats Edward IV’s sons as extinguished. Under the hypothesis that Henry himself had them killed after 1485, the objection made in 1983 by Jeremy Potter, a Ricardian writer, stands: he would have produced the corpses and a story blaming Richard. The silence therefore counts a little against survival and a little for the outcome in which nobody under Henry knew where the bodies lay.

The claimant of 1491–97 and the Gelderland manuscript

The second claimant appeared at Cork in the autumn of 1491, was entertained by Charles VIII, King of France, in 1492, by Margaret of York from late 1492, by Maximilian from 1493 and by James IV from 1495, landed in Cornwall in September 1497, surrendered at Beaulieu Abbey in Hampshire, confessed, and was hanged at Tyburn, London’s gallows, on 23 November 1499. The survival thesis holds that he was Richard of York. Its documents are the Arnhem narrative, the claimant’s own letters and charters, and the acts of the courts that received him. Against them stand the records of three jurisdictions that had no stake in the answer.

What it can bear. This is the story in the claimant’s own name, six weeks before the Dresden pledge and before the Gelderland text, whose narrative ends at Margaret’s court and whose copy the archive dates about 1500. Two things follow. Richard of York was born on 17 August 1473 (his christening and his 1478 marriage are dated by it) and was ten when the boys vanished; “nonum fere agens annum” is a year short. James Gairdner, the Victorian historian of Richard III’s reign, saw this in 1898 (p. 268 n.): “Warbeck, however, supposed the character he was personating to have been not quite nine years old in 1483; which, we may presume, nearly tallied with his own age,” adding the Venetian envoy Trevisan’s report that he was 23 in November 1497 (CSP Venice I, no. 760), so born about 1474. The observation is Gairdner’s. Set beside it, the Gelderland text makes the same boy “about fourteen” at Lisbon. The letter also explains the Gelderland text: a claimant had to tell his story to courts, and the Arnhem copy is a fuller version of the same story into which the real Werbecque’s travels have been added. Tyrell and Forest

Michael Hicks has called the Arnhem narrative “the earliest record connecting Richard, duke of York, with Sir James Tyrell and Miles Forest”, and Tim Thornton cites it in the same sense (in the journal History, 109, 2024, n. 50); the observation is theirs. The narrative names Sir James Tyrell and “my lord Foriest” among the men who had custody of the boys. More’s History, written about thirty years after the events and some fifteen years after the Arnhem copy, names Tyrell as the commissioner and Miles Forest as one of the two killers, and Tim Thornton has shown (History 106, 2021) that Forest’s sons Edward and Miles were at Henry VIII’s court in More’s own circle in the 1510s, so that More had a plausible route to the family. The two texts, one from the claimant’s camp and one from the Tudor court, agree on who kept the boys and disagree on what became of them. What the overlap most naturally shows is a shared body of Yorkist knowledge, current among the exiles of the 1490s, about the men in charge of the Tower in the late summer of 1483. That Forest was one of the custodians is probably true. The overlap does not show whether Forest then helped to remove Richard, as the Arnhem text says, or helped to kill him, as More says.

Finding on the claimant of 1491–97. On the ledger’s weights the chance that Richard of York survived in any form is about 4 in 100 (2 alone, 2 with his brother), nearly all of it the general allowance for evidence not yet found; that he survived as this claimant is a part of that figure. Every document that treats him as York is his own, a backer’s, or a routine grace issued on his own petition. The three independent bodies that examined the question before his capture (the Portuguese witnesses at Setúbal, the Spanish sovereigns through Rui de Sousa, the French council) identified him as a commoner of Tournai. The Gelderland narrative, the fullest of his stories, has absorbed that boy’s itinerary, and the story in his own name misstates the prince’s age. What would change this: a record from 1483–91 of Richard of York’s whereabouts made by someone not later paid to say so, or physical evidence, which the Tournai and English family lines might one day supply.

What it can bear. Until one is, the bones cannot bear a date, an identity or a cause of death. They are also the one piece of evidence that could settle the whole question: children of the right ages in the wrong century would end the murder story as told, and a match to the Yorkist maternal line with a date in the 1480s would end the survival story.

Finding on the murder sources. Nothing here is knowledge of a death. What the sources establish is a sequence: two boys alive on 21 June 1483; withdrawn from sight after Hastings’ execution; a suspicion of death in London by July and a report of killing that summer in the Hanseatic port of Danzig; a rebellion raised to free them that turned, by October, into a rebellion for another king; the boys’ own household men swearing to that king at Christmas; both deaths asserted as fact in France by January; and a king who, through two years in which the rumour was costing him his southern support, never produced the boys, never explained their absence, and in March 1484 gave their mother an oath that covered her daughters only. The later narratives add names and methods that contradict one another and prove nothing beyond the strength of the belief. The case for death in 1483 is a case from conduct, Richard’s above all. It is strong because the conduct is not in dispute and has no good explanation on any hypothesis in which the boys were alive and could have been shown. It is not proof; the numbers in the next section quantify a judgment and do not prove it.

The strongest case for survival

Put at its best, the case runs like this. Henry VII never named the boys as victims, never produced bodies, never published Tyrell’s confession, and never addressed the question publicly. Elizabeth Woodville came to terms with Richard in 1484 and was shut away by Henry in 1487, weeks before a Yorkist rising. The Earl of Lincoln, who had a better claim than Warwick, went to war behind a boy. A Habsburg receipt calls that boy a son of Edward IV and so, in his way, does Henry’s own court poet; the official Lambert Simnel is ten years old while the crowned youth leads an army. The 1490s claimant was polished, was recognised by the aunt who had seen the real boy in 1480, was vouched for by the King of the Romans down to marks on his body, was married by James IV to a kinswoman of his own, and told a coherent story of his escape that survives in a manuscript of about 1500 at Arnhem. Meanwhile the murder story rests on Mancini, who says he could learn nothing; on Crowland, who reports a rumour; on a French chancellor making a political speech; and on More, who wrote thirty years later and describes a burial that he says was undone.

None of this is false, but none of it survives two tests. The 1487 boy was dated in his first regnal year by the men who governed in his name, which Edward V could not have been unless the count restarted, for which no precedent is known; the 1490s claimant was placed in a Tournai family by Portuguese witnesses deposed in April 1496, a year before Henry VII had him, and by Tournai’s own records. Patrons at war with Henry VII had reason to recognise any claimant: Charles VIII received the same man and dropped him at the treaty of Étaples, his peace with Henry VII in November 1492. Richard’s silence weighs more than Henry’s. A king in Richard’s position, whose southern gentry had risen on a report that his nephews were dead, would have shown two living nephews if he had them. He did not do so in 1483, in 1484 after the death of his own son made the succession urgent, or in 1485 when Henry Tudor’s invasion was expected.

What would overturn this verdict

The identity of the Dublin king would be reopened by a dated Irish document of 1487 reckoning “King Edward’s” regnal year from April 1483, or by proof that the memoranda-roll abstracts were mis-copied.

The survival question would be reopened by a record independent of claimants and their patrons showing a prince alive after the autumn of 1483: a household or estate entry, a safe-conduct, a letter, securely naming Edward V or Richard of York rather than “the lord bastard” (which fits John of Gloucester, Richard III’s illegitimate son, as well) or “the Duke of York” as a claimant styled himself. So would proof that the Setúbal depositions or Symonds’s confession were fabricated, or that Tournai’s Werbecque family did not exist as described. So would a demonstration that the Gelderland narrative contains checkable facts that only the real Richard of York could have known, as opposed to facts that Yorkist exiles at Margaret’s court would have known; nothing of that kind has been identified.

The bones in the urn could also decide it. Radiocarbon dating and sexing of the 1674 bones, with a comparison of mitochondrial DNA (inherited through the mother) against a Woodville maternal line reported as traced in 2018, would not say who was responsible. But bones of two boys of the right ages, of the right century and of that line, found where More said the bodies were first laid, would end the survival hypothesis; bones of the wrong century or sex would remove C5 and lower the verdict to about 82 per cent (the ledger’s drop-C5 line) without touching 1483. As of March 2026, the latest report found, neither the Abbey’s governing body, the Dean and Chapter, nor the Crown had changed the position taken in 1993, 1995 and 2018.

What is new here

Nothing on this page is a new document, a new reading of a manuscript or a new fact. What follows says where each argument comes from, so that the checking and arrangement done here can be told from the scholarship it rests on.

The verdict is not new. That both boys most probably died in 1483 in Richard III’s custody is the position of Ross (1981), Horrox (1989), Pollard (1991), Hicks (2003, 2024), Cunningham (2003), Horspool (2015) and Skidmore (2017). Re-reading the sources behind it found it to stand.

The regnal-year argument is Randolph Jones’s and Chris Phillips’s. Jones (2014, 2021, 2023) worked it out from the Irish exchequer abstracts and the Butler patent; Phillips (2024, pp. 9–10) restated it. It was checked here against the printed records and against Smith’s 1996 dating of the patent. The Lille receipt’s printed history and impossible chronology are Phillips’s too (2024): Finot abstracted it in 1895, Bletzer quoted it in 2013, and its “June 1487” purchase cannot fit a force that sailed by early May. Finot’s abstract was read for this page and its French is quoted above.

The identification of the 1490s claimant as Piers Werbecque of Tournai is Gairdner’s (1898), with Wroe (2003) on Setúbal. The age discrepancy in the 1493 letter (“nonum fere agens annum”) is Gairdner’s observation (p. 268 n.). The reading of the Gelderland text as propaganda that either hypothesis requires is Hicks’s (2024), anticipated by Enklaar’s question in 1951; that it tracks the 1493 letter and the 1497 confession in outline has been said by Amin and, in the opposite sense, by the Richard III Society’s research pages. The Tyrell and Forest overlap with More is Hicks’s and Thornton’s. Forest’s sons at court, the 1502 itinerary and the Capell chain are Thornton’s (2021, 2024). The argument from Henry VII’s silence is Potter’s (1983) and older.

What is new is arrangement and checking. The three-jurisdiction table for 1487, built on Phillips’s 2024 inventory with Smith (1996) and Jones (2014) added, is arranged to test the survival thesis’s claim that the English records are Tudor propaganda. The Gelderland point is sharpened: the stations in the Arnhem text that are absent from the 1493 letter are exactly the Setúbal and confession stations, in the same order. The murder sources are set out side by side in their original words and classed as rumour, inference or claimed knowledge, with one small textual note: Masselin’s printed text of Rochefort’s speech reads impune trucidari, where trucidatos is sometimes quoted. And the five-cluster ledger with its sensitivity tests shows that the verdict is carried by the events of 1483 and survives the removal of More, the bones and Thornton’s findings together. None of this changes the answer; each part makes an existing argument easier to check.

What was not done. The Gelderland manuscript was not read in full in Dutch, only in Langley’s translation and the quoted extracts; the Setúbal depositions and the Tournai records were read in Gairdner’s and Wroe’s printings, not the originals; Mancini’s Latin was not available; Hicks’s 2024 note was available only in abstract; the Ricardian Bulletin items of 2023–25 were read through intermediaries; Langley’s book itself was read in extracts and reviews, not in full. None of these is likely to change the reading, but each is a place where it could be checked further.

Method and files

  • The evidence ledger (ledger.py) states, for five correlated clusters of evidence, the probability of that evidence under each of five hypotheses (death in 1483; Edward alone survived; Richard alone survived; both survived; something else), combines them from a stated prior, adds a ten per cent allowance for possibilities not represented, and reports how the result changes when any cluster is dropped or softened. Its output is published with it.
  • Five research notes record every text consulted, with the original wording, edition, page and a verified or unverified mark, and list what could not be obtained: Mancini’s Latin, the Gelderland Dutch in full, the Setúbal depositions, the full texts of Hicks (2024) and of the Ricardian Bulletin notes of 2023–25.

This page, the five research notes, the ledger script and its output are published together. Everything here is a reading of published and digitised evidence; nothing was seen in manuscript, and every unverified item is marked as such in the notes.
