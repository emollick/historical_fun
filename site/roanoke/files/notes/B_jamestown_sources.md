# Jamestown-era and later documentary testimony on the fate of the 1587 Roanoke colonists (1607–1625, with the later notices of 1651–1888)

Compiled from complete texts downloaded into `sources/` (inventory in §16). Every quotation below was copied from one of those files; nothing is quoted from memory.

## 0. Conventions

- **VERIFIED** = I read the words in the edition/transcription named, whose complete text is in `sources/`. **UNVERIFIED** = taken from a secondary summary or from a work I could not obtain; the words are given only as the secondary source gives them and are flagged.
- Spelling, capitals and punctuation are those of the edition cited. Long-s (ſ) in the EEBO-TCP transcriptions and in the 1617/1626 Purchas OCR is printed as s; u/v and i/j are left as printed. `[?]` = a letter the TCP transcribers could not read; `[...]` = my omission; other square brackets are editorial (Arber's, Kingsbury's, Major's, Barbour's or mine, as stated).
- "TCP" = EEBO Text Creation Partnership XML transcription (original pagination and marginal notes preserved; margins shown here as *[margin: ...]*). "OCR" = archive.org djvu text; the page numbers I give for OCR editions were established from the running heads in the OCR, not guessed.
- Scenario letters used throughout: **(a)** assimilated at Croatoan/Hatteras; **(b)** moved inland to the Chowan/Roanoke-river country and dispersed; **(c)** some group slaughtered by Powhatan c. 1607; **(d)** tried to sail home; **(e)** went to the Chesapeake (Quinn's thesis).
- Order of documents: chronological by composition, 1607/8 → 1888. Items that turned up in the reading (Hakluyt's 15 April 1609 dedication naming Ritanoe; the Council's *True and Sincere Declaration* of Dec. 1609; Hamor 1615 on Ocanahoen; the Virginia Company records of 1619–1622 on Roanoke; Yeardley 1654) are included in their places.

### 1.1 Opechancanough's report (Dec. 1607) — TCP image 12; Arber I p. 17 — VERIFIED (TCP)

> "the King too[ke] great delight in vnderstanding the manner of our ships and sayling the seas, the earth & skies and of our God: what he knew of the dominious he spared not to acquaint me with, as of certaine men cloathed at a place called Ocanahonan, cloathed like me, the course of our riuer, and that within 4 or 5 daies iourney of the falles, was a great turning of salt water: I desired he would send a messenger to Paspahegh, with a letter I would write, by which they shold vnderstand, how kindly they vsed me, and that I was well least they should reuenge my death: [...] their cruell mindes towards the fort I had deuerted, in describing the ordinance & the mines in the fields, as also the reuenge Captain Newport would take of them at his returne, their intent, I incerted the fort, the people of Ocanahonum and the back sea, this report they after found diuers Indians that c[?]nfirmed"

Arber I p. 17 prints the key clause "as of certaine men cloathed at a place called Ocanahonan, cloathed like me: the course of our riuer" (VERIFIED, OCR line 10909).

Barbour's textual note (Complete Works I, note w to ll. 15-16) — VERIFIED in the Virtual Jamestown extract: "'Ocanahonan'; in margin, '[Oc]onahawan'; and in ll. 26-27, below, 'Ocanahonum.' The phrase 'as of certaine men cloathed at a place called Ocanahonan' has been corrected to read, 'as of certaine men at a place 6 dayes jorny beyond Ocanahonan.' See the Generall Historie, 110: 'five daies journey from us'". Barbour 1975 (p. 4, VERIFIED) says the correction is "in pen and ink by an anonymous annotator" in a copy of the 1608 quarto. Barbour n. 111 (VERIFIED): "Ocanahonan (Ocanahowan) seems to have been a Mangoak (non-Powhatan) town near the modern Virginia-North Carolina boundary, west of the Chowan River".

*Informant:* Opechancanough, "the King" of Pamunkey, while Smith was his prisoner (c. 16–29 Dec. 1607), before Smith was carried to Powhatan. *Implications:* the earliest datable Jamestown intelligence of Europeans ("cloathed like me") in the interior, at a place called Ocanahonan; nothing about a killing. The contemporary manuscript correction moves the clothed men "6 dayes jorny beyond Ocanahonan". Supports (b); says nothing for or against (c); irrelevant to (a)/(d)/(e) except that the direction is inland/south.

### 1.2 Powhatan's report (c. 30 Dec. 1607 – 1 Jan. 1608, Werowocomoco) — TCP image 13; Arber I p. 20 — VERIFIED (TCP and Arber)

> "Beyond them he described people with short Coates, and Sleeues to the Elbowes, that passed that way in Shippes like ours. Many Kingdomes hee described mee to the heade of the Bay, which seemed to bee a mightie Riuer, issuing from mightie Mountaines betwixt the two Seas, the people cloathed at Ocamahowan. He also confirmed, and the Southerly Countries also, as the rest, that reported vs to be within a day & a halfe of Mangoge, two dayes of Chawwonock, 6. from Roo[?]ock, to the south part of the backe sea: he described a countrie called Anone, where they haue abundance of Brasse, and houses walled as outs. I required his discourse, seeing what pride hee had in his great and spacious Dominions, seeing that all hee knewe were vnder his Territories."

Arber I p. 20 repunctuates: "The people cloathed at Ocamahowan, he also confirmed; and the Southerly Countries also, as the rest that reported vs to be within a day and a halfe of Mangoge, two dayes of Chawwonock, 6. from Roonock, to the south part of the backe sea: He described a countrie called Anone, where they haue abundance of Brasse, and houses walled as ours." (VERIFIED, OCR lines 11119-11124; Arber reads "Roonock", "ours", "requited"). Barbour n. 134 (VERIFIED): "see Strachey, Historie, 56-57, for a parallel, with 'Anoeg' for Smith's 'Anone'".

*Informant:* Powhatan (Wahunsenacawh) himself; "the rest" = Opechancanough and "an Indian which had been prisoner to Powhatan" (Smith's own words as quoted by Brown, Genesis I p. 186). *Implications:* Powhatan "confirmed" clothed people at Ocamahowan and located Mangoge, Chawwonock and "Roonock" to the south, and a brass-rich "Anone" with "houses walled as ours" (cf. Strachey's Anoeg "whose howses are built as ours", §5.4, and the stone-walled houses at Peccarecamek/Ochanahoen "so taught them by those Englishe", §5.2). Powhatan volunteers this in Dec. 1607/Jan. 1608 without any hint of a massacre — which is either evidence against (c) as of that date, or (if the Council of 1609 and Strachey are right that the killing happened "vppon the first arrivall of our Colonie") evidence that he was pointing the English away from his own doings. Supports (b). The short-coated people "in Shippes like ours" beyond the head of the Bay are a different report (northern/European ships), not Roanoke.

### 1.3 The Paspahegh king and Panawicke (Jan.–Feb. 1608) — TCP image 15; Arber I p. 23 — VERIFIED (TCP and Arber)

> "We had agreed with y<sup>t</sup> king of Paspahegh to conduct two of our men to a place called Panawicke beyond R[?]onok, where he reported many men to be apparelled. Wee lended him at Warraskoyack, where playing the villaine, and deluding vs for rewards, returned within three or foure dayes after without going further."

Arber I p. 23 reads "Panawicke beyond Roonok" and "Wee landed him at Warraskoyack, where [he] playing the villaine" (VERIFIED, OCR line 11316 for the first clause; the bracket is Arber's). Barbour n. 154 (VERIFIED): "Panawick ('Panawaioc,' etc.) appears on Theodore de Bry's map of North Carolina (based on John White's map), but its location is uncertain (Quinn, Roanoke Voyages, II, 849, 872). The incident is not mentioned in the Proceedings or the Generall Historie, though there is reference to it in the Smith/Zúñiga map (Barbour, Jamestown Voyages, I, 240). Strachey gives further information (Historie, 34). Note that some such phrase as 'like me' is missing at the end of the sentence."

*Informant:* Wowinchopunck, weroance of Paspahegh (the tribe on whose land Jamestown stood). *Implications:* a second, independent Powhatan-confederacy report of "many men ... apparelled" at a named place "beyond Roonok", i.e. south of Roanoke; the guide pocketed the reward and turned back; the Zúñiga chart (§2) records both the landing and that he "reported our men to be and went to se[e]". Supports (b) (Panawiock is placed by Barbour between the Pamlico and the Roanoke, by the First Colony Foundation near the Cashie River or Salmon Creek in Bertie County) and is at least compatible with (a) if "beyond Roonok" is read loosely.

## 2. The "Zúñiga chart" (drawn at Jamestown, sent to England June 1608) and Don Pedro de Zúñiga's covering letter (Highgate, 10 Sept. 1608)

Encyclopedia Virginia: "likely Smith's" and "written on four sheets of paper adhered together"; Barbour calls it "the Smith/Zuniga map"; Brown: "This chart must have been sent to England by Captain Francis Nelson, who left Virginia June 2, 1608 ... It illustrates Captain John Smith's 'True Relation' (LIV.), and was sent from Virginia with it."

**Editions used.** Alexander Brown, *The Genesis of the United States* (1890) I, no. LVI (letter, pp. 183-184) and no. LVII "Chart of Virginia" (pp. 184-190) — `sources/brown_genesis_united_states_vol1_1890_djvu.txt` (https://archive.org/download/genesisofuniteds0001alex/genesisofuniteds0001alex_djvu.txt); Philip L. ### 2.1 Zúñiga to Philip III, 10 Sept. 1608 — Brown I pp. 183-184 (Brown's English translation) — VERIFIED

> "SIRE: — I have thought proper to send Y. ### 2.3 Brown's commentary (I pp. 185-186, 190) — VERIFIED

> "[Mem. — The three rivers given on the chart, south of the James, were probably intended for the Neuse, the Tar, and the Roanoke rivers. Ocanahowan was probably supposed to be on the Neuse.]" — on the Paspahegh legend: "This is possibly in the present Sampson County, North Carolina." — on the landing: "[Mem. — The landing from the chart was probably in Pagan Creek, Isle of Wight County.]" — and (p. 190, after quoting McMillan 1888): "It will be noted that the Croatan tradition is not at variance with the chart, from which it seems the Indians and lost colonists went from Roanoke westward up the present Roanoke River to Ocanohowan, and from thence to Pakrakanick (or Peccarecamek, Strachey), probably on the Neuse River, near Sampson County, where it seems they were reported to be in 1608."

Brown also notes (p. 186) that Smith "does not mention this incident [the Panawicke attempt] at this time in his General History; but in referring to an expedition of December, 1608, under his own command, he says, that he sent from Warraskoyack, Master Sicklemore and two guides 'to seeke for the lost company of Sir Walter Raleigh's.'"

### 2.4 Barbour's placing of the names (1975, pp. 4-5, 10-13) — VERIFIED

> "It shows Ocanahowan on the south side of a river which must be the Roanoke some distance above its confluence with the river now called the Chowan." — "between Lane's comments and the Smith/Zuniga map it can readily be deduced that Ocahanowan was a Mangoak town" — "we shall perhaps not be far wrong if we place Ocanahowan west of the Chowan River, and west of the Algonquian tribes of the coastal region, but within the territory occupied by Iroquoians." — "it is possible that Hocomowananck is identifiable with Ocanahowan (John Lawson's Unanauhan, a Tuscarora village)" — "Hocomowananck was almost certainly at or near modern Roanoke Rapids, North Carolina" — "Tentatively we can place it at or near Hocomowananck."

*Informants behind the chart:* the same as §1 — Opechancanough/Powhatan (the four clothed men who "came from Roonock to Ocanahowan") and Wowinchopunck (the landing and the "went to see"). The chart adds what the *True Relation* text does not: the **number four**, and the direction of movement "from Roonock to Ocanahowan". *Implications:* the number "4 men" reappears in the Council's instructions of May 1609 ("foure of the englishe aliue", §4) and in Strachey ("fower men, two boyes, and one yonge mayde", §5.2): the 1608 chart is the root of that tradition. Strong support for (b); nothing about a slaughter; it locates the survivors well south of Powhatan's country, which tells against (e) as the survivors' final position and is neutral on (a) except that Roonock is treated as the point of departure.

## 3. George Percy, *Observations gathered out of a Discourse of the Plantation of the Southerne Colonie in Virginia by the English, 1606* (written 1607-08; printed, abridged, by Purchas 1625)

**Editions used.** Purchas, *Hakluytus Posthumus or Purchas his Pilgrimes* (1625) part IV, lib. 9 cap. 2, p. 1689 — TCP A71306 `sources/purchas_pilgrimes_1625_part4_TCP_A71306.txt` (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/textcreationpartnership/A71306/master/A71306.xml) and OCR `sources/purchas_pilgrimes_1625_part4_djvu.txt`; MacLehose (Glasgow) reprint vol. ### 3.1 The yellow-haired boy (May 1607) — Purchas 1625 IV p. 1689; Glasgow XVIII p. 413 — VERIFIED (TCP and Glasgow)

> "At Port Cotage in our Voyage vp the Riuer, we saw a Sauage Boy about the age of ten yeeres, *[margin: Yellow haired Virginian.]* which had a head of haire of a perfect yellow and a reasonable white skinne, which is a Miracle amongst all Sauages."

*Informant:* Percy's own eyes, during the first exploration up the James (the passage sits in his account of the voyage with Newport in May 1607; "Port Cotage" is not identified by Purchas). *Implications:* a child born c. 1597 with European colouring on the James River. It is compatible with (e) (Englishmen living among the Chesapeake/James tribes before 1607) but equally with earlier European contact (the Spanish Ajacán mission 1570-72, Lane's men on the Chesapeake 1585-86, or unrecorded visits); Percy makes no link to Roanoke. It is the only physical-appearance evidence in the Jamestown corpus, and Strachey never mentions it. Weak on its own.

## 4. The Council of the Virginia Company, *Instruccons orders and constitucons ... to Sir Thomas Gates knight Governor of Virginia* (London, May 1609)

**What it is.** The Company's instructions to the first Lieutenant-Governor under the second charter; Gates sailed 2 June 1609 in the *Sea Venture*, was wrecked on Bermuda, and the instructions reached Virginia only in May 1610. Manuscript: Bodleian Library, Ashmolean MS 1147 ff. 175-190a. **Edition used.** Susan M. Kingsbury (ed.), *The Records of the Virginia Company of London* III (Washington 1933), document V, pp. 12-24 — `sources/kingsbury_records_virginia_company_vol3_1933_djvu.txt` (https://archive.org/download/recordsofvirgini03virg/recordsofvirgini03virg_djvu.txt; page numbers confirmed by the running heads "MAY, 1609 15" and "MAY, 1609 17"); a companion extract of §§15-16 is `sources/kingsbury_vol3_1609_instructions_extract.txt`; Barbour 1975 p. 7 prints §15 in a lightly modernised form (VERIFIED, and used to control the OCR). Kingsbury's sigla are kept: "§ ... §" encloses words she marks as interlined or altered in the MS (her apparatus not re-read: the meaning of the sigla is UNVERIFIED), "[178]", "[181]" are folio numbers; the OCR loses her superscript abbreviations (yo<sup>r</sup> = your, w<sup>ch</sup> = which, p̄ = pro/pre etc.).

### 4.1 §7 — the priests — pp. 14-15 — VERIFIED (OCR, page confirmed)

> "7 You shall, with all propenseues and diligence, endeavour the conversion of the natiues to the knowledge and worship of the true §god§ and their redeemer Christ Jesus, as the most pious and noble end of this [178] plantacon, wch the better to effect you must procure from them some convenient nomber of their Children to be brought vp in yor language, and manners, and if you finde it convenient, we thinke it reasonable you first remoue from them their Iniocasockes or Priestes by a surprise of them all and detayninge them prisoners, for they are so wrapped vp in the fogge and miserie of their iniquity, and so tirrified with their continuall tirrany Chayned vnder the bond of Deathe vnto the Divell that while they liue amounge them to poyson and infecte them their mindes, you shall neuer make any greate progres into this glorious worke, nor haue any Civill peace or concurre with them. And in case of necessity, [p. 15] or conveniency, we pronounce it not crueltie nor breache of Charity to deale more sharpely with them and to proceede even to dea[th]e with these murtherers of Soules and sacrificers of gods images to the Divill referringe the consideracon of this as a waighty matter of important consequence to the Circumstances of the busines and place in yor discrecon"

(Kingsbury's side-heads on p. 14: "Conversion of Virginians", "Education of children", "Iniocasock preestes".)

### 4.2 §15 — Ohonahorn, Ritanoc, Peccarecamicke and the four Englishmen — p. 17 — VERIFIED (OCR, page confirmed; controlled against Barbour 1975)

> "15 Foure dayes Journey from yor forte Southewards is a towne called Ohonahorn seated where the Riuer of Choanocki devideth it self into three braunches and falleth into the sea of Rawnocke in thirtie fiue degrees this place if you seeke by Indian guides from James forte to Winocke by water, from thence to Manqueocke, some twenty miles from thence to Caththega, as much and from thence to Oconahoen you shall finde a braue and [181] fruiteful seate euery way vnaccessable by a straunger enemy, much more abundant in Pochon and in the grasse silke called Cour del Cherva and in vines, then any parte of this land knowne vnto vs Here we suppose, if you make yor principall and cheife seate, you shall doe most safely and Richely because you are in the part of §the§ land inclined to the southe, and two of the best riuers will supply you, besides you are neere to Riche Copper mines of Ritanoc and may passe them by one braunche of this Riuer, and by another Peccarecamicke where you shall finde foure of the englishe aliue, left by Sr Walter Rawely wch escaped from the slaughter of Powhaton of Roanocke, vppon the first arrivall of our Colonie, and liue vnder the proteccon of a wiroane called Gepanocon enemy to Powhaton, by whose consent you shall neuer recouer them, one of these were worth much labour, and if you finde them not, yet seach into this Countrey it is more probable then towards the north."

Kingsbury's side-heads (OCR garbled): "Ohonahoen, chief [seat]", "Rawnocke", "Winocke", "Oconahoen", "Pochon", "Ritanoc", "Peccarecamicke", "Powhaton", "Gepanocon a wi[roance]". Barbour reads the third station as "Gaththega" (OCR "Caththega"); he glosses "in thirtie five degrees [correctly, 36° N lat.]". §16 begins: "These three habitacons seeme enoughe for the nomber of the people nowe transported".

*Informant:* the Council in London. Its sources can only have been (i) Newport, who reached England in Jan. 1609 with Smith's letters and the *Map of Virginia* material, and (ii) the two Powhatan Indians he brought, Namontack and Machumps (see §4a and §5.2). *Implications:* this is the **first written statement of a Powhatan slaughter of the Roanoke colonists**, dated May 1609 — a year before Strachey set foot in Virginia. It is explicit for (c) ("the slaughter of Powhaton of Roanocke, vppon the first arrivall of our Colonie" — i.e. c. April-May 1607) and equally explicit for (b) (four survivors at Peccarecamicke under Gepanocon, "enemy to Powhaton", with copper mines at Ritanoc). The closing "it is more probable then towards the north" shows the Council thought the survivors lay south of the James, not in the Chesapeake, which weighs against (e) as a *surviving* location. Grammar: "the slaughter of Powhaton of Roanocke" reads most naturally as "the slaughter made by Powhatan of [the colony of] Roanoke"; it is not a statement that the killing took place on Roanoke Island. The order to seize the "Iniocasockes or Priestes" (§7) is justified in the text by conversion, not by the slaughter; the connection between priests and slaughter is made only by Strachey (§5.7).

## 4a. Richard Hakluyt, Epistle Dedicatorie to *Virginia richly valued* (dated Westminster, 15 April 1609; printed London 1609)

**Editions used.** The companion file `sources/hakluyt1609_virginia_richly_valued_dedication.txt`, which contains (a) W. B. Rye's Hakluyt Society reprint (1851) pp. 1-7 and (b) the OCR of the 1609 original (John Carter Brown copy); both from archive.org (URLs in the file header). Both OCRs garble the italic type of the passage; the wording below is what the two readings agree on, with the 1609 OCR's letter-forms corrected (its raw line is: "Befides, our owne Indians hane lately renealed either this or another rich mine of copperor gold in atowne called Ritanoe, neere certaine mountaines hing Weft of Reanoat."). VERIFIED as to substance and the name Ritanoe; individual garbled letters reconstructed.

> "But what neede I stand vpon forren testimonies, since Master Thomas Heriot, a man of much iudgement in these causes, signified vnto you all, at your late solemne meeting at the house of the right honourable the Earle of Exeter, how to the Southwest of our old fort in Virginia, the Indians often informed him, that there was a great melting of red mettall, reporting the manner in working of the same. Besides, our owne Indians haue lately reuealed either this or another rich mine of copper or gold in a towne called Ritanoe, neere certaine mountaines lying West of Roanoac."

*Informant:* "our owne Indians" = the Powhatan men then in London, Namontack and Machumps (in England from Jan. 1609); Harriot's testimony at a Company meeting at Exeter House. *Implications:* **Ritanoe was already known in London as a copper/gold town "West of Roanoac" a month before the Gates instructions** and three years before Strachey wrote. It supports the reading that the whole Ritanoe/Eyanoco story came from Machumps in London in the winter of 1608-09 (so Strachey, §5.2: "by the relation of Machumps"), and that the Council's §15 and Strachey's p. 26 are two redactions of the same informant's report, not independent testimonies. Hakluyt puts Ritanoe "neere certaine mountaines lying West of Roanoac"; the Council reaches it "by one braunche of this Riuer" (the Chowan/Roanoke system). Neutral on (a)-(e) except as a marker of copper-country (b).

## 4b. The Council of Virginia, *A True and Sincere declaration of the purpose and ends of the Plantation begun in Virginia* (London, Dec. 1609; printed 1610)

**Edition used.** Brown, *Genesis* I, no. CXIV, pp. 337-353; the passage is on p. 349 (running head "A TRUE AND SINCERE DECLARATION. 349") — VERIFIED (OCR).

> "in the intelligence of some of our Nation planted by Sir Walter Raleigh, yet a live, within fifty mile of our fort, who can open the womb and bowels of this country; as is testified by two of our colony sent out to seek them, who, (though denied by the savages speech with them) found crosses and Letters the Characters and assured Testimonies of Christians newly cut in the barks of trees:"

*Informant:* the Council, writing after the return of the 1609 ships (autumn 1609), on the strength of letters from Virginia; "two of our colony sent out to seek them" can only be Sicklemore (to Chowanoke, Dec. 1608–Jan. 1609) and/or Nathaniel Powell and Anas Todkill (to the Mangoags, 1609) — see §6.3, §7.5. *Implications:* the only Jamestown-era claim of physical evidence of survivors ("crosses and Letters ... newly cut in the barks of trees"), and a claim that the savages "denied ... speech with them". "Within fifty mile of our fort" points to the Nottoway/Meherrin/Chowan zone — (b). The Company had every motive, after the loss of the *Sea Venture*, to print the most hopeful gloss. CCXVII) reprints the p. 26 passage with its side-note. **Not reached:** L. B. Wright and V. ### 5.1 *Praemonition to the Reader*, p. 20 — VERIFIED

> "and thus we will commune and entreate with them, truck, and barter, our commodityes for theires, and theires for ours (of which they seeme more faine) in all love and freindship, untill, for our good purposes towards them, we shall finde them practize violence or treason against us (as they have done to our other colony at Roanoak) : when then, I would gladly knowe (of such who presume to knowe all things), whether we maye stand upon our owne innocency or no, or hold yt a scruple in humanitye, or any breach of charity (to prevent our owne throats from the cutting), to drawe our swordes, et vim vi repellere ?"

*Informant:* Strachey's own argument (no source named). *Implication:* "treason ... to our other colony at Roanoak" — the Roanoke colony is treated as a known case of Indian treachery; "Roanoak" here is the colony's name, not necessarily the island. (c).

### 5.2 Book I cap. 2, p. 26 — the stone houses, Machumps, Ritanoe, Eyanoco — VERIFIED (BSB OCR; Brown's transcription agrees)

> "This high land is, in all likelyhoodes, a pleasant tract, and the mowld fruictfull, especially what may lye to the so-ward ; where, at Peccarecamek and Ochanahoen, by the relation of Machumps,<sup>1</sup> the people have howses built with stone walles, and one story above another, so taught them by those Englishe whoe escaped the slaughter at Roanoak, at what tyme this our colony, under the conduct of Capt. Newport,<sup>2</sup> landed within the Chesapeake Bay, where the people breed up tame turkeis about their howses, and take apes in the mountaines, and where, at Ritanoe, the Weroance Eyanoco preserved seven of the English alive—fower men, two boyes, and one yonge mayde (who escaped and fled up the river of Chanoke), to beat his copper, of which he hath certaine mynes at the said Ritanoe, as also at Pamawauk are said to be store of salt stones."

Major's notes: "<sup>1</sup> An Indian subsequently mentioned." "<sup>2</sup> In 1607, Captain Newport sailed in command of a squadron of three vessels, with one hundred and ten settlers, and reached Chesapeake Bay ..." (VERIFIED). Side-note of the Sloane MS as printed by Major and transcribed by Brown (I p. 185): "Howses of stone, tame Turkyes, and Monkyes, supposed at Peccartcanick." (VERIFIED in Brown; the BSB OCR of the margin reads "Howses of ... turkyes and ... yes, suppose at Peccart-canick", consistent).

Princeton-MS text as printed by Wright & Freund p. 34 and quoted by Barbour 1975 p. 8 — UNVERIFIED against Wright & Freund, VERIFIED as Barbour's quotation: "This highland is in all likelyhoodes a pleasant Tract, and the Mould fruictfull, especially what may lye to the Southward, where at Peccarecanicke and Ochanahoen, by the Relation of Machumps [brother of one of Powhatan's wives], the People have howses built with stone walls, and one story above another, so taught them by those English who ascaped the slaughter at Roanoak, at what tyme this our Colony under the Conduct of Captain Newport) landed within the Chesapeack Bay, and where the people breed up tame Turkeis about their howses, and take Apes in the Mountayns, and where at Ritanoe, the Weroance Eyanoco [cf. the instructions to Gates, above] preserved 7. of the English alive, fower men, twoo Boyes, and one young Maid, who escaped and fled up the River of Chaonoke, to beat his Copper, of which he hath certayn Mynes at the said Ritanoe, as also at Pannawaick are said to be store of salt stones." Its side-notes (Barbour, same page): "Peccarecanick between 35. and 36. degrees of the Line: and in her warme Vallyes may be planted, Sugar-Canes, Oranges, Lemons, and all sortes of Southren fruites, and whatsoever, the South-Spaine, Italy, Barbary, Greece, Judea, and Syria bring forth being answerable to the same height." and "Likewise Silk of the grass: and habundaunce of the dying Roote." — note that these side-notes differ entirely from the Sloane side-note printed by Major: an MS variant worth checking in Wright & Freund.

*Informant:* Machumps — named by Strachey here and only here as the source. Strachey describes him at p. 54 (§5.6) as an Indian "who was sometyme in England" — he went to England with Newport in Dec. 1608 and the First Colony Foundation states (its article, VERIFIED as its statement) that "During the winter of 1609 Machumps spoke with William Strachey ... in London"; he returned to Virginia in the Gates fleet of 1609-10. *Implications:* (c) — a "slaughter at Roanoak" dated to Newport's landing (April-May 1607); (b) — survivors ("those Englishe whoe escaped") teaching stone building at Peccarecamek and Ochanahoen, and seven (4 men, 2 boys, 1 girl) at Ritanoe under Eyanoco beating copper, having "fled up the river of Chanoke" (Chowan). The two boys and a girl imply children born in the colony (cf. Purchas's "some children were borne to them there", §8.6). Note that the Council's §15 has *four* at Peccarecamicke under Gepanocon, Strachey *seven* at Ritanoe under Eyanoco: the same informant's report (see §4a) filtered twice, or two reports conflated. "Pamawauk"/"Pannawaick" with "store of salt stones" is the Panawicke/Panawiock of Smith and the chart. Neither Sloane nor (per Barbour) Princeton says where "Roanoak" was.

### 5.3 Book I cap. 2, pp. 27-28 — South Virginia and Roanoke not under Powhatan — VERIFIED

Side-note (foot of p. 27): "Ronoak not under the commaund of Powhatan." Text (p. 28):

> "South Virginia is a very low, sandy soyle, without rocks, or any stones at all; yt is thick sett with woodes of divers kindes, and in all things resembleth North Virginia, excepted the lownesse of the land and want of stones; yt hath divers rivers in yt, but none navigable to our knowledge; yt hath many islands, which lie into the sea before the firme land, but the water is not deepe for shippinge betweene them and the mayne. Yt is said to have of the same silke whereof the Chynoes make their damaske, called by the Portugalls sone del cherua, in great aboundaunce, and sondry apothecary druggs, which are nowe found likewise as frequent in our north parte; it is a fruitfull countrey, and not much subject to cold ; in this country it was that Sir Walter Raleigh planted his two colonies, in the islande aforesaid, called Roanoack. No parte of this sowth country is supposed to be under Powhatan, but under an absolute Weroance, as powerfull and great as Powhatan."

*Implications:* Strachey's "Roanoack" is the island, in a South Virginia "not ... under Powhatan"; he therefore did not think Powhatan ruled Roanoke — the slaughter he alleges (§5.7) is of people "out of his territory". ("sone del cherua" = the Council's "Cour del Cherva", the silk-grass: the two texts share a source.)

### 5.4 Book I cap. 5, pp. 48-49 — Powhatan's bounds: the Mangoages, Chawonoaks, Anoeg, and Weinock — VERIFIED

> "The greatnes and boundes of whose empire, by reason of his powerfulnes and ambition in his youth, hath larger lymitts then ever had any of his predicessors in former tymes, for he seemes to comaund south and north from the Mangoages and Chawonoaks bordering upon Roanoake, and the Old Virginia, to Tockwogh, a towne pallisadode, standing at the north end of the bay, in forty degrees or thereabouts; south-west to Anoeg (not expressed in the mappe), whose howses are built as ours, ten daies distant from us, from whence those Weroances sent unto him of their comodityes ; as Weinock, a servant, in whom Powhatan reposed much trust, would tell our elder planters, and could repeat many wordes of their language he had learned among them in his ymployment thither for his kinge, and whence he often returned, full of presents, to Powhatan, west to Monahassanugh, ..."

*Informant:* "Weinock, a servant" of Powhatan who had travelled to Anoeg, as reported by "our elder planters" (the 1607 men, i.e. Smith's circle). *Implications:* Anoeg = Smith's Anone ("houses walled as ours", §1.2); Strachey again puts Powhatan's southern frontier at "the Mangoages and Chawonoaks bordering upon Roanoake". If the Anoeg/Anone houses "built as ours" are English-taught, this is (b) again; but Strachey does not say so here.

### 5.5 Book I cap. 5, p. 50 — "the same cuppe ... at Ronoak" — VERIFIED

> "he doth often send unto us to temporize with us, awayting perhapps a fit opportunity (inflamed by his furious and bloudy priests) to offer us a tast of the same cuppe which he made our poore countrymen drinck of at Ronoak,<sup>1</sup> not yet seeming willing to hold any open quarrell or hostility with us;"

Major's note 1: "The colony planted by Sir Walter Raleigh, which Powhatan destroyed." (VERIFIED). *Implication:* (c); priests again the instigators; "at Ronoak" = the colony, unlocated.

### 5.6 Book I cap. 6, p. 54 — who Machumps was — VERIFIED

> "He was reported by the said Kemps, as also by the Indian Machumps, who was sometyme in England, and comes to and fro amongst us as he dares, and as Powhatan gives him leave, for yt is not otherwise safe for him, no more then yt was for one Amarice, who had his braynes knockt out for selling but a baskett of corne, and lying in the English fort two or three daies without Powhatan's leave; I say they often reported unto us that Powhatan had then lyving twenty sonnes and ten daughters, besyde a young one by Winganuske, ..."

(Side-notes: "His ..." / "Twenty ... daughters.") *Implication:* Machumps was a Powhatan insider with limited freedom to talk to the English; Strachey's information from him was gathered "amongst us" in Virginia (1610-11) as well as, per FCF, in London (1609). The Barbour gloss "brother of one of Powhatan's wives" (Winganuske) is Barbour's, not Strachey's here.

### 5.7 Book I cap. 9, pp. 85-86 — the slaughter, "twenty and od yeares", the priests — VERIFIED

> "declaring (in the attempt thereof) unto the several weroances, and making the comon people likewise to understand, how that his majestie hath bene acquainted, that the men, women, and childrene of the first plantation at Roanoak were by practize and comaundement of Powhatan (he himself perswaded therunto by his priests) miserably slaughtered, without any offence given him either by the first planted (who twenty and od yeares had peaceably lyved intermixt with those salvages, and were out of his territory) or by those who nowe are come to inhabite some parte of his desarte lands, and to trade with him for some comodityes of ours, which he and his people stand in want of; notwithstanding, because his majestie is, of all the world, the most just and the most mercifull prince, he hath given order that Powhatan himself, with the weroances and all the people, shalbe spared, and revenge only taken upon his Quiyoughquisocks, by whose advise and perswasions was exercised that bloudy cruelty, and only how that Powhatan himself and the weroances must depend on his majestie, both acknowledging him for their superiall lord ; [...] the true God, and his governour king James, comaunding that the childrene of men be preserved and not slaughtered without offence given, as the devill and his Quiyoughquisocks have ordeyned. Against which Sathanicall invention, maye yt please his majestie to make an ordinaunce, that the fathers of those childrene, and all that consent unto the sacrifices hereafter, shalbe put to death as traytors to God and his majestie."

*Informant:* none named. The framing — "his majestie hath bene acquainted ... he hath given order ... revenge only taken upon his Quiyoughquisocks" — is a policy statement matching the Council's instruction to seize the priests (§4.1) and its slaughter statement (§4.2); Strachey was in London when both were drafted (May 1609) and almost certainly writes from Company doctrine, not from a Virginia informant. *Implications:* (c) in its fullest form: "the men, women, and childrene of the first plantation at Roanoak" (note: "first plantation" — Strachey elsewhere counts Raleigh's "two colonies"; here the 1587 colony is meant, since Lane's men left alive) killed "by practize and comaundement of Powhatan", at the priests' persuasion. **"who twenty and od yeares had peaceably lyved intermixt with those salvages, and were out of his territory"** — 1587 + "twenty and od" = c. 1607-1610; read with p. 26 ("at what tyme this our colony ... landed") and Kingsbury §15 ("vppon the first arrivall of our Colonie"), it dates the killing to c. 1607 and describes the victims as having lived *intermixed with Indians* (assimilation — (a)/(b)/(e)) and *outside Powhatan's territory* — which on Strachey's own geography (§5.3, §5.4) means south of the Mangoags/Chowanokes line, not on the Chesapeake. Quinn's (e) requires reading "out of his territory" as the Chesapeake tribe's land (which Strachey does treat as a "province" Powhatan destroyed, §5.9), but Strachey never says the Roanoke people were among the Chesapeakes.

### 5.8 Book I cap. 7, p. 94 — Machumps at Sir Thomas Dale's table — VERIFIED

> "I have heard Machumps, at Sir Thos. Dale's table, once or twice (upon our request) repeat the said grace as yt were, howbeit I forgot to take yt from him in writinge."

*Implication:* fixes Strachey's personal acquaintance with Machumps in Virginia in 1611 (Dale arrived May 1611).

### 5.9 Book I cap. 8, p. 101 — the prophecy and the extinction of the Chesapeakes — VERIFIED

> "[It is] not long since that his priests told him how that from the Chesapeack Bay a nation should arise which should dissolve and give end to his empire, for which, not many yeares since (perplext with this divelish oracle, and divers understanding thereof), according to the ancyent and gentile customs, he destroyed and put to sword all such who might lye under any doubtful construccion of the said prophesie, as all the inhabitants, the weroance and his subjects of that province, and so remaine all the Chessiopeians at this daye, and for this cause, extinct. Some of the inhabitants, againe, have not spared to give us to understand, how they have a second prophesie likewise amongst them, that twice they should give overthrow and dishearten the attempters, and such straungers as should invade their territories or labour to settle a plantation among them, but the third tyme they themselves should fall into their subjection, and under their conquest ;"

*Informant:* "Some of the inhabitants" (unnamed Indians). *Implications:* this is the passage on which Quinn built (e): the Chesapeake tribe "extinct", killed "not many yeares since" for a prophecy about a nation arising "from the Chesapeack Bay". Strachey does **not** connect this to the Roanoke colonists in the text; the connection (that the English living with the Chesapeakes were the "nation" foreseen) is an inference. The "second prophesie" ("twice they should give overthrow ... to such straungers as should ... settle a plantation") is a separate tradition in which the two overthrown attempts could be read as Roanoke and Jamestown.

### 5.10 Book I cap. 8, p. 103 — "slaughtered so many of our nation ... seated far from him" — VERIFIED

> "There is no man among themselves so savage, or not capable of so much sence, but that he will approve our cause, when he shalbe made to understand that Powhatan hath slaughtered so many of our nation without offence given, and such as were seated far from him, and in the territory of those weroances which did in no sort depend on him or acknowledg him ; but yt hath ben Powhatan's great care to keepe us, by all meanes, from the acquaintance of those nations that border and confront him, for besides his knowledge how easely and willingly his enemies wilbe drawne upon him by the least countenance and encouragement from us, he doth, by keeping us from trading with them, monoplize all the copper brought into Virginia by the English."

*Implications:* the clearest statement of *where* Strachey placed the victims: "seated far from him, and in the territory of those weroances which did in no sort depend on him" — i.e. beyond the confederacy, consistent with the Chowan/Roanoke-river country (b)+(c), and hard to square with a Chesapeake location (e), since the Chesapeakes were Powhatan's near neighbours. The copper-monopoly motive ties to the Ritanoe copper mines and Eyanoco's English copper-beaters (§5.2).

### 5.11 Book I cap. 8, p. 105 — the Chesapeake re-peopled — VERIFIED

> "I graunt that such the new inhabitants who now people Chesapeak againe (the old extinguished, as you have heard, upon the conceipt of a prophesie), togither with the weroances of Nandsamund, Warraskoyak, and Weanock, are now at peace with him;"

*Implication:* by 1611 the Chesapeake district had been re-peopled by Powhatan's own people — relevant to any archaeology of the "Chesapeake" site (e).

### 5.12 Book I cap. 10, p. 124 — "in their discoveryes to the Mangoagues" — VERIFIED

> "Lyons I will not posetively affirme that the country hath, since our people never yet saw any; howbeit, in their discoveryes to the Mangoagues, they did light once upon twoo skynns, which, by all the judgements in the fort, were supposed to be lyons' skinnes; and this last yeare, myself being at the falls with Sir Thomas Dale, I found in an Indian howse certaine clawes tyed up in a string, ..."

*Implication:* an incidental confirmation, independent of Smith, that English "discoveryes to the Mangoagues" (Powell and Todkill's journey, §6.3) did take place and brought things back to the fort.

### 5.13 Book II caps. 1-5, pp. 141-153 — Strachey's narrative of the Roanoke voyages — VERIFIED (pp. 152-153)

p. 152 (White's 1590 search): "untill they came to the place where the colony was left 1586. Some tracts of feeting they found, and upon a sandy banck, on a tree, curiously carved, these romaine letters, 'Cro', which gave them hope they might be removed to Croatan, for their agreement was, indeed, to remove when Captaine White left them. Howbeit, Captaine White sought them no further, but missing them there, and his company havinge other practizes, and which those tymes afforded, they returned covetous of some good successe upon the Spanish fleete to returne that yeare from Mexico and the Indies,— neglecting thus these unfortunate and betrayed people, of whose end you shall yet hereafter read in due place in this decade."

p. 153, Caput V, "The unfaithfulnes of such who were imployed miscarried the colony": "Thus Sir W. Raleigh, weried with so great an expence, and abused with the unfaithfulnes of the ymployed, after he had sent (as you maye see by these five severall tymes) collonies and supplies at his owne charges, and nowe at length both himself and his successors thus betrayed, he was even nowe content to submit the fortune of the poore men's lives, and lief of the holy accion ytself, into the favour and proteccion of the God of all mercy, ... By which meanes, for seventeen or eighteen yeares togeather, yt lay neglected, untill yt pleased God at length to move againe the heart of a great and right noble earle amongst us, Earle of [Southampton] ..."

*Implications:* Strachey knew the "Cro"/Croatoan clue (a) and misdates the colony to 1586; "unfortunate and betrayed people, of whose end you shall yet hereafter read in due place in this decade" promises a fuller account of their "end" that the surviving *Historie* never gives — the second "decade" was never written or is lost. "Seventeen or eighteen yeares" of neglect (1590 → 1607/08) is his reckoning of the interval, consistent with "twenty and od yeares" of survival from 1587.

This is the Jamestown text that most directly contradicts Strachey's survivors at Peccarecamek/Ochanahoen/Ritanoe (which Barbour and FCF place in or beside Mangoak country) and the Council's four men at Peccarecamicke; it supports (c) (dead) but says nothing of Powhatan or a slaughter, and it was written by Smith, who in 1612 and 1624 never mentions Powhatan's "confession" (see §8.4). Neither text says the search reached Ocanahowan.

**Edition used.** OCR of the 1888 pamphlet — `sources/mcmillan_sir_walter_raleighs_lost_colony_1888_djvu.txt` (https://archive.org/download/sirwalterraleigh00mcmi/sirwalterraleigh00mcmi_djvu.txt; page numbers from running heads "LOST COLONY. ### 14.1 George Lowrie's speech, 1864 — pp. 16-17 — VERIFIED

> "During the late war between the States, an incident occurred which caused the writer to investigate the traditions of this tribe. Three young men of the Lowrie family were drafted, according to military law, to work on the fortifications at Fort Fisher in Eastern North Carolina, and while on the road to the nearest depot in Robeson County, they were killed, it is supposed, by a white man who had them in custody. An inquest was held, and at its conclusion, an old Indian, named George Lowrie, addressed the people assembled, in substance as follows: 'We have always been the friends of white men. We were a free people long before the white men came to our land. Our tribe was always free. They lived in Roanoke in Virginia. When the English [p. 17] came to Roanoke, our tribe treated them kindly. One of our tribe went to England in an English ship and saw that great country. When English people landed in Roanoke we were friendly, for our tribe was always friendly to white men. We took the English to live with us. There is the white man's blood in these veins as well as that of the Indian. In order to be great like the English, we took the white man's language and religion, for our people were told they would prosper if they would take white men's laws. In the wars between white men and Indians we always fought on the side of white men. We moved to this land and fought for liberty for white men, yet white men have treated us as negroes. Here are our young men shot down by a white man and we get no justice, and that in a land where our people were always free.'"

McMillan adds (p. 17): "The incident above related occurred in the latter part of 1864, and owing to the troubled state of the country at that time, and for several years afterwards, no investigation could be made till the year 1875, when the writer became a citizen and had opportunity of interviewing leading persons of the tribe."

### 14.2 The "summary of traditions" — pp. 17-19 — VERIFIED

> "The tribe once lived in Roanoke in Virginia, as they persist in calling Eastern North Carolina. The name Roanoke is applied to the country around Pamlico Sound, embracing Hyde, Tyrell and Dare counties on the North, with the series of islands as far South as Carteret county and embracing that county with Craven and Jones Croatoan or Croatan was a locality far to the South, off the coast of Carteret, and was the principal seat of the tribe. Their leading man was made Lord of Roanoke. The name [p. 18] Manteo they do not recognize, but are familiar with Mayna, a name very common among them and representing a very quiet, law abiding people. At an early period after the English colony became incorporated with the tribe, they began to emigrate westward. The first settlement made was probably in what is now Sampson county on several small rivers, tributary to Black river. A portion located on the Cape Fear, near a place now bearing the name of 'Indian Wells' and at Heart's Creek in Cumberland county, now Fayetteville. It is impossible to ascertain at what date the tribe located in Robeson but it is probable that they have resided there for 200 years. According to their universal tradition they were located there long before the troubles with the Tuscaroras began in 1711. [...] The name Dare was not recognized by them in our first investigations but we afterwards discovered that they pronounce the name variously as Darr, Durr and Dorr. This discovery was made when we related to an old chronicler of the tribe the story of Virginia Dare, the first white child born on American soil. This name Dorr or Durr has disappeared on the Lumber river since the war of 1812. [...] These chroniclers affirm that the Dares, Coopers, Harvies and others retained their purity of blood and were generally the pioneers in emigration."

p. 19: "The name Manteo is not familiar to them. While they have a tradition of their leader or chief who went to England, yet they have preserved no name for him. The nearest approach to the name Manteo, is Maino or Mainor. An old woman, whom we interviewed, spoke of their great man as Wonoke. This name may be a corruption of Roanoke, for we must remember Manteo was made Lord of Roanoke." — "They have no tradition as to any river named Roanoke. This name is invariably applied by them to the territory previously described as occupied by their tribe on the Eastern coast."

### 14.3 The 1732 grant and the Berry descent — pp. 14-15 — VERIFIED

> "The first grant of land to any of this tribe, of which there is written evidence in existence, was made by King George the Second in 1732, to Henry Berry and James Lowrie, two leading men of the tribe, and was located on the Lowrie Swamp, East of Lumber River in present county of Robeson in North Carolina. A subsequent grant was made to James Lowrie in 1738. According to tradition there were deeds of land of older date, described as 'White' deeds and 'Smith' deeds, but no trace of their existence can be found at this date." — p. 15: "James Lowrie, was a descendant of James Lowrie of Chesapeake, who married a Croatan woman in Virginia, (as Eastern North Carolina is still designated by the tribe) and became the progenitor of all the Lowries belonging to this tribe. [...] Henry Berry, the grantee previously mentioned, was a lineal descendant of the English colonist, Henry Berry, who was left on Roanoke Island in 1587. (See list of names of Lost Colony.)"

### 14.4 Language — p. 20 — VERIFIED

> "The language spoken is almost pure Anglo Saxon, a fact which we think affords corroborative evidence of their relation to the lost colony of White. Mon (Saxon) is used for man, father is pronounced fayther, and a tradition is usually begun as follows: 'Mon, my fayther told me that his fayther told him,' &c. Mension is used for measurement, aks for ask, hit for it, hosen for hose, lovend for loving, housen for houses. They seem to have but two sounds for the letter a, one like short o. Many of the words in common use among them have long been obsolete in English speaking countries."

### 14.5 The surnames — pp. 22-24 — VERIFIED (the italics that mark the "Robeson" names are lost in the OCR, so which names McMillan italicised cannot be recovered from this text)

p. 22: "In investigating the traditions prevalent among this singular people, we found many family names identical with those of the lost colony of 1587. For the information of the reader, we give a list of the names of all the men, women and children of Raleigh's colony, which arrived in Virginia and remained to inhabit there. This list is found in 1st Volume of Hawk's History of North Carolina and copied from Hakluyt, Vol. III, page 280." [then the 1587 list, Hakluyt's spellings] — p. 24: "The names in the foregoing list in italics, are those which are found at this time among the Indians residing in Robeson county and in other counties of North Carolina. The traditions of every family bearing the name of one of the lost colonists, point to Roanoke as the country of their ancestors. If we accept their traditions they held communication with the Eastern coast long after their exodus, and it is not improbable that it was a party of this tribe which Lawson describes in 1714, as visiting their old hunting grounds and who described their ancestors as people who 'could talk in a book.'"

### 14.6 McMillan's use of the older sources — pp. 12-13 — VERIFIED

He cites Lawson (misdated "1714"), Yeardley's 1654 letter via Hawks ("So that at that early day the island was occupied by Indians who knew nothing of the lost Englishmen and who pointed out Raleigh's fort as an object of curiosity, without any tradition as to the fate of those who built it."), and the Rev. Mr Blair's 1703 letter to Lord Weymouth on "a great nation of Indians ... computed to be no less than 100,000. many of which live among the English, and all as far as I can understand, a very civilized people." He says (p. 12) of the Chowan/Mangoak searches: "The Mangoacks were seated northwest from Albemarle and it is not surprising that the messengers returned without definite information".

### 14.7 Conclusion — p. 27 — VERIFIED

> "The number of family names to be found among them, identical with those of the colonists of Roanoke is further corroborative of their traditional descent. The line of emigration from their original seat on the coast was westward, and can be traced as far west as the French Broad, in Buncombe county. [...] The writer has been much interested in investigating the traditions prevalent among the Croatans and expresses his firm conviction that they are descended from the friendly tribe found on our eastern coast in 1587, and also descended from the lost colonists of Roanoke who were amalgamated with this tribe."

*Assessment (as required, flagged 19th-century):* McMillan's evidence is (i) an 1864 speech reported "in substance" twenty-four years later; (ii) traditions gathered 1875-1888 from a community already labelled "Croatan" by the 1885 statute he himself procured; (iii) the surname coincidences (common English names) and the "Darr/Durr/Dorr" pronunciation elicited *after* he told the Virginia Dare story; (iv) archaic dialect features found in many isolated Anglophone communities. Nothing in it is independent of Lawson 1709 (which McMillan explicitly builds on) and it stands or falls with (a). Brown's 1890 endorsement (§2.3) shows how quickly it entered the scholarly literature.

## 17. Open questions and surprises

1. **The "confession" is Purchas's, not Smith's.** The musket barrel, brass mortar and pieces of iron exist only in a marginal note Purchas set beside Smith's Sicklemore sentence in 1625 (p. 1728), and the "hee had beene at their slaughter" sentence is in Purchas's own essay *Virginia's Verger* (p. 1813), not at p. 1728. Smith's 1608, 1612 and 1624 texts never mention it (§7.8). Every modern statement that "Powhatan confessed to Smith" rests on these two Purchas passages. Whether Purchas had it from Smith in conversation, from a lost paper, or built it out of the Council/Strachey tradition cannot be decided from the texts; the objects are the only element with no other source.

2. **The slaughter story is a year older than Strachey.** The Council's instructions of May 1609 already state "the slaughter of Powhaton of Roanocke, vppon the first arrivall of our Colonie" (§4.2), and Hakluyt already knows Ritanoe in April 1609 (§4a). Strachey (in London until June 1609, in Virginia from May 1610) reproduces the Council's doctrine almost clause by clause (§5.7: "his majestie hath bene acquainted ... revenge only taken upon his Quiyoughquisocks" ↔ the Council's §7 on the priests). The independent witness behind both is Machumps in London, winter 1608-09 — a single Powhatan informant, not two traditions.

3. **Where was "Roanoak"?** In Strachey "Roanoak/Ronoak" is the colony ("our other colony at Roanoak", "the first plantation at Roanoak", "our poore countrymen ... at Ronoak") and, separately, the island ("the islande aforesaid, called Roanoack", p. 28). He nowhere says the killing happened on the island; on the contrary he says the victims "were out of his territory" (p. 86), "seated far from him, and in the territory of those weroances which did in no sort depend on him" (p. 103), and that "No parte of this sowth country is supposed to be under Powhatan" (p. 28). Read on its own terms, Strachey places the massacred colonists *south of Powhatan's reach*, i.e. in the Chowan/Roanoke country — not in the Chesapeake. Quinn's (e) has to override Strachey's own geography with the Chesapeake-prophecy passage (p. 101), which never mentions the English.

4. **Does "twenty and od yeares" refer to the colonists?** Yes: "the first planted (who twenty and od yeares had peaceably lyved intermixt with those salvages, and were out of his territory)" — the antecedent is the 1587 planters. 1587 + 20-odd = 1607-1610, which matches "at what tyme this our colony, under the conduct of Capt. Newport, landed" (p. 26) and "vppon the first arrivall of our Colonie" (Council). Strachey's "seventeen or eighteen yeares togeather, yt lay neglected" (p. 153) counts 1590 → 1607/08 from the other end. So both the Council and Strachey put the killing in spring 1607, twenty years after the landing — and describe the victims as having lived "intermixt with those salvages" throughout: the slaughter tradition itself presupposes twenty years of assimilation.

5. **Does Smith 1624 contradict Strachey?** In substance yes: Smith's only fieldwork report is "they were all dead" from the Mangoags and "little hope and lesse certainetie" from the Chowanokes (1609), and he never records a killer, a confession, or survivors; Strachey (1612) has seven survivors under Eyanoco and a Powhatan massacre. Smith's 1624 additions (§7.5) even show that Powell and Todkill walked only three days south-west into a riverless upland — they never reached the Roanoke River where Barbour and FCF place Ocanahowan — so "all dead" is second-hand from a people who were themselves the neighbours of the alleged survivors. Neither Smith nor Strachey ever claims an Englishman saw a survivor.

6. **Four or seven?** The chart (1608) and the Council (1609) say four men at Pakerakanick/Peccarecamicke; Strachey says "seven ... fower men, two boyes, and one yonge mayde" at Ritanoe, having "fled up the river of Chanoke". Same four men plus three children, or two different groups? The children imply births in the colony (Purchas: "some children were borne to them there").

7. **Roanoke = beads.** After 1610 "Roanoke" in Virginia is also shell money (Hamor, Bland, Lawson). The 1619 council letter's "thre riuers of Ronoake Powsatan and Pamunky" (§10b.1) is the river; Rayner's 1621 "Voyadge ... to the Southward to Roanocke" is the island/sound. No text after 1612 uses "Roanoke" for the colony.

8. **A lost 1621 relation.** Marmaduke Rayner's written "Relacon" of a voyage "to the Southward to Roanocke" (summer 1620/21) was read to the Company on 10 July 1621 and is not printed by Kingsbury (§10b.3). It is the only recorded English return to Roanoke between Mace (1602/03) and Yeardley's men (1653). Its whereabouts (Ferrar Papers? Manchester Papers?) is a research lead.

9. **The Spaniards at Ocanahowan.** By 1611 the colonists thought Ocanahoen held a "Spanish Plantation" (Hamor, Smith) and Maguel reports silk-clad people visited by Spanish ships (§9). "Men cloathed" in 1608 need not have meant Roanoke English to the Indians who reported them; the identification is the English listeners'.

10. **Percy's yellow-haired boy** (May 1607) is the only physical observation of European descent among the Virginia Indians and no later writer connects it to Roanoke; it is at least as consistent with Spanish (1570-72) or 1585-86 English contact on the Chesapeake as with (e).

11. **Bland's English woman.** In 1650 the governor of Virginia ordered Bland "to enquire for an English woman cast away long since, and was amongst those Nations" (Tuscarora) and to speak with "an Englishman amongst them" (§12) — a living tradition of English among the southern Iroquoians, sixty years on, that no modern account I read discusses.

12. **The Strachey side-notes differ between manuscripts.** Sloane 1622 (Major): "Howses of stone, tame Turkyes, and Monkyes, supposed at Peccartcanick"; Princeton (Wright & Freund p. 34, per Barbour): "Peccarecanick between 35. and 36. degrees of the Line: and in her warme Vallyes may be planted, Sugar-Canes, Oranges, Lemons ..." — the latitude note supports a location between the Roanoke and the Pamlico/Tar (the Neuse mouth is nearer 35°). Needs checking in Wright & Freund.

13. **Strachey p. 124** ("in their discoveryes to the Mangoagues, they did light once upon twoo skynns") is an unnoticed corroboration that the 1609 Mangoag journey happened and brought objects back to the fort.

14. **The Council thought south, not north.** "if you finde them not, yet seach into this Countrey it is more probable then towards the north" (May 1609): whatever the Company had heard, it did not expect survivors in the Chesapeake or north of the James.

15. **Purchas's Pilgrimage editions (1613-1626) never adopt the confession**, even in 1626 after it had appeared in the 1625 Pilgrimes; the story belonged to the Smith-derived Virginia chapters only.

16. **Beverley (1705) and Lawson (1709)** are independent of each other and of the Jamestown tradition: Beverley = Croatoan then "cut them off"; Lawson = assimilation at Hatteras from the Hatteras Indians' own mouths. Neither knows Strachey (unprinted until 1849) or Purchas's note.

17. **McMillan (1888)** is downstream of Lawson and Hawks; his only independent data are the Robeson County traditions and surnames, and the "Darr/Durr/Dorr" pronunciation was elicited after he had told the Virginia Dare story. Brown printed it in 1890 as compatible with the Zúñiga chart — the origin of the Lumbee–Zúñiga linkage in the scholarly literature.
