Historical Mysteries

Eighteen historical mysteries examined from the original documents. Each case ends in a verdict, a figure for how sure that verdict can be, what is new in it, and a comparison with what historians have said.

The eighteen cases below are questions historians have argued over for decades or centuries: who wrote a famous poem, where a battle was fought, what killed nine hikers on a mountainside in Russia's Ural Mountains in 1959, and whether a garden that ancient writers counted among the wonders of the world existed, and if so where. Each is examined here from the original documents: charters, court records, inscriptions, shipping lists, newspaper files and manuscripts, most of them now available online.

They stay unsettled because the record is thin, late or contradictory. The earliest surviving account of the Pied Piper was written about a century and a half after the event it describes. The Beale ciphers, three pages of numbers said to give the site of buried gold, exist only in a pamphlet printed more than sixty years after the gold was said to have been buried. The poem that celebrates the battle of Brunanburh names the place, and no source written before the twelfth century says where it was. Historians have taken sides on each question, and every page names the sides and who holds them.

Each case reads the surviving record in full where it exists online, sets out the strongest argument on each side, and ends in a verdict with a figure for how sure it can be, such as "about 85 in 100". The figures are judgements rather than measurements, and a label beside each verdict says how strong the case is; the notes under the table explain both. Every case links to its full report and to the code, data and notes behind it, so that any claim can be checked against the sources.

Researched and written by Claude, an AI system, from the sources each case cites. Claude also drew the maps and charts marked "made for this report" from the data named in their captions.

Of the eighteen cases, two rest on strong evidence, ten are probable, five have a leading candidate and one is unsettled. Seventeen agree with a position some historians already hold; one (The Bixby letter) provisionally shifts part of the accepted account; none overturns a prevailing view.

How to read the table

Strong

The evidence leaves little room for another answer. The verdict is still a reading of the documents, and a new document could change it.

Probable

The evidence clearly favours one answer over all the others, but does not prove it. The percentage moves when the same evidence is weighed differently.

Leading candidate

Several answers remain possible and are put in order. The first is ahead of each rival, and the order can change when one judgement changes.

Unsettled

No answer is clearly ahead of the others. The report may still say which one it favours.

Agrees with

The conclusion agrees with a position some historians already hold, not always the prevailing one. The case may raise or lower the confidence, correct a piece of supporting evidence, or explain how the event happened.

Shifts

The broad answer stands, but a central part of the accepted account changes: how the event happened, when it happened, or the weight given to a key piece of evidence.

Overturns

The verdict rejects the prevailing view outright.

How to read the percentages. "About 85 in 100" says how likely the verdict is judged to be on the evidence weighed and the assumptions stated in the report. Where a figure comes from a calculation, the calculation shows what follows from its inputs, and the inputs are themselves judgements. The range beside each figure shows how far it moves when the assumptions change.

What every case does

  1. States a verdict first, with a figure for how sure it is. "Cannot be settled on present evidence" counts as a verdict.
  2. Separates the primary sources from later scholarship and cites every load-bearing claim to something a reader can check: a URL, an edition and page, an archive reference, an inscription number. Where the wording matters, the source is quoted in its original language.
  3. Names the strongest opposing case and answers it, and says what evidence would overturn the verdict.
  4. Says what is new and what earlier scholars had already established.
  5. Keeps the code and data wherever a result was computed, so that it can be re-run.

Each case page carries the full report with its figures, code, data and notes.

Raw downloads and files over 2 MB are kept out of this site; each case's working-files page lists them. Copies of sources still in copyright are not republished; a list says where each came from.
