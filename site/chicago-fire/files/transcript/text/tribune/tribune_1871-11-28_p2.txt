Ralph Waldo Emerson Last Evening at the*
There is probably no American, either [ln the
field of authorship or upon the platform, who is
better able to enlighten the public upon esthetic
matters than Ralph Waldo Emerson. In the
domain of letters he has long been one of the
reigning monarch*, and as a lecturer his success
has been scarcely less pronounced. His style is
transparent, and he unites in himself the power
of both thinking profoundly, and bringing home
to the understanding of even the illiterate
listener the full profundity of his thought. This
quality is as valuable as It is rare.
Mr. Emerson lectured last evening at the
Michigan Avenue Baptist Church to on audience
each as few speakers could have drawn to the
place under circumstances so Inauspicious. The
lecture was the now one entitled Nature and
Art,” this season for the first time given to the
public. It is needless to say that it was well re
ceived. The applause was discreetly timed, and
bespoke the culture of the audience. The address
embodied the ordinary tine qualities of the dis
tinguished litterateur— the pith and point, the
sparklingantithnccs the felicity of illustration, and
that surprising power, in no one more remarkable,
of flashing some great truths into the mind of the
auditor, by a happy word or a wcll-tumed
phrase.
There are few intellectual banquets offered to
the public so completely and thorough
satisfying as one of Emerson’s lectures.
