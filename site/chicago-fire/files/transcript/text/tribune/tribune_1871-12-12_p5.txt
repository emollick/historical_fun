—ln Mr. Sumner’s bill, widen proposes to au
thorize the issue of compound interest notes in
lieu of the present legal-tender United States
notes, and as a step toward specie payments,
that gentleman is unquestionably in error In as
suming that the compound interest notes would
circulate as currency at all, even for a week af
ter their issue. The experience of the former
issue of compound interest notes was
that they did not circulate as currency
even in the flush” times, when the rate of In
terest afforded by them was a matter of much
less importance—relatively to the other profits
derived from the use of currency—than it is at
present. Compound interest notes, which would
net tbe holders even 2or 3 per cent per annum,
would be hoarded in the same way that the “3
per cent certificates” were. These latter were
just as available for circulation as National Bank
notes, but the fact that they drew interest made
them worth more from the day that they were
issued than either National Bank notes or legal
tenders.” The fact that compound interest notes
would bo worth more, would also prevent their
cm ulation as currency. There can bo no
doubt, however, that the issue of com
pound interest notes in lieu of greenbacks,
would be a very decided step toward specie pay
ments,by making a contraction of the currency in
circulation, and of all the plans that will be pro
posed there will probably be none that will more
fiui cly place the currency on a specie basis, or do
it with less disturbance to finances generally
than paying interest in some way, on the curren
cy furnished by the government, in other words,
to either turn the greenbacks gradually into
compound interest notes, or to fund them. The
difference now between gold and currency is
small enough that if they were made to come to
par by some positive legislation for that pur
pose, the disturbance to finances would probably
lie very small compared to what has heretofore
been anticipated.

formed by the union of boroughs” in the man

in England the “boroughs” retained, and still
retain, their local self-governments.
In the United States, at the beginning of the
government, in imitation of our English forefath

into wards, with simply imaginary election lines,
without any local governments in the wards.
Having commenced thus, must wo continue in
the same way It is true, that no other State in
the Union has abolished the ward system, but
Illinois, in tho formation of her late constitution,
has shown herself to be the first State of tho
Union, in the science of government.

testimony (with one single exception) was, that
tho ward system was productive of extravagance

To the Editor of The Chicago TrUntne:
Sir: As our citizens are; now alive to municipal
abuses and wrongs, and ns our Legislature is im
portuned to provide remedies, I propose to make
a few suggestions in reference thereto.
Municipal evils have received a large shara of
the though# and attention of the thinkers of the
country during the last year. Several articles
have appear?d from time to time iu the Ration
and other metropolitan journals, thoroughly ex"

None, however, have suggested, and few have
undertaken to suggest, adequate remedies. What
ever suggestions have been made have chiefly re
lated to the executive departments, but none to
the legislative. "?ct It i& notorious that, bad os
are the administrative departments, the legisla
tive is, if possible, worse.
The question to be determined in the outset is.
What is the direct and immediate cause of this!
It seems to me that it is principally duo to the
mode of selecting Aldermen. In this city there
is, and formerly. In most of the cities of the coun
try, there was, but one board, or body, elected
from comparatively small districts by the votes
of such districts, it was-believed that the crea
tion of anotner and smaller body, elected from
somewhat larger districts* by the votes of such
districts, would bo a cheek upon the other body
and an improvement. This expedient has been
tried in most of the large Eastern, as well
as Western cities, and has, especially
in New York, proven a delusion and a
snare. It has only increased the number
of blood-suckers very nearly in proportion to the
number of such body. The trouble with the sys
tem is, that, in both cases, the representatives
are elected from too small districts. In our sys
tem of divisions and wards, this feature is pecu
liarly objectionable. It leads to jealousies and
rivalries, and these to combinations and rings, al
ways resulting in bad legislation. An Alderman
desirous of making a little cheap popularity in
his own division or ward concludes that Just the
thing to secure his re-eleetion Is to get an appro
priation for an engine house, school house, park,
or some other purely local purpose; and, to carry
his scheme, combines withother Aldermen of like
wants and purposes, until scheme upon scheme
is combined mountain high, and without any par
ticular thought or care as to their necessity or
propriety.
It is unnecessary to cite-cases of this and divers
similar kinds, as recollection will furnish many.
Another result of the present mode of electing
Aldermen is, that they, in fact, and to the public
detriment to a great extent, control the estimates
made by, and the action-and appointments of, tho
Boards of Public Works ami Police. Expressly
or impliedly. Aldermen vote for appropriations
for these boards, or to-be expended by them, be
cause a friend or adherent has been, or will bo,
appointed to some place; or because the boards
have recommended an appropriation for some
pet scheme. Other and equally obnoxious meas
ures are carried, acts done, and appointments
made, to secure the votes of Aldermen; which
may be traced to the desire of Aldermen to carry
their own wards for re-election, or to secure tho
election of some friend.
The idea seems to have become prevalent
