POSTAL TELEGRAPHY.
A subject is now before congress which
will rovolutionixe (for the bettor, we hope,
and we do not think it can bo worse) the
ontiro telegraph system of the United States.
The proposition is to purchaso tho telegraph
lines now owned by the different companies,
and run them under government super
vision. In other words, to conduct tho sys
tem of telegraphing in much the same man
ner that the mail system is now conducted,
and with a much reduced rate for messages.
The proposition is to buy out the present
telegraph companies and pay a sum various
ly estimated at from $70,000,000 to $200,
(00,000 for the lines now in use, and for
every United States telegraph office to be
connected in the future with the postoflice.
The idea is a good one but to purchase
the telegraph lines at the value estimated by
tho present companies, who own tho mo
nopoly, would, it seems to us, be precisely
similar to purchasing a road animal at a
jockey's valuation and, while we are in
favor of the postal telegraph system as a
matter of public benefit, we should oppose
the gigantic sum to bo paid for lines which
the government could build for a third of
the money.
There aro other objectionable features to
tho postal telegraph system as now brought
forward, and which wo have not yet seen
stated by any of the press. This is the fact,
that tho so-called “telegraph poles” on
which tho wires are hung, are simply a
nuisance and stupidly ludicrous. The poles
now in existence aro long, ungainly sticks
of cedar, cypress or pine, and aro blown
j down by every storm of wind or rain, and
certain to eventually fall over or be shattered
by lightning, or warpeil out of the earth by
the frest.
Our idea of a government postal telegraph
system would hardly depend upon the pres
ent system of long poles stuck into the
ground; but we should approve an iron
foundation for a socket, perhaps, and sur
mounted by a well finished shaft of wood'
protected from the weather (by some method
of carbolizing, or other wood preserving
process,) and also protected from the light
ning.
After this, we would wish to see an ex
amination of character and education for
the position of reporter, or for the post-tcl
egraph officer. We arc tired of witnessing
the utter ignorance and stupidity of press
dispatches sant over the wires by some irre
sponsible party, who is foisted upon the
public without the slightest regard to his
fitness or his capability. We oppose
ignorance in every form. We believe it
would bo cheaper for government to build
the lines, and employ the operators during
good behaviour, and have them in the con
dition of the army and navy—a corps of
government employes without partisan ten
dencies; and we feel assured that, sooner or
later, this must bo the method to be pur
sued, to have successful working of the
system of postal telegraphy.
