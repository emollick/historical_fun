1 he select committee to whom was
referred the resolution to inquire into
the expediency of establishing a House
of Refuge in the city of Wheeling, in
Ohio county, have had the subject
under consideration and directed me to
report. At the last session of the
General Assembly the subject was sub
mitted to the Senate, for which a bill
and a favorable report was made, but
it was not acted on, and some additional
facts and statistics are now submitted
to the consideration of the Senate.
The establishment of educational and
industrial institutions for juvenile de
linquents, has of late excited the in
terest of the friends of humanity
on both 6ides of the Atlantic,
and enlisted the advocacy of the
purest and wisest men in both hemi
spheres. The immense number of
youthful offenders without parents or
protectors, with the pressing wants of
the needy and destitute, ignorant and
uneducated, and the very many who
were consigned to the jails and State
prisons to be pupils of old convicts;
presented such a revolting picture o>
moral desolation that it suggested the
necessity of these reformatory institu
tions. They are of recent origin. They
originated in the private enterprise and
the voluntary contributions of the be
nevolent and humane. The rapidity
with which they have multiplied, and
their eminent adaptation to the end
to be accomplished, have been recog
nized and sanctioned by the legislative
interference of most of the enlight
ened governments of the age. The
first establishment in Germany was
that of Dussell, on the Rhine. It was
founded by Count bon Der Riche, who
had, before entering upon this estab
lishment, opened a similar one on his
own estate, in Westphalia, as early as
1816. The second was at Benggen, in
1820. In 1825 another was established
at Keren. And the last and most dis
tinguished is the one at Kawbe Hans,
in 1833.
But the great impulse was given to
these institutions in Germany, about
the year 1848. Their efficacy iiad been
satisfactorily demonstrated. The hid
den depravity of a large portion of the
youthful population was brought to the
public attention, and the best men of
the country became interested and
engaged in sustaining and advocating
this great practically tested scheme for
correcting and arresting it. At this
time the number of reformatory estab
lishments, including morally destitute
children, as well as criminal ones, and
including those Provinces of Russia
and that part of Europe which usually
follows the reforms of Germany, the
whole number of reformatory institu
tions would amount to about one hun
dred.
In France there are now seventeen
of these institutions under government
control, and thirty-five under private
management.
The English government within the
last fourteen years has been aroused to
the importance of these institutions,
although its legislation has not been
distinguished by that character which
has secured the confidence of their
friends, as those which are best calcu
lated to attain the end in view, still the
parliamentary acta of lS40-'47 and '54,
is a complete acknowledgment of the
adequacy and utility of ihe system, and
the great statesmen Brougham and
Palmerston have publicly approved ol
it, bv their efforts to sustain and for
ward it.
The crown has been io the habit, re
cently, of granting conditional pardons
to such juvenile offenders as was re
commended by the inspectors, on con-j
dition of their remaining at the Phi
lanthropic Society's Farm School, at
Red Hill. This institution was founded
in 1788; it was removed from London
to Red Hill in 1849. The society has
two great objects the reiormation of
the children it receives, and the ame
lioration of their condition generally.
About one hundred are sent thither an
nually, and for these the government
pays 25 a head. The establishment
of Parkhurst has existed about four
teen years. It ?9 intended to accommo
date about 628 inmates. They are all
boys. They are sent to Parkhurst by
order of the Secretary of State, from
Mill-baiik prison, where they are col
lected from all the prisons of the
country. They are all fcoys under sen
tence of transportation. They are se
lected by the directors of the convict
prisons at Mill-bank. Any boy under
eighteen years of age, who may be sen
tenced to transportation, is eligible
for Parkhurst. When their con
duct is good at Parkhurst, the
Governor recommends them to the
Secretary of State for the indulgence of
a leave ticket that they shall be em
barked to Australia, there to receive
their ticket* of leave.
The Scotch act extends the provision
of the law to vagrants as well as con
victed children, and empower the Edu
cation committee of the Privy Council
to aid in the establishment of reforma
tory schools.
In the United States the system has
met a very general legislative endorse
ment and popular approval. The State
of Connecticut at tbe session of the
Assembly in 1851, appropriated $10,000
for a State Reform School. Rhode
Island established the Providence Re
form School in 1850. New Jersey au
thorized the establishment of a House
of Refuge, February 23, 1850. There
are two of these institutions in the
State of New York; two in Pennsylva
nia; one in Ohio; one in Maryland;
and one in Louisiana. Vermont, in
1852, passed an act authorizing the se
lection and to obtain the refusal of a
lot of land not less than fifty acres for
a Manual Labor School. New Hamp
shire, at a session of the Legislature of
1854, authorized the selection and to
obtain the refusal of a lot of land not
less than fifty acres for a site for a
Manual Labor School. Massachusetts,
also, authorized the establishment of a

Retributive justice has failed to stay
the crime, and to reclaim the young of
