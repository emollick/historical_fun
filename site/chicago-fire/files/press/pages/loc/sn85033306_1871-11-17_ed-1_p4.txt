A Japanese Hotel.
As I was about to pass my first
night in a Japanese house, I watched
anxiously the preparationsof sleeping.
These are very simple a mattress in
the form of a very thick quilt, about
esven feet long by four wide, was
spread on the lloor, and over it was
laid an ample robe, very long, and
heavily padded, and provided with
long sleeves. Having put on this
dress, the sleeper covers himself with
another quilt, and sleeps, i. e., if he
has had some years practice in the use
of this bed.
But the most remarkable feature
about a Japanese bed is the pillow.
This is a wooden box about four inches
high, eightinches long, and twoinches
at the top. It has a cushion of folded
papers on the upper side to rest the
neck on, for the eialiorate manner
of dressing the hair does not permit
the Japanese, especially the women,
to press the head on the pillow. Every
morning the uppermost paper is takeu
off from the cushion, exposing a clean
surface without the expense of wash
ing a pillow-slip.
I passed the greater part of the night
in learning how to poise my head in
this novel manner; and when I fin
ally c losed my eyes, it was to dream
that 1 was being slowly beheaded, and
to wake at the crisis to find the pillow
bottom side up, and m. neck resting
on the sharp edgeof the box. During
my stay in the country I learned
many of its customs, mastering the
use of the chop-sticks, and accustom
ing my palate to raw fresh fish, but
the attempt to balance my head on a
two-inch pillow I gave up in despair,
after trying in vain to secure the box
by tying it to my neck and head.
