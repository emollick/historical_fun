Chicago · the night of 8 October 1871

The Great Chicago Fire

How did the O'Leary barn on DeKoven Street catch fire on the evening of Sunday 8 October 1871, at what hour, and can anyone be named for it?

What happened. On the warm, dry evening of Sunday 8 October 1871, at the end of a summer with little rain, a fire started in the cow barn behind the two small cottages of Patrick and Catherine O'Leary, an Irish couple who kept five cows and sold the milk, at 137 DeKoven Street on the West Side of Chicago. By Patrick O'Leary's account his wife went to bed about eight and he half an hour after her; on the neighbourhood's time that put him in bed about ten minutes, and her about forty, when the barn caught, though the O'Learys cited no clock. Their tenants the McLaughlins, who rented the front cottage while the O'Learys lived in the rear one, had company that evening for a cousin newly arrived from Ireland, with two tunes on a fiddle and two dances. Daniel Sullivan, a neighbour from across the street who drove a delivery wagon for a living and had a wooden leg, had sat with the O'Learys until they turned in; some time after half past eight by the neighbourhood's clocks he saw fire in the barn, ran across, shouted fire, cut two cows loose and got out as the loft burned above him. Within half an hour five buildings were burning; the fire crossed the river that night and burned through the centre of the city for two days.

Why it is a mystery. Nobody saw the fire start, and within a day the newspapers had blamed Mrs O'Leary and a cow that kicked over her lamp, in two extras (special editions) of 9 October, one of them the Chicago Evening Journal's, an afternoon paper, a story that Michael Ahern, a reporter for the Chicago Republican in 1871, boasted forty years later he had made up; the Board of Police and Fire Commissioners, the city board that ran both the police and the fire department, held an inquiry in November and December 1871, questioned fifty-one witnesses and found no proof that anyone had been in the barn after dark. H. A. Musham, whose 1940 study is the longest older account of the fire's first hours, kept the cow story; Richard Bales, a lawyer with the Chicago Title insurance company, which holds the only land records to have survived the fire, argued in a 1997 article and a 2002 book that Sullivan could not have seen the barn from where he said he sat, that he lied to hide his own presence in it, and that he started the fire, alone or with Dennis Regan, another neighbour who had called that evening; and on the strength of Bales's research the Chicago City Council exonerated Mrs O'Leary and her cow by resolution in October 1997. Others have blamed a guest from the McLaughlins' party going to the barn for milk, boys gambling in the loft by lantern light (a claim first made public in 1944 on behalf of Louis M. Cohn, a Chicago businessman who died in 1942 and said he had been one of them), a spark from a chimney, the new hay heating by itself, and fragments of Biela's comet, a comet that had broken up and been lost to sight some twenty years earlier; A. T. Andreas, who interviewed the neighbours and the firemen for his History of Chicago in 1884, and Carl Smith, the Northwestern University historian whose 2020 book is the most recent full history of the fire, both concluded that the cause cannot be known. Three things keep the question open: the witnesses' words survive but disagree with one another by up to an hour; the shorthand record of the inquiry survives only as 1,100 handwritten pages at the Chicago History Museum; and Bales's argument about Sullivan's view, the blocked view, the only evidence that names anyone, has been repeated in print for nearly thirty years without a published re-examination.

Verdict

A flame carried into the barn by a person, about 85 percent; the person cannot be identified from the surviving evidence. Among the possible carriers, Daniel Sullivan and a member of the O'Leary household stand first, close to even. The fire began inside the barn, on the side where wood and shavings were kept, at about 8.40 by the neighbourhood's clocks.

What is new here

  • Five documents not previously used in the published discussion, as far as could be checked against a book read only in fragments: the entries in Edwards' city directory for 1871 (the annual list of residents with their trades and addresses) showing Sullivan and White as teamsters (drivers) for the same firm, Chase, Hanford & Co., an oil merchant on South Water Street; the Tribune's reports of the inquiry sittings of 29 November and 4 December 1871, on pages missing from the Library of Congress's online newspaper collection (30 November p. 4 and 5 December p. 2) and recovered from another scan; the silence of the Illinois Staats-Zeitung, the city's German-language daily, on the inquiry and the Board's report; the dating of the Evening Journal's O'Leary interview to 21 October 1871; and a second Chicago extra of 9 October 1871 carrying the cow story.
  • The sightline test: a 3-D model built from the 1891 atlas, the 1906 Sanborn map, the present-day parcel, one photograph and the deeds Bales found in the tract books, run with uncertainty ranges over all four placements of Sullivan already in the record (his affidavit's "a few lots east", the Board's "nearly opposite", Andreas's "in front of his own house", and Bales's midpoint of White's west parcel), 7,200 draws. The model adds no document to the record.
  • The three-cluster reading of the 46 time statements, grouped by the clock each rests on, and the 10 to 15 minute offset between the neighbourhood's clocks and the fire department's, a re-reading of the Tribune's and Andreas's reports. By Patrick O'Leary's account he had been in bed about ten minutes (none to twenty) and his wife about forty (thirty to fifty) when the barn caught, on times for which the O'Learys cited no clock. Sullivan's sequence of events is consistent once the clock times he gave are discarded: an hour at the O'Learys' from about a quarter past seven, twenty to twenty-five minutes on the sidewalk, the fire seen at about 8.40.
  • The inference from computed sunrise that the 5 a.m. milking was in darkness, so a lamp was kept in the barn every day and its remains prove nothing; whether Bales makes the point could not be checked, since his chapter 2 was read only in snippets.
  • The re-working of the arithmetic in Bales's Appendix B, where he adds up the distance Sullivan ran to the barn: his 193 feet depends on the seat he chose, and a man who had driven a dray (a delivery wagon) for fifteen years on a wooden leg covers it in thirty to fifty seconds.
  • The finding of Anthony DeBartolo, a Chicago Tribune writer (1997, 1998), that Cohn's recorded will contains no confession is his and is not claimed as new; it is repeated because the contrary is often printed.

Already established by others. The Board of Police found in December 1871 that the cause could not be determined from the evidence; Andreas restated that in 1885 and Carl Smith in 2011 and 2020. Where this verdict stands among historians

Agrees withthe Board of Police (1871), Andreas (1885) and Carl Smith (2020) that the cause cannot be determined

What historians have said. The Board of Police (December 1871), Andreas (1885) and Carl Smith (2011, 2020) found the cause undetermined, and Musham (1940) affirmed the cow story. Bales argued in 1997 and 2002 from the Chicago Title land records and the houses on the block that Sullivan could not have seen the barn from where he said he sat, that his story covers his own presence in the barn, and that he, perhaps with Regan, started the fire; his case has been repeated in print for nearly thirty years. The Chicago City Council, by resolution of 28 October 1997 introduced by Alderman Edward M. Burke and approved by its Committee on Police and Fire on 6 October 1997 after a hearing at the fire academy that stands on the site of the barn, resolved to "forever exonerate Mrs. O'Leary and her cow from all blame in regard to the Great Chicago Fire of 1871": a legislative judgment on Bales's research, which adds nothing to the evidence about the night.

It agrees with the official finding that the cause cannot be determined and adds no name. It differs from Bales (2002) on Sullivan: his sightline argument holds for the one seat he chose, the midpoint of White's west parcel, and fails for the three other placements the record gives, and his time argument rests on an error about the hour that the official record as a whole makes. Nothing here overturns what historians already think. Bales's refutation of the cow story stands and the analysis here depends on it, and any model of the block depends on his tract-book research.

What would overturn it

  • The full inquiry transcript, read in the original, holding a statement by Sullivan or Regan about what they saw from the street that fits no placement at all, or an admission by anyone that they were in the barn.
  • A record of Dalton's house (a builder's contract, an insurance survey, a photograph) fixing its width and height: with the house two feet lower or five feet narrower, the ridge is visible from White's parcel in most draws; with it two feet higher, nothing under ten feet of flame is.
  • The 1870 or 1872 directory, or a deed, settling whether No. 134, Sullivan's house, was the east half of Lot 18 or the west half of Lot 19.
  • A text from October to December 1871 in which Mrs O'Leary, or anyone in the house, admits being in the barn; none was found in about 120 issues of 1871 papers, but the Chicago Times of 18 October and the Evening Journal extra of 9 October were read only through later quotation.
  • Documentation of Cohn's story from before 1942, which would do the same for the alley scenario.

Limits

  • The inquiry transcript was not read in the original: the Chicago History Museum's transcription could not be obtained, and Bales's Appendix D, his transcription of the witnesses, could be recovered only in fragments of about 100 characters.
  • Bales's book could not be read in full: it was read through two search services that return fragments of a book rather than its pages, 128 Google Books queries returning 534 snippets, each tagged with its page number, from 187 pages, and 435 Internet Archive search-inside queries returning 1,439 fragments of about 100 characters joined into 76 passages; his Figures 54, 64 and 78 and his 1997 article were not seen.
  • Carl Smith's 2020 book was read through its excerpt in American Heritage magazine; the later literature on the fire, Sawislak (1995), Pauly (1984), Miller (1990), Cromie (1958) and Kogan and Cromie (1971), was not obtained; Ahern's and Cohn's originals are known through later quotation. The 1997 resolution is cited from its own text as reproduced by the Glessner House Museum, a Chicago house museum, and from the press of October 1997; the Journal of the Proceedings, the City Council's own record, for 28 October 1997 was not reached.
  • The barn's orientation is unsettled between Andreas's plan and Musham's, and the heights of Dalton's house, of the O'Learys' two cottages and of the cottage at No. 133 rest on one photograph and typical construction; the model carries these as ranges. It leaves out trees, smoke, the coal shed in the passage beside the cottage and the light of the fire itself. Its result is a range of heights, and it cannot settle a claim that falls inside those ranges.
  • The 1870 and 1872 directories could not be obtained, nor could several archive holdings; no paywall or bot wall (a block on automated visitors) was bypassed.
