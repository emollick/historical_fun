Tennessee · Grinder's Stand on the Natchez Trace, 11 October 1809

The death of Meriwether Lewis

Did Meriwether Lewis shoot himself at Grinder's Stand on the Natchez Trace in the night of 10 to 11 October 1809, or did someone else fire the shots?

What happened. On the evening of 10 October 1809 Meriwether Lewis, who with William Clark had commanded the expedition that crossed the continent to the Pacific and back in 1804 to 1806, and who was now governor of the Louisiana Territory at St. Louis, rode up alone to Grinder's Stand, a settler's cabin that took in travellers on the Natchez Trace, the old road from the Mississippi to Nashville, about 72 miles south-west of Nashville in Tennessee. He was 35 and on his way to Washington to defend his public accounts, since the War Department had refused to pay bills he had drawn as governor and his private creditors had closed in; he had come down the Mississippi ill with fever, and Captain Gilbert Russell, commanding Fort Pickering (the army post where Memphis now stands), had found him deranged and kept him a fortnight before he took the road with James Neelly, the government's agent to the Chickasaw nation, through whose land the Trace ran, with Neelly's servant and with his own servant John Pernier; Neelly had stayed a day behind to find two strayed horses. The innkeeper, Robert Grinder, was away; his wife Priscilla gave Lewis the house and slept in the kitchen cabin, the servants slept in the stable, and in the night she heard two pistol shots; the servants found Lewis shot in the head and in the body, and by some accounts cut with a razor, and he said he had shot himself, asked for water, and died about sunrise. Neelly came up later that day and buried him near the stand, and wrote to Thomas Jefferson, the former president, who had planned the expedition and remained Lewis's patron, that the governor had killed himself; Jefferson, Clark and the newspapers accepted it, no doctor saw the body and no inquest (the official inquiry into a death) was held.

Why it is a mystery. None of the people who wrote about the night was in the room: everything comes from Mrs Grinder, who told the story at least four times over 36 years and told it differently each time, and from two servants whose words survive only as others reported them, and nobody recorded powder marks, the position of the pistols or the state of his hands. The case for suicide was made by the people who knew him: Jefferson wrote in 1813 that Lewis had suffered "hypocondriac affections" (depression, in the usage of the day) from early life, Russell reported two attempts on his own life on the river, a letter that James House, a captain at Nashville, wrote thirteen days before the death, passing on what Amos Stoddard, an army major and Lewis's friend, had heard from a man just come from Fort Pickering, says the same, and Neelly found him "at times deranged in mind" on the road; most historians since Dawson Phelps, the National Park Service historian of the Trace (1956), and Donald Jackson, editor of the expedition's letters (1962), have held that view. The case for murder began in Tennessee, where in 1848 the state legislature appointed a committee to put a monument over the grave, and the committee's report to the legislature of 1849 to 1850 said that death "by the hands of an assassin" seemed "more probable"; local tradition accused the innkeeper and the Lewis family accused the servant; and since the novelist Vardis Fisher's Suicide or Murder? (1962) argued that every document was second-hand and every witness interested, the question has been argued in print by surgeons, lawyers, document examiners and historians: the surgeon E. G. Chuinard (1991 to 92) has said Lewis could not have survived and moved as described and named Neelly as the killer; the historian John Guice (1998 to 2006) has argued that the story never fitted, from the moonless night, on which Mrs Grinder claimed to have watched Lewis stagger about the yard, to the two hours she left him wounded; the forensic scientist James Starrs and the independent researcher Kira Gale (2009, 2016) have argued for a plot by General James Wilkinson, the army's senior officer, and called Russell's 1811 statement, his written account of Lewis's last weeks given two years later, a forgery; and Tony Turnbow, a historian of the Trace, has argued for a robber and a cover story arranged by Neelly. Thomas Danisi, Lewis's biographer, holds that Lewis fired the shots in a malarial delirium and that "suicide" is the wrong word; in 1996 a coroner's jury in the county where the grave lies recommended exhumation, a federal court in 1998 and the Department of the Interior, which manages the grave as part of the Natchez Trace Parkway, in 2010 were asked to allow it, and the grave has not been opened.

How it was judged. Every document of 1809 to 1813 that bears on the death was read, and so was the modern debate, from Fisher's book to 2025, in full where it is in articles and in fragments where it is in books; the events were put in date order, Mrs Grinder's tellings laid side by side, her one visual claim checked against the moon, and the reported wounds compared with modern forensic case series (published collections of similar deaths); then sixteen evidence items were scored in an explicit model, a calculation written out so that a reader who disagrees with any number can change it and see what follows, in which each item's weight is entered as a range of how much more likely the evidence would be if Lewis shot himself than if someone else did, and the ranges are combined under four starting assumptions (priors), two sceptic settings that discount every item, and a leave-one-out check in which each item is dropped in turn. The case rests mainly on the month before the death rather than on the night itself, for which there is no reliable witness: attempts on his own life were reported thirteen days before he died (House to Frederick Bates, Lewis's deputy and enemy, 28 September 1809) and are attested through three chains of report that do not depend on the disputed statement Russell gave in 1811, though only House's is certainly independent of Russell himself; Jefferson and Clark accepted the report at once; and the two men who could have robbed him, Neelly and Pernier, went straight to his patrons, while his pistols, rifle, watch, horses and two trunks of papers were all accounted for (the watch with one open question, noted under Limits). The reported wound sequence, a head shot that missed the brain and then a shot to the trunk with hours of survival, is one that suicides with a pistol are documented to produce, and every forensic pathologist who has examined the surgical claim that it is impossible has rejected it; every murder theory requires at least one of the three people at the stand to have lied about a man the servants found alive and talking, and the conspiracy version requires the record to have been false from a fortnight before the death. The verdict is that Lewis shot himself: self-inflicted about 85 percent, homicide about 15 percent, accident under 1 percent; murder keeps about 15 percent because the body was never examined for the signs that would decide a shooting and because the model cannot allow for the possibility that the three people at the stand agreed on a false story, and an exhumation showing a wound Lewis could not have given himself, or a record of an 1809 inquest naming a killer, would overturn it.

Verdict

Lewis shot himself: self-inflicted about 85 percent, homicide about 15 percent, accident under 1 percent. "Self-inflicted" covers both a decision to die and a delirium in which the same acts were done without one.

Probable

The figure comes from an explicit weighing with its sensitivity (how far the result moves when the inputs change) printed beside it, and it summarises judgments rather than measuring anything. Nobody who saw the body knew what to look for, no archival work was done, and the principal opposing books (Fisher 1962, Guice 2006, Starrs and Gale 2009) were read only in snippets, though the opposing articles were read in full.

Sixteen evidence items were weighed in an explicit model, every item's weight entered as a range, under four priors, two sceptic settings and leave-one-out. The central run (even prior, meaning equal starting odds for self-infliction and homicide, and conservative grouping, in which items that rest on one fact count once) gives 92% self-inflicted, with the central 90% of the model's runs lying between 36 and 99; the sceptic runs, which halve or quarter the weight of every item, give 76% and 63%; a murder-leaning prior of 3 to 1 with every item quartered gives 32%. No single item moves the central result by more than 9 points (dropping the item for the valuables left with the body gives 83%). Accident, entered as a simple discharge (one pistol going off by mishap), is under 1% in every base run; the sceptic runs lift it to 2% under the even prior and to at most 2.5% under the murder-leaning prior; and it reaches 7 to 17% only under a flat prior that starts it at a third. The headline of 85% sits below the central run because the model cannot allow for how badly the body was examined: nobody who saw it knew what to look for (the range of the shot, soot from the powder, the state of the hands).

The record cannot separate a decision to die from a delirium in which the same acts were done without one, and the verdict does not depend on which illness lay behind the derangement. The weight falls on the evidence of attempts and derangement in the month before the death, reported through separate chains (House's informant from Fort Pickering, Russell, Neelly, Pernier as Clark reported him, and Mrs Grinder as Neelly and the ornithologist Alexander Wilson, who visited the stand in May 1810, reported her), one of them written down thirteen days before the death, and on base rates for multiple-shot and complex suicides from small modern series, which show the reported wound sequence to be a recognised shape of a failed-then-completed suicide.

The strongest case against is surgical and documentary. Chuinard (1991 to 92) held that Lewis could not have survived the second shot for two hours and moved as described; Guice (2002) showed that the night was moonless and asked how Mrs Grinder could have seen Lewis stagger across the yard; Starrs and Gale (2009) and Gale (2016) call Russell's 1811 statement a forgery and House, Bates, Wilkinson and Jefferson a conspiracy. The surgical claim is contradicted by every forensic pathologist who has addressed it, including the one who testified at the 1996 inquest. The moon's phase on the night, new on 9 October and set an hour after sunset on the 10th, contradicts only Wilson's telling of Mrs Grinder's story, the one version in which she claims to have seen Lewis stagger about the yard; the telling Neelly carried to Nashville within the week, in which she heard the shots and woke the servants, claims nothing seen. The attempts on the boat are attested through three chains that do not depend on Russell's statement, one of them written before the death, so the conspiracy would have had to begin a fortnight before Lewis died.

What was done

Every document of 1809 to 1813 that bears on the death was read in saved copies. The petition of Lewis's servant John Pernier to Thomas Jefferson, the former President, of 10 February 1810 was read in full; the letter of James House from Nashville of 28 September 1809 and the 1811 statement of Gilbert Russell, the captain at Fort Pickering, are known only in excerpts. The modern debate, from the novelist Vardis Fisher's Suicide or Murder? (1962) to 2025, was read in full where it is in articles and only in search snippets where it is in books, Fisher's among them. No archive was visited.

The events were put in date order, and the tellings of Priscilla Grinder, the innkeeper's wife, who told the story at least four times over 36 years, were laid side by side; her one visual claim, that she watched Lewis stagger about the yard by moonlight, was checked against the phase of the moon, recomputed for the site and the night. The reported wounds were compared with modern forensic case series, published collections of similar deaths, using base rates (how often each feature occurs in those series) for suicides with more than one shot and for complex suicides, those using more than one method, gathered from forensic abstracts.

Sixteen evidence items were scored in an explicit model, a calculation written out so that a reader who disagrees with any number can change it and see what follows. Each item's weight is entered as a range of how much more likely the evidence would be if Lewis shot himself than if someone else did; the ranges are combined under four starting assumptions (priors), two sceptic settings that halve or quarter the weight of every item, and a leave-one-out check (each item dropped in turn). The model picks each score at random within its range 100,000 times and reports the middle value and the central 90 percent of the results.

What is new here

  • John Pernier's own petition to Jefferson of 10 February 1810 (Founders Online, the National Archives' free online edition of the founders' papers, item Jefferson/03-02-02-0171), read in full and placed in the sequence of his last months. It shows him destitute and refused by the government, asking $271.50 in wages "to pay my just debts & resqued from prison"; he died of laudanum (a tincture of opium) eleven weeks later. Chalkley 2004, the fullest study of Pernier, did not use it. Gale 2016 used its $271.50 and its fear of prison as evidence that Pernier was a witness who had been silenced; the petition names no threat, and what it records is wages, debt and sickness.
  • Three attestations of the suicide attempts on the boat that do not depend on Russell's disputed 1811 statement (House to Bates, 28 September 1809, written before the death; Russell's lost letters as Clark summarised them to his brother on 26 November 1809; the Washington newspaper, the National Intelligencer, of 27 November 1809), as an answer to the claim that the statement is a forgery. Only House's chain is certainly independent of Russell as a source.
  • Forensic base rates applied to the wound pattern: multiple-shot suicides are 1.6 to 8% of firearm suicides, the first head shot missed the brain in 5 of the 11 cases in the largest series, published by Karger and Brinkmann in 1997, torso shots predominate (a series published by Jensen in 2025), unplanned complex suicide (a change of method after the first fails) is a named category, and survival with the power to act after a trunk wound is ordinary. The series were read in abstracts and are used as orders of magnitude.
  • The evidence model, with its sensitivity, leave-one-out and grouping printed beside the headline.
  • Small points about dates and documents: Wilson's visit was Sunday 6 May 1810, as the editions of his works by George Ord (1828) and Alexander Grosart (1876) already implied (the "May 28th, 1811" printed in the Port Folio, a Philadelphia magazine, misled Clay Jenkinson in his 2011 study of Lewis's character); the $99.58 Treasury check that Russell had lent Lewis at Fort Pickering, and which Russell listed in January 1810 among the money "none of which it is said could be found", did reach Washington and was forwarded to Jefferson, as the editors of the Retirement Series of Jefferson's papers note, so the missing money, one of the two items that most favour murder, did not include it; the monument committee's "assassin" (report of 1849 to 1850) rests on two stated grounds, the loneliness of the place and a story that the servant fled to Natchez, which the documents refute; and two pieces of contemporary St. Louis testimony (the St. Louis Missouri Gazette, 2 Nov 1809: fever "never failed of depriving him of his reason"; Stoddard, writing from Nashville on 28 Sept 1809 about Lewis's illness at Fort Pickering: "under the hands of the surgeons mate Dr. Smith") are not used in any of the works read.

Already established by others. The documentary case for suicide was made by Dawson Phelps (1956) and Donald Jackson (1962, 1978), and it has been the majority position among historians since; Paul Russell Cutright, a historian of the expedition (1986), James Holmberg of the Filson Historical Society (1992), the physician David Peck (2009) and the National Park Service hold it, and Danisi accepts that Lewis fired the shots and rejects the word suicide. Other lines followed here were opened by earlier writers: Holmberg 1992 on Clark's letters to his brother; Guice 2002 on the moonless night, which the calculation here confirms; the Lewis researcher Ann Rogers 2005 on the shot sequence being a presumption; Michael Carrick, a firearms historian, 2006/2009 on the pistols; Tom Chalkley 2004 on Pernier; Peck 2009/2022 on the medical answer to Chuinard; Danisi 2013 to 14 and Turnbow 2012/2015 on the Neelly dispute (whether Neelly was where he said he was on 11 October), left open; Clay Jenkinson 2011 on Mrs Grinder as a witness against her own interest.

What historians have said. Suicide has been the majority position among historians since Dawson Phelps (1956) and Donald Jackson (1962); Paul Russell Cutright, a historian of the expedition (1986), James Holmberg of the Filson Historical Society (1992), the physician David Peck (2009) and the National Park Service hold it, and Danisi accepts that Lewis fired the shots and rejects the word suicide. The case for murder has been made by Fisher (1962), Chuinard (1991 to 92), Guice (2006), Starrs and Gale (2009), Gale (2016), the Tennessee monument committee (appointed 1848, report 1849 to 1850) and the 1996 coroner's jury. The field is not settled: among authors who have stated a position in print since 2006, 13 are for suicide, 6 for murder in three versions that exclude one another, 3 for a self-inflicted death that was not suicide, and 6 or 7 undecided. At a panel of historians in 2009, two hundred years after the death, the historians put suicide at 96 percent and the audience voted 350 to 20 for murder.

The verdict agrees with that majority and with the documentary case behind it, and the question is not treated as settled. The case for murder is stated in its authors' words and answered by name in section 9 of the full report, which is linked from this page. What is added to the majority case is its basis: the weight moves from testimony about the night, which is all second-hand, to the evidence of attempts and derangement in the month before the death, reported through separate chains, one of them written down thirteen days before the death, and to base rates for multiple-shot and complex suicides from small modern series, which show the reported wound sequence to be a recognised shape of a failed-then-completed suicide.

What would overturn it

  • Exhumation showing a penetrating entry to the forehead from behind or above rather than a grazing groove, a trunk trajectory a man cannot give himself, a ball of a calibre no gentleman's pistol fired, or a range beyond arm's reach. The Department of the Interior refused exhumation on 25 August 2010 as a "final decision".
  • A record of an 1809 inquest naming a killer. None has been found in 130 years of looking, and the county sources of 1894 to 1904 say the papers are lost or never existed.
  • The full text of the 1845 "schoolteacher" account (Mrs Grinder's last telling, given to a passing stranger about 1839 and printed in 1845) turning out to contain details that can be checked, instead of the additions its fragments suggest.
  • House's letter proving misdated, or Stoddard's informant proving to be Neelly or Russell coaching the story, which would remove the only attestation of the attempts written before the death.
  • A document from Pernier's last months showing money or goods of Lewis's in his hands beyond the horse of Lewis's that Neelly sent him east on and that Jefferson told him to sell and credit to the estate.
  • Anything placing Neelly at the stand on the night of the 10th, which would make him a witness and a suspect at once.
