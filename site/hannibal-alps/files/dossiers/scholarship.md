# Hannibal's Alpine pass (218 BC): scholarship and testimonia dossier

Scope: (1) who has argued for which pass, with what argument, and where each puts Polybius' "Island"; (2) the ancient testimonia outside Polybius 3 and Livy 21; (3) the case for and against each candidate pass; (4) the state of the debate 2010-2026 with "nothing has happened since" checks; (5) the access log. Raw texts are under `texts/scholarship/` and `texts/testimonia/`.

**Status labels used throughout.** FULL = complete text obtained and the cited pages read. PART = complete OCR obtained, only the cited chapters read (or a substantial extract). SNIP = Open Library search-inside snippets only (no page numbers unless a running head or index entry appears inside the snippet). ABS = Crossref/publisher abstract or metadata only. SEC = the author's verdict is known only from a named secondary source. NONE = not obtained. Anything from memory rather than a fetched source is marked "(unverified)".

**Headline.** No ancient text names Hannibal's pass. The modern literature has never converged: the Isère–Arc–Col du Clapier family (Perrin 1883/87, Azan, Colin, Wilkinson, Walbank "hesitatingly", Proctor, Lazenby, Huss, Lancel, Hunt, Kuhle & Kuhle, Hornblower 2024) is the most-favoured single answer among ancient historians since 1956; the Montgenèvre has a persistent minority (Hennebert, Caspari, Bullock Hall, Connolly 1981, Hoyos 2006/2015, W. T. Arnold's "best Durance pass"); the Col de la Traversette is the de Beer line (Torr 1924, de Beer 1955-69, Bagnall, Prevas 1998/2017, Mahaney 2007-2026, Seibert reportedly, Berti & Vollrath 2026); the Petit-Saint-Bernard survives among French regional scholars (Bocquet, Coninck) and in older German and English work (Mommsen, Lenschau, Law, Hyde); the Mont Cenis (Napoleon, Larauza, Maissiat, Osiander, Jullian) and the Col de Larche/Argentière (Freshfield) have lost their advocates; and a substantial group of recent syntheses declines to decide (Goldsworthy 2000, MacDonald 2015, Lendering 2022; Lancel 1995 calls the whole exercise "plus chimérique que de prétendre trouver le vrai site d'Alésia"). The label "resolved" is not available for any pass on the basis of the scholarship.

## 2. The ancient testimonia outside Polybius 3 and Livy 21

Texts saved under `texts/testimonia/` with the edition noted in each file header. Translations are the Loeb's where one was obtained (Jones for Strabo, Rolfe for Sallust and Ammianus, Cary for Zonaras); otherwise my own, marked (tr. mine).

### 2.1 Varro apud Servius, ad Aen. 10.13 (Thilo–Hagen II, 1884; text from la.wikisource, `Servius_ad_Aen_10.13_latin_wikisource_Thilo.txt`)

> *quas Hannibal post bella Hispaniae, quae xvii annis confecit, ante exustas aceto infuso rupit: Iuvenalis "et montem rupit aceto". denique loca ipsa quae rupit, Poeninae Alpes vocantur. quamvis legatur a Poenina dea, quae ibi colitur, Alpes ipsas vocari. sane omnes altitudines montium licet a Gallis Alpes vocentur, proprie tamen iuga montium Gallicorum sunt. quas quinque viis Varro dicit transiri posse: una, quae est iuxta mare per Ligures; altera, qua Hannibal transiit; tertia, qua Pompeius ad Hispaniense bellum profectus est; quarta, qua Hasdrubal de Gallia in Italiam venit; quinta, quae quondam a Graecis possessa est, quae exinde Alpes Graiae appellantur.*
>
> (tr. mine) "…which Hannibal, after the Spanish wars that he finished in seventeen years, broke open, having first burned them and poured on vinegar (Juvenal: 'and broke the mountain with vinegar'). Indeed the very places he broke open are called the Poenine Alps — though one reads that the Alps themselves are so named from the goddess Poenina who is worshipped there. …Varro says they can be crossed by five roads: one, which is beside the sea through the Ligurians; a second, by which Hannibal crossed; a third, by which Pompey set out for the Spanish war; a fourth, by which Hasdrubal came from Gaul into Italy; a fifth, which was once held by Greeks and is therefore called the Graian Alps."

What it says: Varro (1st c. BC) knew five Alpine roads and distinguished Hannibal's from Pompey's, from Hasdrubal's and from the Graian (Little St Bernard) road. It does not name Hannibal's pass. Servius himself, in the same note, places the burned rocks in the *Poeninae Alpes* (Great St Bernard), i.e. he shares the "vulgar error" Livy 21.38.6-9 rejects.

How scholars read Varro's order:
- *Geographical, south to north* (Freshfield 1914 pp. 20-21; Torr 1924 para 24; de Beer 1956/1969; Prevas): coast road; Hannibal's; Pompey's = Mont Genèvre; Hasdrubal's = Mont Cenis; Graian = Little St Bernard. On this reading Hannibal's pass lies between the Corniche and the Montgenèvre: Col de Larche (Freshfield), Col de la Traversette (Torr, de Beer). Freshfield: "Is it not then reasonable to assume that Varro in his catalogue took all the five passes in their geographical order, beginning from the sea? Is it not the only plausible assumption?" (p. 20). Torr adds that the list "agrees with Strabo's quotation from Polybios in making Hannibal cross the next pass to the coast-road" (p. 12).
- *Not geographical* (Mommsen, CIL V p. 809, and Strachan-Davidson, as reported by Freshfield p. 21): Varro's second = Great St Bernard, i.e. Varro shared Livy's "vulgar error"; Mommsen could not place the fourth ("Col de Monte Viso" or "by Val Chisone").
- *Sceptical* (Wilkinson 1911 pp. 45-46): Freshfield "assumes that these five passes correspond to the five modern high-road passes", but "between Mont Blanc and the sea there are more than thirty passes crossed by regular mule-tracks or by footpaths"; Varro's list cannot fix a col. Torr (paras 24, 59, 62) also warns that Varro, as quoted, makes Hannibal and Hasdrubal cross different passes, whereas Livy 27.39.7 ("per munita pleraque transitu fratris, quae antea invia fuerant") and Appian Hann. 52 make Hasdrubal use Hannibal's; either Servius's quotation is interpolated or Varro assigned the Cenis to Hasdrubal.
- Lazenby (snippet) judges de Beer "almost certainly wrong to locate Hannibal's pass between the Montgenevre and the coast" on the strength of Varro.

### 2.2 Sallust, Hist. 2.98.4 Maurenbrecher (Pompey's letter to the Senate, winter 75/74 BC; `Sallust_Hist_2.98M_Epistula_Pompei_latin_Ahlberg.txt`, checked against the Loeb OCR)

> *hostisque in cervicibus iam Italiae agentis ab Alpibus in Hispaniam submovi; per eas iter aliud atque Hannibal, nobis opportunius, patefeci.*
> "…[I] had driven the enemy, who were already at the throat of Italy, from the Alps into Spain; and over those mountains I had opened for you another and more convenient route than Hannibal had taken." (Rolfe, Loeb 1921, p. 415)

Which pass is "Pompey's": the Mont Genèvre, by near-universal agreement since Mommsen — Freshfield pp. 11-12 ("reasons which are accepted by almost all scholars from Mommsen onwards"); Torr paras 25-26: Appian BC 1.109 puts Pompey's road near the sources of the Rhône and the Po, which Strabo 4.6.5 corrects to the sources of the Durance and the Dora, "enough to show that Pompey's pass was Mont Genèvre"; and Strabo 4.1.3's Roman road Tarascon–Cavaillon–Durance–Embrun–Briançon–Cesanne "must thus have crossed the Alps by Mont Genèvre, and presumably was Pompey's". Marindin (CR 13, 1899) tried "Mont Genèvre plus the Col de Lautaret" (Freshfield p. 12). The consequence everyone draws: Hannibal's pass was *not* the Mont Genèvre (Freshfield p. 11: "if the Mont Genevre had been already known as Hannibal's Pass in Polybius's time, Pompey would hardly have ventured to assert that by crossing it he had opened a new route"; de Beer 1969 snippet; Torr para 71). The Montgenèvre advocates (Connolly p. 165; Hoyos 2015) must therefore read *iter aliud* as a new road-line rather than a different col — W. T. Arnold's formulation, quoted by Freshfield p. 12 n. 3: "The Romans had a clear idea of the main Alpine wall, and would not rank a new way over secondary obstacles as a new pass 'per Alpes'… 'nobis opportunius' distinctly pointed to the low and easy Mont Genevre as opposed to a rather more difficult pass… not far off."

### 2.3 Strabo 4.6.5-7 and 4.6.12 (Meineke; `Strabo_4.6.5-12_greek_Meineke.txt`; Jones, Loeb II 1923 pp. 271-295, `Strabo_4.6.5-12_english_Loeb_Jones_1923.txt`)

4.6.12: *τέτταρας δ᾽ ὑπερβάσεις ὀνομάζει μόνον· διὰ Λιγύων μὲν τὴν ἔγγιστα τῷ Τυρρηνικῷ πελάγει, εἶτα τὴν διὰ Ταυρίνων, ἣν Ἀννίβας διῆλθεν, εἶτα τὴν διὰ Σαλασσῶν, τετάρτην δὲ τὴν διὰ Ῥαιτῶν, ἁπάσας κρημνώδεις.* — "But he [Polybius] only names four passes over the mountains: the pass through the Ligures (the one that is nearest the Tyrrhenian Sea), then that through the Taurini, which Hannibal crossed, then that through the Salassi, and the fourth, that through the Rhaeti — all of them precipitous passes" (Jones p. 293). Jones's n. 5: "Polybius (3. 56) does not say where Hannibal crossed the Alps… Both the ancient writers and modern scholars differ"; Caspari (EB) inclines to the Mont Genèvre, Lenschau (RE) to the Little St Bernard.

What "through the Taurini" does and does not settle: it excludes the Salassi passes (both St Bernards) and the coastal road, and puts Hannibal's pass among those debouching on Turin — Mont Cenis, Clapier, Montgenèvre (Walbank's three, per Connolly p. 165) and, for the de Beer school, also the Traversette (Saluzzo is 60 km from Turin; Torr para 30 measures to Saluzzo). Torr p. 11 notes Strabo's "through the Taurini" is not exactly what Polybius says (3.56.3, descent "to the Insubres"), and Freshfield p. 11 uses it to argue that Polybius counted the fighting with the Taurini as part of the passage. Since this is Strabo's paraphrase of Polybius, it is the strongest early witness that the *Taurini* pass, not the Graian or Poenine one, was Hannibal's.

4.6.5: *μετὰ δὲ Ὀυοκοντίους Ἰκόνιοι καὶ Τρικόριοι καὶ μετ᾽ αὐτοὺς Μέδουλλοι, οἵπερ τὰς ὑψηλοτάτας ἔχουσι κορυφάς· τὸ γοῦν ὀρθιώτατον αὐτῶν ὕψος σταδίων ἑκατὸν ἔχειν φασὶ τὴν ἀνάβασιν, κἀνθένδε πάλιν τὴν ἐπὶ τοὺς ὅρους τοὺς τῆς Ἰταλίας κατάβασιν.* — after the Vocontii come the Iconii and Tricorii, then the Medulli "who have the highest peaks", with a hundred-stade ascent and a like descent to the Italian border; up in hollows a large lake and two springs, one feeding the Druentia to the Rhône, the other the Duria to the Po; the Medulli lie "above the confluence of the Isar and the Rhodanus" (Jones p. 271-3). This is the passage from which the Medulli are put in the Maurienne (Arc), and it makes Strabo's Vocontii–Tricorii–Medulli sequence run from the Drôme/Durance uplands northwards — relevant to Livy 21.31.9 (Tricastini, Vocontii, Tricorii) and Ammianus 15.10.11.
4.6.6: the Taurini, *Λιγυστικὸν ἔθνος*, on the Italian side, with the lands of Donnus and Cottius; then the Salassi; above them the Ceutrones, Caturiges, Varagri, Nantuates. 4.6.7 and 4.6.11: the two roads from the Salassi/Lugdunum side, the wagon-road through the Ceutrones (Little St Bernard) and the short steep Poeninus (Great St Bernard) — the roads Livy 21.38 rejects for Hannibal.

### 2.4 Cornelius Nepos, Hannibal 3.4 (Fleckeisen/Halm; `Nepos_Hannibal_3_latin_Fleckeisen-Halm.txt`)

> *ad Alpes posteaquam venit, qua Italiam ab Gallia seiungunt, quas nemo umquam cum exercitu ante eum praeter Herculem Graium transierat (quo facto is hodie saltus Graius appellatur), Alpicos conantes prohibere transitu concidit, loca patefecit, itinera muniit, effecit ut ea elephantus ornatus ire posset, qua antea unus homo inermis vix poterat repere.*
> (tr. mine) "When he came to the Alps, which separate Italy from Gaul, and which no one before him had ever crossed with an army except the Greek Hercules (whence that pass is today called the Graian), he cut down the Alpine peoples who tried to bar his crossing, opened up the ground, built roads, and made it possible for a harnessed elephant to go where before a single unarmed man could scarcely crawl."

What it says: Nepos does not say Hannibal used the *saltus Graius*; the relative clause explains the name. Read as an implication that Hannibal's pass was the Graian (Little St Bernard) by the Little St Bernard school (Law, Lenschau); Torr para 58 (pp. 31-32) shows the term was elastic ("Ptolemy iii.1.35-36 makes the Graian Alps include Briançon and Embrun. Tacitus (Hist. ii.66) speaks of a legion marching from Turin across the Graian Alps, clearly meaning the Genevre or Cenis passes, as the Little St Bernard is not accessible from Turin"). Pliny 3.123 and 3.134 (below) attach Hercules to the Graiae and the Poeni to the Poeninae. The "Hercules" road is also the basis of Connolly's argument for the Montgenèvre (the Herculean way = the Durance road, so Diod. 4.19.3-4; Hornblower 2024 p. 85-86 n. 52 notes Diodorus does not mention the Alps).

### 2.5 Ammianus Marcellinus 15.10.2-11 (`Ammianus_15.10_latin_LatinLibrary.txt`; Rolfe, Loeb I 1935 pp. 175-187, `Ammianus_15.10_english_Loeb_Rolfe_1935.txt`)

15.10.2-7: the Cottian road from Segusio up to *stationem nomine Martis* (Ad Martis = Oulx) seven miles on, then the steep climb *ad Matronae … verticem* (Mont Genèvre) and down to Brigantia, built by King Cottius; 15.10.9: the Poenine Alps and the Graian named from Hercules; 15.10.10-11 (Latin as printed in the Loeb OCR, agreeing with the Latin Library text):
> *Quae Hannibal doctus a perfugis, ut erat expeditae mentis et callidae, Taurinis ducentibus accolis, per Tricasinos et oram Vocontiorum extremam, ad saltus Tricorios venit. Indeque exorsus, aliud iter antehac insuperabile fecit; excisaque rupe in immensum elata, quam cremando vi magna flammarum acetoque infuso dissolvit, per Druentiam flumen, gurgitibus vagis intutum, regiones occupavit Etruscas.*
> "But Hannibal learned of this from deserters, and being of a nimble and crafty wit, came, under the guidance of natives from among the Taurini, through the Tricasini and the extreme edge of the Vocontii to the passes of the Tricorii. Starting out from there, he made another road, where it hitherto had been impassable; he hewed out a cliff which rose to a vast height by burning it with flames of immense power and crumbling it by pouring on vinegar; then he marched along the river Druentia, dangerous with its shifting eddies, and seized upon the district of Etruria." (Rolfe p. 187; his notes identify Ad Martis = Oulx and Matrona = Mont Genèvre)

What it says: Ammianus (4th c., drawing on Timagenes for the Alps, unverified) reproduces Livy's tribal sequence (Tricastini, Vocontii, Tricorii), adds Taurini guides, and has Hannibal reach the Durance *after* the Tricorii and the rock-splitting — i.e. a Durance-basin pass, which Torr (para 61) and the de Beer school stress and which the Isère school treats as Livian confusion. Note the two Latin texts differ at one word: Latin Library *in solidam solvit*, Loeb *dissolvit* — check Seyfarth before citing.

### 2.6 Appian, Hannibalica 4 (= 1.4 Mendelssohn; `Appian_Hannibalica_1-2_greek_Mendelssohn.txt`)

> *…κακοπαθῶν χιόνος τε πολλῆς οὔσης καὶ κρύους, τὴν μὲν ὕλην τέμνων τε καὶ κατακαίων, τὴν δὲ τέφραν σβεννὺς ὕδατι καὶ ὄξει, καὶ τὴν πέτραν ἐκ τοῦδε ψαφαρὰν γιγνομένην σφύραις σιδηραῖς θραύων, καὶ ὁδὸν ποιῶν ἣ καὶ νῦν ἐστιν ἐπὶ τῶν ὀρῶν ἐντριβὴς καὶ καλεῖται δίοδος Ἀννίβου.*
> (tr. mine) "…suffering badly from the deep snow and the cold, cutting and burning the timber, quenching the ash with water and vinegar, and breaking with iron hammers the rock that was thus made crumbly, he made a road which even now is well trodden on the mountains and is called 'Hannibal's pass'."

What it says: in the 2nd c. AD a road over the mountains was popularly called *diodos Annibou*; Appian does not locate it. Mahaney 2026 (p. 4) cites it for the southern route; it is equally consistent with any col. Appian Hann. 52 (Hasdrubal used the road Hannibal had opened, *hodopoiēmena proteron hypo Annibou*, per Torr para 62) and Iberica 13-14 (the march to the Alps; sixth month from New Carthage, Hann. 4) were checked in the Perseus files and add no pass.

### 2.7 Zonaras 8.23 (epitome of Cassius Dio; Cary, Loeb Dio II 1914, `Zonaras_8.23_Dio_Loeb_vol2_Cary_1914_pp70-95_region.txt`)

"For the mountains there are exceedingly precipitous, and the snow, which had fallen in great quantities, was driven by the winds and filled the chasms, and the ice was frozen very hard… many of them perished by reason of the cold and lack of food; many also returned home. There is a story to the effect that he himself would also have turned back but for the fact that the road already traversed was longer and more difficult than the portion remaining before him." No pass, no tribe, no landmark; useful only for the snow/ice tradition.

### 2.8 Pliny, NH 3.117, 3.123-125, 3.133-138 (Mayhoff via Perseus; `Pliny_NH_3.117-138_latin_Mayhoff.txt`; 3.31-37 from the Latin Library)

- 3.117: *Padus, e gremio Vesuli montis celsissimum in cacumen Alpium elati finibus Ligurum Bagiennorum visendo fonte profluens…* — the Po rises on Monte Viso, "the loftiest summit of the Alps", in Bagienni territory. Cited by the Traversette school (de Beer, Mahaney) because the Traversette descends straight to the Po's source; Pliny says nothing of Hannibal here.
- 3.123: *Salassorum Augusta Praetoria iuxta geminas Alpium fores, Graias atque Poeninas — his Poenos, Grais Herculem transisse memorant* — "they relate that the Carthaginians crossed by the Poenine [gate], Hercules by the Graian." This is the *vulgar* Poeninus tradition (Great St Bernard), the same one Livy 21.38.6-9 and Servius record; Pliny reports it without endorsement (*memorant*). 3.124: *Vercellae Libiciorum ex Salluis ortae* — the Libui/Libici at Vercelli, whom Livy 21.38.7 says the Poenine and Cremonis routes would have reached. 3.125: the Caturiges, *Insubrum exsules*, extinct.
- 3.133-134: Alpine length and breadth per Coelius, Timagenes, Nepos, Livy; the Lepontii "left from Hercules' company, with limbs frost-bitten in the crossing of the Alps", and the Graii "of the same army" as inhabitants of the Graian Alps.
- 3.137-138: the Tropaeum Alpium list — *SALASSI · ACITAVONES · MEDVLLI · VCENNI · CATVRIGES · BRIGIANI · SOGIONTI · BRODIONTI · NEMALONI · EDENATES · VESVBIANI…* — and *Non sunt adiectae Cottianae civitates XV, quae non fuerant hostiles, item adtributae municipiis lege Pompeia.* Freshfield (p. 12) uses the exemption of the fifteen Cottian *civitates* to argue that Pompey's road, and later Cottius's, was the Mont Genèvre.
- 3.36-37 (Narbonensis): Vocontii a federated *civitas* with two capitals, Vasio and Lucus Augusti; the Tricastini (*Augusta Tricastinorum*) — the tribes of Livy's itinerary (not quoted here at length; see the saved file).

### 2.9 Caesar, BG 1.10.3-5 (`Caesar_BG_1.10_latin_LatinLibrary.txt`, checked against the Perseus XML)

> *…qua proximum iter in ulteriorem Galliam per Alpes erat, cum his quinque legionibus ire contendit. Ibi Ceutrones et Graioceli et Caturiges locis superioribus occupatis itinere exercitum prohibere conantur. Compluribus his proeliis pulsis ab Ocelo, quod est oppidum citerioris provinciae extremum, in fines Vocontiorum ulterioris provinciae die septimo pervenit; inde in Allobrogum fines, ab Allobrogibus in Segusiavos exercitum ducit.*

In 58 BC the "nearest road" from Cisalpina into Further Gaul ran from Ocelum (Val di Susa; Avigliana or Drubiaglio, unverified) to the Vocontii in seven days, past Ceutrones, Graioceli and Caturiges holding the heights — i.e. the Mont Genèvre/Durance corridor (Torr, Freshfield) or, for the Cenis school, the Mont Cenis (Jullian). It fixes the Vocontii as the first Transalpine people reached from the Cottian passes and shows that the Durance road was the ordinary military road twenty years after Pompey.

### 2.10 Silius Italicus, Punica 3.477-556 and 4.1-60 (`Silius_Punica_book3/4_latin_LatinLibrary.txt`)

Epic amplification of Livy: perpetual snow, no road, the Graian Hercules as the only predecessor, the descent to the Taurini. No topographical datum independent of Livy; not used as evidence by any modern advocate I found except for colour.

### 2.11 Ptolemy 3.1.30-36 (Müller 1883, OCR in `editions/`)

Taurini "under the Salassi", Augusta Taurinorum; the Graian Alps extended to include Briançon and Embrun (Torr para 58 citing 3.1.35-36); Ptolemy 3.1.30-32 places Aosta and Ivrea with the Salassi and Vercelli and Lomello with the Libici (Torr para 20). Used only to gloss Livy's Libui and Nepos' Graian pass.

### 2.12 Roman road distances (Itinerarium Antonini and Burdigalense, Parthey–Pinder 1848 OCR: `Itinerarium_Antonini_and_Burdigalense_Alpine_routes_Parthey-Pinder_1848_OCR.txt`; numerals must be checked against the scan)

- Itin. Ant. 339.6-341.5 Wess., *a Mediolano Arelate per Alpes Cottias*: … Taurinis – Fines xviii – Segusione xxxiii – Ad Martis xvi – Brigantione xviii … (Mont Genèvre road). Itin. Burdig. 555-556: *inde incipiunt Alpes Cottiae … Byrigantum, inde ascendis Matronam, Gesdaone, Ad Marte, Secussione, inde incipit Italia.*
- Itin. Ant. 344.3-346.3, *per Alpes Graias*: Augusta Praetoria – Arebrigium – Bergintrum – Darantasia … (Little St Bernard road).
- Itin. Ant. 356-358: Ramae – Eburoduno – Caturrigas – Vapinco – Monte Seleuco – Luco – Dea Vocontiorum – Augusta – Valentia – Ursolis – Vienna (the Durance–Gap–Drôme road), the line Torr (para 29) and Strabo 4.1.3 (Tarascon 63 m.p. to the ascent, 99 to Embrun, 71 to Cesanne) use to match Polybius' 1,400 stades "along the river".
- The Peutinger Table is cited only through Azan p. 33 (the Fossae Marianae–Arles–Avignon–Orange–Valence–Vienne road, 204 km from the sea to the Isère confluence) and Bocquet (fr.wiki: a Graian route Aosta–Isère cities); not obtained. The Vicarello goblets were not obtained (they give the coastal Gades–Rome road, no inland Alpine pass — unverified).

### 2.13 Timagenes, Justin 32.3, Diodorus 25

Timagenes survives for the Alps only through Ammianus 15.9-12 (cf. 15.9.2, not re-read here; unverified). Justin 32.3 (saved, `Justin_32_latin_LatinLibrary.txt`) concerns the Gauls' migrations and Philip/Perseus, nothing on Hannibal's pass. Diodorus 25 (fragments) was not obtained (Perseus Diodorus book 25 not under the tried paths) — nothing is expected there beyond the Hercules road of 4.19.

### 2.14 Coelius Antipater's *Cremonis iugum* (Livy 21.38.6-7 — outside this remit, but the readings belong here)

Livy: the "vulgar" view is the Poeninus; "Coelius says he crossed by the Cremonis iugum"; both would have led him to the Libui, not the Taurini, so Livy rejects both. Readings: (a) Little St Bernard / Mont Cramont (Law 1866; Lenschau; Livy's own inference — Torr para 20 p. 10, para 57 pp. 31); (b) a pass at the head of the Dora Riparia mistaken by Livy, who like Strabo 4.6.5 confused the two Duriae (Torr para 57, p. 31: "Livy may have made the same mistake and fancied that Hannibal would go down the Dora Baltea… when Coelius really meant the pass at the source of the Dora Riparia"); (c) Col de Grimone on the Drôme road (de Beer; Lazenby n. 39 "de Beer's neat identification of the Cremonis iugum with the Col de Grimone"; de Beer 1969 snippet dismisses Mont Cramont as "not worth any [consideration]"); (d) Lazenby (snippet, cut): Livy "right to identify… with a pass leading into the territory [of the Libui]". Since Coelius drew on Silenus, who was with Hannibal (Cic. Div. 1.24.49, per Torr para 57), this is the one ancient datum that could name the pass, and it is irrecoverable.

### 2.15 The Gallic peoples (Barruol 1969 not obtained; Rivet 1988 snippets; primary texts above)

Allobroges: Rhône–Isère, up to the Grande Chartreuse (Pol. 3.49.5-13; Caes. BG 1.10.5; Hoyos 2015 p. 106). Tricastini: the Tricastin, Saint-Paul-Trois-Châteaux (Pliny 3.36; Livy 21.31.9; Amm. 15.10.11). Vocontii: Vaison and Luc-en-Diois/Die (Pliny 3.37; Strabo 4.6.5; Caes. BG 1.10.5). Tricorii: between the Vocontii and the Medulli (Strabo 4.6.5), i.e. the Drac/Trièves–upper Durance uplands (Hoyos 2015 p. 106: "on the other side of the Vercors massif"; en.wiki "Tricorii" citing Hoyos 2006 pp. 442-4). Caturiges: Chorges/Embrun (Pliny 3.137; Caes. 1.10.4; Itin. Ant. Caturrigas). Medulli: the Maurienne, "highest peaks" (Strabo 4.6.5; Pliny 3.137). Ceutrones: Tarentaise, Little St Bernard road (Strabo 4.6.6-7, 11; Caes. 1.10.4; Pliny 3.135). Graioceli: Caes. 1.10.4 only; usually the upper Maurienne/Mont Cenis (unverified). Segovii/Segusini: Segusio (Pliny 3.123; Amm. 15.10.3). Taurini: Ligurian, on both sides of the Po around Turin (Strabo 4.6.6; Pliny 3.123; Pol. 3.60.8; Livy 21.38.5). Insubres: Milan (Pliny 3.124-125; Pol. 3.56.3). Salassi: Aosta valley (Strabo 4.6.7; Pliny 3.123, 134). Libui/Libici: Vercelli (Pliny 3.124; Livy 21.38.7). Guy Barruol's *Les peuples préromains du Sud-Est de la Gaule* (1969) remains the standard reference (unverified — not obtained; only his 1963 Memini article first page on Persée).

## 3. The case for and against each pass, in the advocates' words where available

**Col du Clapier (2,482 m; Savine-Coche and Petit Mont-Cenis variants).** For: the view of the Po plain from a promontory that "would hold several thousand men" (Wilkinson pp. 8-9; Colin p. xv: "Ce n'est pas un lambeau de plaine qu'on aperçoit, mais bien… 'toute l'Italie'"); Polybius' distances from the Rhône (Colin pp. 318-321: Isère–Arc–Clapier fits 1,400 + 1,200 stades, "à condition de passer, non pas par le mont Cenis, mais par le Clapier"); the Italian end nearest Turin (Hornblower p. 86); old snow lying on a high pass (Lazenby); the "acropolis" impression (Wilkinson p. 8). Against: "a lofty and difficult by-pass" (Freshfield preface); Connolly p. 165: from the col itself no view ("Only by trekking along the hillside to Monte Aria is this possible"), the north side "completely iced up" if old snow lay on the south side, and "Those who have argued that Hannibal crossed the Clapier must explain why he did not use the Mont Cenis which is both lower and easier"; Torr para 69: "Hannibal had no motive for going so far north"; Bocquet: the Ambin glacier and Po-plain fog (fr.wiki); Mahaney: no rockfall, no firn. Advocates: Perrin, Azan, Colin, Ferrand, Wilkinson, Dion, Meyer, Lavis-Trafford (Savine-Coche), Hoyte, Walbank (hesitantly), Proctor, Huss, Lazenby, Barruol, Lancel, Kuhle & Kuhle, Hunt, Hornblower; slightly preferred by Bradford.

**Col de la Traversette (2,947 m).** For: Varro's order and Pompey's Genèvre put Hannibal south of the Genèvre (Freshfield, Torr, de Beer); Polybius' "along the river" = the Durance road (Torr para 29); the view (Torr para 69; Connolly concedes it is "the only pass from which the plain of the Po can be seen", p. 164); year-round snow (Prevas); the two-tier rockfall, firn and the Clostridia mire (Mahaney); elephant energetics (Berti & Vollrath 2026). Against: the Combe de Queyras gorge, 15 km "with precipitous sides" ("Hannibal would have had to have been a maniac to take his army this way", Connolly p. 164; Azan pp. 62-3 quoting Roman on "l'horreur de cette gorge"); altitude "more than 100 m higher than the Montgenèvre" and the lower Col de la Croix on the way (Connolly p. 164); the 1979 Royal Engineers' verdict on elephants and scree (Connolly p. 165); "rather than a pass, a narrow breach" (Lancel); Walbank 1956 (not read); Kuhle & Kuhle on the philology of the "rockfall"; Hunt ("might not even be that ancient geologically"). Advocates: Torr, de Beer, Cottrell?, Bagnall, Pyatt, Prevas, Seibert (reportedly), Mahaney, Berti & Vollrath.

**Col du Montgenèvre (1,854 m).** For: the lowest and easiest pass, the "road of Hercules", the only road to the Taurini Polybius knew (Connolly p. 165); Livy's Tricastini–Vocontii–Tricorii–Durance itinerary leads there (Hoyos 2015 p. 106; Hoyos 2006 Klio); Caesar's 58 BC road; W. T. Arnold: "the evidence converges on a Durance pass. The best Durance pass is certainly the Mont Genevre." Against: Pompey's *iter aliud* (Freshfield pp. 11-12; de Beer; Torr para 71); "too low and too easy" and no view (de Beer; Azan p. 63 quoting Macé: "Du col du mont Genèvre… on n'aperçoit que des [gorges]"); nine days from the Durance to a summit ten miles from it (Torr para 68); Lancel: the Herculean way was exactly what Scipio expected. Advocates: Gibbon 1763 (de Beer table), Hennebert, Bullock Hall 1898 (Freshfield p. 12), Caspari, Connolly, Hoyos, Yardley–Hoyos.

**Col du Mont-Cenis (2,083 m; Petit Mont-Cenis 2,182 m).** For: Napoleon's military judgement; the shortest road to Turin (Jullian p. 479); easier than the Clapier (Connolly). Against: no view from the plateau ("un cirque de montagnes enferme complètement", Colin p. xv; Azan pp. 58-61; Wilkinson p. 8 n. 1). Advocates: Simler, Larauza, Maissiat, Ellis (Little Mont Cenis), Osiander, Jullian, Napoleon; no advocate since Hoyte's expedition crossed it faute de mieux (1959).

**Col de Larche / Argentière (1,991 m).** For: Varro's second pass in geographical order (Freshfield pp. 20-21); the Vars–Argentière route with villages and cornfields "all the way" (p. 28); an easy col; "probably the pass which Hannibal intended to cross" (de Beer, who then diverts him to the Traversette). Against: no view (Torr para 69); 40 miles to the plain (Torr para 55); Wilkinson pp. 45-46; Freshfield's own W. T. Arnold preferred the Genèvre; Hoyos 2015 keeps it only as the Genèvre's "neighbor". Advocates: Freshfield alone in the 20th c.; Hoyos as an alternative.

**Petit-Saint-Bernard (2,188 m).** For: Coelius' Cremonis iugum, Nepos' saltus Graius, the Ceutrones wagon-road (Strabo 4.6.11), the opulence of Allobrogian lands (Mommsen, Bocquet), Livy's "turn to the left" from the Rhône. Against: Livy 21.38.7-8 (leads to the Libui); no view and 75 miles to the plain (Torr para 55; Colin p. 321: "On ne voit de là que d'affreux précipices"); Strabo 4.6.12 "through the Taurini". Advocates: de Luc, Wickham & Cramer, Long?, Law, Arnold, Mommsen, Lenschau, Hyde, Pais, Dodge, Coninck, Debelmas, Bocquet.

**Grand-Saint-Bernard (Poeninus), Col Agnel, Col de la Croix, Col de Malaure.** The Poeninus is the "vulgar" ancient view (Livy 21.38; Pliny 3.123; Servius) held by Whitaker 1794 and refuted by Livy's own argument; Torr para 23 ("incredible that Hannibal should have crossed a pass so far eastward"). Agnel and Malaure are named as by-ways only (Freshfield p. 28; Wilkinson p. 9 on Malaure's view "from a narrow ridge on which no body of troops could be stationed"; Cottrell's short list); the Col de la Croix is on Connolly's road to the Traversette (p. 164). No advocate found for any of them.

## 4. How the debate stands in 2026, and the "nothing has happened since" checks

**Exact quotations held (for the report).**
- Walbank 1957 (Commentary I) is not obtained; his own retrospect, *Polybius, Rome and the Hellenistic World* (2002), snippet: "…hesitatingly came down in favour of the Col du Clapier (which was also Huss' choice). A slight variant…"; and "More recently J. Hunt 2017 (snippet): "Walbank says that too much ink has been [spilled]…"; "not even including philology, as argued by Walbank, 'Hannibal's Pass,' 37-45, esp. 43-45."
- Lazenby 1998 (snippets): "…fulfil all the requirements — the Col de Clapier. This is 2482 metres high, has a wonderful [view]…"; "the Isere, moreover, and Hannibal here left the Rhone to march on up the Isere, as those…"; "The distance between the gorge above Pontcharra and the Col de Clapier is approximately 120 kilometres, and this…"; "[de Beer] was almost certainly wrong to locate Hannibal's pass between the Montgenevre and the coast"; "de Beer's neat identification of the 'Cremonis iugum' with the Col de Grimone on RN 5".
- Hoyos 2015, *Mastering the West* pp. 105-106: "This zigzag route is plausible for an army aiming to elude Roman observers: along the Rhone to the Isère, over to the Drac valley, and from there to the high Alps and the pass. Such a route would best lead to the Montgenèvre pass or one of its neighbors such as the Larche — each a fairly easy climb up from Gaul, followed by a steep descent down to Italy. Other candidates (the best known are the Col du Clapier and the Traversette, both farther north) are much more difficult to fit into what evidence we do have."
- Goldsworthy 2000/2007, "The March to Italy": "Even in Livy's day, there was a fierce dispute over which pass the Carthaginians had taken over the Alps… Their conclusions have varied enormously and unfortunately the nature of our sources makes it impossible to resolve these disputes. I do not intend to discuss this topic since it would be impossible to do it justice in the framework of a treatment of all three Punic [Wars]"; and "which pass is perhaps more fiercely debated than any other aspect of his route."
- Hornblower 2024, p. 86: "Hannibal's own exact route across the Alps is disputed to this day. He clearly left the Rhône to follow its tributary the Isère, but there agreement ends. The likeliest mountain pass must be one whose Italian end is closest to Turin, where he is first heard of in action after his descent. That probably indicates that the best candidate for the pass is the Col de Clapier. Astonishingly, all his elephants got through"; n. 54: "Walbank 1985: 107–19 [orig. 1956]; for the Col de Clapier, Proctor 1971: 210–16; Lazenby 1978: 45–6 with plate iv; 302 map 3. But Hoyos in Yardley 2006: 620–30 at 626–9 prefers the Col de Montgenèvre."
- de Beer 1956/1969 (snippets): "the pass which Pompey crossed, the Mont-Genevre, was not the pass that Hannibal crossed. If he had crossed it…"; "…Isere basin and the knowledge that Hannibal did not cross the Mont-Genévre, leave only the Ubaye and the [Guil]…"; "That leaves the Col de la Traversette (2,950 metres), accessible through the valley [of the Guil]…"; "identify the Cremonis jugum with the Mont Cramont, an outlying spur of the Mont-Blanc range, is not worth any [consideration]"; "…in thinking that by Cremonis jugum Coelius meant the Petit Saint-Bernard; Coelius, whose testimony because…"; on Skaras: "he calmly altered Skaras into Arar to make it agree with Livy"; the Col de Larche "probably the pass which Hannibal intended to cross"; the Traversette the "strongest competitor".
- Kuhle & Kuhle 2011 (abstract): "For a long time now, historians have considered the way across the Col de Clapier as the most likely invasion route of Hannibal's army across the Alps… a critical reassessment of the ancient texts and a closer look at the geomorphological situation clearly show that this interpretation is unwarranted."
- Berti & Vollrath 2026 (abstract): "Among the possible alpine passes, the route crossing at the Col du Clapier was considered the most likely candidate. Recent philological and geomorphological analyses suggest instead a route via the Col de la Traversette… Our analysis favors the route of the Traversette."
- Mahaney 2026, p. 8: "…the great captain crossed either Mt Cenis or the Col du Clapier, no matter the evidence against it. The facts compiled by Polybius, Sir Gavin de Beer and myself not only argue otherwise, but the rockfall itself, a one-of-a-kind landform on the lee of the Alps, certifies the route."

**Checks on "nothing since" claims.**
1. "No one has examined the Traversette geomorphology since Mahaney": false — Kuhle & Kuhle 2011 and 2014 reassessed both the texts and "the geomorphological situation" (abstract), and Berti & Vollrath 2026 modelled the pass energetically.
2. "Classicists have ignored the geoarchaeology": partly false — MacDonald 2015 (n. citing Mahaney 2010), Hunt 2017 (p. 284 n., citing Kuhle & Kuhle against Mahaney), Hoyos 2015 (n. 5, "Mahaney 2010" listed among "notable modern discussions") engage it; Hornblower 2024 n. 54 does not cite Mahaney at all, which is itself a datum on the mainstream's reception.
3. "The Clapier is the consensus": overstated — it is the most-cited single answer (Kuhle & Kuhle: "for a long time now, historians have considered the way across the Col de Clapier as the most likely"; Berti & Vollrath: "was considered the most likely candidate"; Hornblower "probably"), but Hoyos (Montgenèvre), the French Petit-Saint-Bernard school (Bocquet), and the Traversette school are live, and Goldsworthy, MacDonald and Lendering refuse a verdict.
4. "No new pass or route has been proposed since 1971": false — Savine-Coche (Lavis-Trafford 1956/Meyer 1964, revived by Lancel and Hunt), de Galbert's Belledonne short cut (2005), Seibert's divided army (1993), Bocquet's Petit-Saint-Bernard revival, Mahaney's Grîmone–Aygues–Guil line.
5. "Nothing has been done on the Roman roads of the Cottian Alps": false — Artru 2013, 2016 (Persée).
6. "No one has done a physical/quantitative model of the crossing": false — Berti & Vollrath 2026 (PNAS) did, favouring the Traversette; earlier the 1979 Royal Engineers survey (Connolly p. 165) and Hoyte 1959 tested feasibility with an elephant.

**Majority view, stated carefully.** Among professional ancient historians writing in English 1956-2024 the Isère–Arc–Clapier/Savine-Coche route is the modal answer, always hedged (Walbank "hesitatingly", Hornblower "probably", Lancel "chimérique"); the Montgenèvre is the principal scholarly alternative (Hoyos); the Traversette is favoured by the earth-science literature and by popular authors in the de Beer line, and now by a PNAS energetics paper; the Petit-Saint-Bernard is a French regional position. No candidate commands a majority of all authors, and the two most recent peer-reviewed contributions (Hornblower 2024; Berti & Vollrath 2026) point in opposite directions.
